## Selective Citation of Successful Predictions Creates a Hindsight-Bias Illusion of Predictive Skill

Citing one's own past correct warnings (e.g. opposing a war that later went badly) as general evidence of superior judgement is unreliable without a systematic record, because successful predictions are more memorable and more likely to be cited than failed ones—without a full record of predictions, probabilities, and falsification criteria, a claim of predictive superiority remains anecdotal rather than demonstrated.

### Scope & Conditions

Applies to any argument-from-track-record that cites specific hits without also disclosing the full set of predictions made, including misses.

### Evidence

> "There is also a selection problem: successful warnings are more memorable than failed ones, and the transcript does not provide a systematic record of predictions, probabilities, or falsification criteria. Without that, claims of predictive superiority remain anecdotal rather than demonstrated."

### Implications

- A single striking correct call (even a well-known one) is weak evidence of a person's general forecasting reliability unless their full track record, including misses, is available.
