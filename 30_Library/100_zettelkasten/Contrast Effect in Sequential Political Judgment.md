## Contrast Effect in Sequential Political Judgment

When an evaluator is exposed to an extreme, norm-violating political figure, that figure sets an anchor so low that subsequent evaluations of a moderately flawed politician are pulled upward—because the two are compared against each other rather than against an absolute standard.

### Scope & Conditions

Applies to sequential evaluation generally; documented in non-political domains such as speed dating. Strongest when the anchor is highly salient and the comparison is made close in time. It is an instance of the general psychological "contrast effect," where perception of a stimulus is exaggerated by juxtaposition with a differing baseline.

### Evidence

> "If an individual is exposed to a highly negative, extreme, or norm-violating stimulus, subsequent stimuli of moderate or even poor quality are perceived much more positively than they would be in strict isolation."

### Implications

- Voters can rate a routinely dishonest politician as trustworthy purely through comparison, without any change in that politician's actual behaviour.
- The effect is a property of the evaluation sequence, not of the object being evaluated.

### Library Sources

- _Thinking, Fast and Slow_ (Daniel Kahneman), Ch. 11 "Anchors"—the canonical treatment of anchoring: "Any number that you are asked to consider as a possible solution to an estimation problem will induce an anchoring effect." Same underlying mechanism this atom applies to sequential political evaluation.
- _Cognitive Illusions: Intriguing Phenomena in Judgement, Thinking and Memory_, Section 12 "Anchoring effect"—experimental detail on how a prior judgment (e.g. estimating a raccoon's weight) assimilates a later, unrelated judgment (a giraffe's weight) toward it—structurally the same assimilation this atom describes for political figures.
- _Predictably Irrational_ (Dan Ariely)—behavioural-economics demonstrations that an arbitrary first anchor shapes subsequent valuations even when the evaluator knows the anchor was arbitrary.
