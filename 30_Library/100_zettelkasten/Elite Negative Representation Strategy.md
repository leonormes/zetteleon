## Elite Negative Representation Strategy

Elected officials increasingly practise "negative representation"—defining themselves by opposing and attacking the rival party rather than by substantive policy work—because media coverage, small-donor fundraising, and elite peer pressure all reward attack-focused positioning even though the general public says it prefers substantive debate.

### Scope & Conditions

Term and research from Mia Costa's _How Politicians Polarize_. The incentive is structural (media/donor/elite ecosystem), not a simple mismatch between what politicians want and what voters want—voters dislike the rhetoric but the ecosystem rewards it anyway.

### Evidence

> "While the mass public actually dislikes negative, attacking rhetoric and strongly prefers politicians to focus on substantive policy solutions, politicians continue to polarize because the broader political ecosystem heavily and predictably rewards negative representation."

### Implications

- Politicians can rationally keep polarizing even while accurately believing voters dislike it, because the electoral and financial incentives point the other way.

### Library Sources

- _How to Be a Liberal_ (Ian Dunt)—describes the post-2008/post-Iraq collapse in "conventions on truth-telling," where trust in government communication, financial institutions, and economic fairness broke down together—the erosion of shared truth-telling norms that negative representation exploits.
- _Data and Goliath: The Hidden Battles to Collect Your Data and Control Your World_ (Bruce Schneier), Ch. 8—documents how personalised marketing lets campaigns micro-target attack ads and fundraising appeals at specific voter segments (income, gun ownership, prior news consumption)—the modern data-driven mechanics behind the negative-campaigning incentive this atom describes.
