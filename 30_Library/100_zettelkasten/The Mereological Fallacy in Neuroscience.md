## The Mereological Fallacy in Neuroscience

Summary: The mereological fallacy is ascribing to a _part_ of an organism—a hemisphere, a brain region—psychological predicates such as "believes," "wants," or "is surprised," which apply logically only to the _whole_ organism.

Details: Named in Bennett and Hacker's critique of neuroscience, the objection holds that hemispheres do not believe in God or want to be racing drivers; _people_ do. Describing split-brain results in terms of two hemispheres each with its own beliefs risks replacing the myth of one indivisible soul with the myth of two little agents (homunculi) inside the skull. As a critique of Harris it is real but not decisive: it challenges the _language_ of "two conscious hemispheres" without settling whether two subjects exist. Harris could reply that the phrasing is shorthand for the underlying neural facts.

### Evidence

> "attributing to a _part_ of an organism properties that logically apply only to the _whole_ organism."

### Implications

- Predicates like belief and intention may be category-misapplied when pinned to brain parts.
- The fallacy is a warning about description, not a proof about how many subjects there are.
