title: Evidence - Mass Literacy Sustained Working-Class Intellectual Traditions Outside Academia
type: evidence
---

Rose's study reconstructs what British working-class readers actually read, from memoirs, library borrowing records, autobiographies and society minute books. The finding is that serious engagement with philosophy, classics and political theory was widespread among people with no formal education past childhood, sustained by institutions they built and funded themselves.

The relevant institutions: Mechanics' Institutes (from 1821, offering technical and general education to working men), mutual improvement societies (small, self-organised reading and discussion groups), and the Workers' Educational Association (founded 1903, still operating). Comparable American traditions ran through Chautauqua and the labour college movement.

This is the strongest empirical leg of the atelic argument: it demonstrates that intellectual engagement is not historically tied to elite status, and that the practice survives without institutional validation, because it demonstrably did.

## Limits of This Evidence

It establishes existence and scale, not sufficiency or comparability. It does not show that autodidactic traditions matched academic ones in rigour, nor that they would recur absent the specific nineteenth-century conditions—cheap print, a self-improvement culture, and the near-total absence of competing entertainment—that produced them.
