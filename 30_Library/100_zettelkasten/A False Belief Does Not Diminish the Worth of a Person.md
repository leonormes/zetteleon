## A False Belief Does Not Diminish the Worth of a Person

Holding a false belief makes a person mistaken about that belief and nothing more: it does not reduce their worth as a human being. Worth is held constant, so an idea can be judged with full severity while the person who holds it is not. ^claim

### Scope & Conditions

Applies when judging people by what they believe, in disagreement and critique. Constant worth is a normative premise, not something evidence can establish. The claim does not say a belief is harmless or that harmful actions are excused. It rules out one inference only, from "this belief is false" to "this person is of less value".

### Evidence

The core is the author's own reasoning. Its first wording came from an AI conversation (Gemini, 2026-07-13) captured in a HEAD note no longer found under that name, so that origin is unverified. Two books support only the premise that a belief is weak evidence about its holder, not the premise that worth is constant:

> "We see the world, not as it is, but as we are—or, as we are conditioned to see it."
> (Covey 2022, ch. 7, PDF p. 35) · [open in Calibre](calibre://view-book/GCcalibreBooks/1683/EPUB?open_at=toc:7%20Habits%20of%20Highly%20Effective%20Peeople)

> "Intuitions come first, strategic reasoning second."
> (Haidt 2012, "Introduction", PDF p. 12) · [open in Calibre](calibre://view-book/GCcalibreBooks/1639/EPUB?open_at=toc:Introduction)

Covey ties a view of the world to conditioning, and Haidt ties moral belief to intuition before reasoning. So a false belief says more about a person's history than about their value.

### Implications

- Evaluate the idea at full severity and keep the person out of the evaluation.
- It is cheaper: judging who is right as a human being needs constant defence and sorting, while judging claims alone removes that load.
- Regret about past dismissiveness is a signal: the old frame, where one wrong premise flagged the whole person, is visible only from outside it.

### Falsifier

- Evidence that holding worth constant leads to worse judgement of ideas than judging by person, or that a person's beliefs predict their conduct so reliably that a person-level verdict is the more accurate one.
