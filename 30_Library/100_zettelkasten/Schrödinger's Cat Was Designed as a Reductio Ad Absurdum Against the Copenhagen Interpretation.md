## Schrödinger's Cat Was Designed as a Reductio Ad Absurdum Against the Copenhagen Interpretation

The Copenhagen interpretation held that a quantum particle exists in all possible states simultaneously until observed, at which point its wave function collapses into one definite state. Schrödinger, siding with Einstein, considered this philosophical nonsense. He designed the cat-in-a-box scenario to scale that logic up to a macroscopic object: if a subatomic particle can be in two states at once until measured, and a cat's fate is tied to that particle, then the cat must also be simultaneously dead and alive. Since a half-dead, half-alive cat is obvious nonsense, Schrödinger's point was that the interpretation itself must be missing something—the thought experiment was a mockery of the idea, not an illustration of it.

### Scope & Conditions

A historical/intent claim about Schrödinger's own 1935 purpose, not a claim about whether the Copenhagen interpretation is correct.

### Implications

- The thought experiment's popular use as a teaching illustration inverts its original purpose.
- Understanding a metaphor's origin can explain why it structurally fails at a task it was never designed for.
