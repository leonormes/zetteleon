## Archery Shot Process - Bowstring Maintenance

Summary:

Bowstring care—twist management, waxing, tag-end trimming, and serving replacement. Preserving consistent brace height, diagnosing correct twist count, wax application, Flemish twist maintenance, and knotless serving method. Sourced from Jake Kaminski and Kazden Ammons.

Details:

### Why Twist Storage Matters

- Brace height should remain relatively the same every time you string the bow; it should only naturally change due to extreme temperature fluctuations or when breaking in a brand new string.
- Simply taking the string off and throwing it in a case causes it to lose twists, altering brace height and forcing constant readjustment before every session.
- To prevent this, always secure the loops against one another when the string is off the bow so they cannot untwist.

### General Principle

- Never let go of the string loops until they are secured. If you release the loops, the string will untwist unexpectedly and change your brace height.

### Method 1: Storing on the Limb (Ideal for ILF Bows)

- When unstringing the bow, grab the bottom loop and immediately hook it onto the top limb alongside the top loop so it cannot untwist.
- Once the limb is removed from the riser, slide the string down, loop it around the centre of the limb, and wrap it through the ILF groove or base of the limb.
- Protect the Serving: Lay the rest of the string flat on the front of the limb and slide it into your limb sleeve. Never bend the centre serving at a sharp angle—this causes the serving to separate and wear prematurely.

### Method 2: Using a Twist Keeper (For Backup Strings or Separate Storage)

- Take both loops off the bow while holding them securely together in one hand.
- Use a paperclip or a dedicated "twist keeper" and clip it directly through both end loops to lock them together.
- Gently fold the string back and forth (avoiding sharp bends on the servings) and store it in a ziplock bag or tube.

### Method 3: The Knot Method (No Tools Required)

- Take the string off and pass one loop directly through the other.
- Then take the loop you just passed through and pass it back through the first loop, pulling them together into a loose knot that locks the twists in place.
- When ready to string the bow again, simply wiggle the loops back and forth to pop them apart easily.

### Why Bowstrings Are Twisted

- The primary reason for twisting: ensure the string becomes and remains perfectly round and consistent.
- When a string has zero twists, individual strands separate and flatten out. An untwisted, flattened string acts inconsistently on release—erratic string path, loud/rattling bow sound, and string slapping the arm.
- Twisting locks the strands together into a tight, circular shape, allowing consistent arrow delivery and greatly reducing vibration.
- The secondary purpose: precisely adjust brace height. Twisting the string shortens it, which pre-bends the limbs further and raises brace height.

### Diagnosing Too Few Twists

- The Strand Test: If you can easily grab the string and separate the individual strands with your fingers, you do not have enough twists.
- The Catch-22: If your string has zero twists and strands are separating, but your brace height is already correct (or too high), you cannot simply add twists—doing so will shorten the string and push brace height out of range. In this case, the string is physically too short for the bow; you must purchase or build a slightly longer string.

### Diagnosing Too Many Twists

- The "V" Crossover: Look at the end loops where the string serving forms a "V" shape around the limb tips. On a properly twisted string, this V will lay completely flat against the limb. If there are too many twists, tension will cause one side of the loop to twist upward and cross over the top of the other, refusing to lay flat.
- Balling Up: When the bow is unstrung and tension is removed, an over-twisted string will immediately want to knot itself up and bundle into a tight ball.
- Inconsistent Roundness: If the string is twisted far too tightly, it will stop being perfectly round. Individual strands may poke or bulge out of the main bundle because tension is too severe.

### The Nocking Point Myth (Debunked)

- The Myth: Many archers believe that adding twists exclusively to the bottom of the string and removing twists from the top can move the nocking point up or down like a threaded nut.
- The Reality: This is false. Adding 100 twists to the bottom and removing 100 from the top results in the exact same string length and does not move the centre nocking point. Once the bow is strung and the string plucked, uneven twists normalise and distribute evenly across the entire string length.

### String Waxing

How often to wax and proper application technique—sourced from Jake Kaminski.

How Often to Wax

- Wax the string whenever it needs it—there is no fixed schedule.
- Signs It Needs Wax: A "fuzzy," white, or frayed appearance on the exposed string material (not the servings). This typically occurs where the string rubs against clothing or the body between shots.
- Before Inclement Weather: Wax before shooting in rain or damp conditions. Wax forms a hydrophobic barrier that prevents the string from absorbing water. A water-logged string becomes heavier, altering the bow's tune and degrading performance.

Why Wax Is Important

- Protection: Shields the string from dirt, grime, and moisture.
- Aesthetics & Consistency: Restores the string's vivid colour and lustre. This matters especially for single-string archers who use the string blur for alignment—a frayed, light-coloured string looks different from a clean, dark one, throwing off alignment and shot consistency.
- Avoid Distractions: Stops stray frayed fibres from tickling your nose or face at full draw.

How to Apply

1. Apply Sparingly: A small amount of wax to the exposed string material only (not the servings). Wax can be synthetic or beeswax.
2. Use Friction: Pinch the string between thumb and index finger and rub back and forth briskly. Friction-generated heat melts the wax, letting it flow into the base fibres.
3. Clean & Shape (Optional): After rubbing in the wax, take a small piece of loose serving thread, wrap it around the string, and pull it down the length. This removes excess wax and makes the string perfectly round and even—restoring it to a "brand new" appearance and feel.

What to Avoid

- Don't Over-Wax: Daily waxing is unnecessary. It doesn't harm the string but is messy and redundant.
- Avoid the Servings: End servings and the centre serving (nocking point area) generally do not need wax, except during a complete downpour where maximum water resistance is wanted.

Alternative View—Wax Every Session

Kazden Ammons (Shane Doran-style tutorials) recommends waxing the string every single time you shoot, arguing that this level of care can make a bowstring last effectively a lifetime. This is more aggressive than Kaminski's "wax when needed" approach. Both agree over-waxing does not harm the string—apply as per your shooting volume and preference.

### Tag End Trimming (Flemish Twist Strings)

Flemish twist bowstrings are woven rather than knotted at the end loops. After hundreds of shots, tiny stray fibres ("tag ends") naturally pop up near the fade-outs (the transition where string material meets the loop weave).

- Does it affect performance? No. Tag ends are cosmetic only.
- How to trim:
  1. Use an extremely sharp tool—X-Acto knife or razor blade. Do not burn them off and do not use a dull knife.
  2. Carefully lift the stray fibre.
  3. Cut away from the main string body to avoid nicking the core fibres.

---

### Serving Replacement (Knotless Method)

The serving is the protective thread wrapped around the centre of the string where the arrow is nocked. It will wear out or loosen after 3,000–5,000 shots (depending on quality) and must be re-served.

The Knotless Method:

1. Pin the Tail: Lay about an inch of serving thread flat against the bowstring in the direction you intend to serve. Tightly wrap the first 15 twists _over_ that loose tail to pin it down permanently. Trim the excess tail.
2. Use a Serving Jig: A quality jig ($30–$35 recommended) is essential for consistent tension. Serve down the string covering the nocking area and extending a few inches below to protect against arm-guard slap.
3. The Back Serve (to finish without a knot):
   - Take the spool _under_ the string to create a loop.
   - Back-serve through that loop 15 times.
   - Pull the serving tight, wrapping over the back-served section while simultaneously unwrapping the loop.
   - This pulls the final tail completely under the outer wraps, creating a permanent, knotless lock.
4. Trim: Cut the final thread perfectly flush with a razor blade.

When to Replace: If the serving itself is breaking down and exposing the underlying string material, it is time to replace the string entirely. Serving replacement extends the string's life; it is not a full repair for a failed string.
