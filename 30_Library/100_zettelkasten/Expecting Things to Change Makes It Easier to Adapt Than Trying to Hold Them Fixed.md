## Expecting Things to Change Makes It Easier to Adapt Than Trying to Hold Them Fixed

Treating plans, self-images and circumstances as temporary makes it easier to adapt when they change than trying to hold them fixed. Zen practice trains this by accepting that everything, including oneself, is always changing. ^claim

### Scope & Conditions

- Applies to things that will change whatever you do, and to the form that valued things take over time.
- Accepting a change is not approving of it or giving up on influencing what happens next.
- Commitments and values can still be held steady while the plans that serve them are revised.

### Evidence

> "if we can accept this impermanence and embrace it, life becomes less of a struggle."
> (Babauta 2023, "Seeing and Working with True Nature", PDF p. 82) · [open in Calibre](calibre://view-book/GCcalibreBooks/741/EPUB?open_at=toc:Seeing%20and%20Working%20with%20True%20Nature)

> "Instead of resisting the impermanence, if we can accept and embrace it, let go of our ideal of what we want life to be, perhaps we’ll struggle less."
> (Babauta 2023, "How to cope with loss", PDF p. 86) · [open in Calibre](calibre://view-book/GCcalibreBooks/741/EPUB?open_at=toc:How%20to%20cope%20with%20loss)

Babauta, writing for a general reader about Zen practice, supports less struggle, and does so tentatively in the second passage. The step from less struggle to easier adaptation is my own reasoning.

### Implications

- Write plans and routines expecting to revise them, and treat a revision as normal rather than as failure.
- Hold a self-description loosely enough that new evidence can change it.
- When a change causes distress, ask which earlier state you are still trying to keep.

### Falsifier

- Compare how people who expect revision and people who expect stability cope with the same disrupted plan. If the first group does not resume useful action sooner or report less distress, the claim is wrong.

### Steelman

Expecting everything to change can weaken the effort to protect what is worth keeping. A person who accepts too readily may adapt to a bad situation that could have been resisted.
