## Asking What Led to a Conclusion Invites Self-Examination Where Correcting the Error Invites Defence

Asking "what led to that conclusion?" invites a person to pick up their own conclusion and examine it, whereas correcting the error invites them to defend it. The question shifts the exchange from error-correction by the critic to trace-logging by the person, so any decoupling of the belief from themselves starts with them. ^claim

### Scope & Conditions

Applies when the question is genuine, asked from real interest in the answer, and when the person can tell how they reached the view. It is weaker when the question is a trap or the person has no access to how they reached the view. It may take longer than a direct correction, and it gives no guarantee that the belief changes.

### Evidence

The core is the author's own reasoning, first worded in an AI conversation (Gemini, 2026-07-13) captured in a HEAD note no longer found under that name, so that origin is unverified. One book supports the kind of question that works. It treats a question you do not already know the answer to as the core of its method, and it does not test identity-fused beliefs:

> "asking questions to which you do not already know the answer"
> (Schein and Schein 2012, "Introduction: What Is Humble Inquiry?", PDF p. 20) · [open in Calibre](calibre://view-book/GCcalibreBooks/467/EPUB?open_at=toc:Introduction:%20What%20Is%20Humble%20Inquiry%3F)

### Implications

- Replace "that's wrong because…" with a real question about how the person reached the conclusion, and then listen to the answer.
- Let the person do the examining. The critic's task is to keep the question open and not to supply the verdict.
- The question works as a step of the longer approach in [[How to Critique an Idea With Someone Whose Identity Is Fused to It]].

### Falsifier

- Evidence that asking what led to a conclusion produces as much defensiveness as correcting it, or that people examine a belief no more when asked than when corrected.
