## Pro-Social Punishment Restores Cooperation

In the Public Goods Game, a multi-person prisoner's dilemma, contributions to the common pool typically rise when players are allowed to pay to punish free riders. This happens even when the punishers have nothing to gain from punishing.

### Scope & Conditions

Laboratory finding by Fehr and Gaechter using costly punishment. Why people punish is debated: one view treats it as a by-product of reciprocity and reputation management, another as the result of selection at the level of groups.

### Evidence

> "When experimenters introduce the opportunity to punish, contributions typically go up. Critically, this happens even when the punishers have nothing to gain from punishing and everyone knows it."
> (Joshua Greene, Moral Tribes, Chapter 2: Moral Machinery)

### Implications

- The willingness to pay a personal cost to punish free riders helps keep shared resources intact.
- Contributions typically go up when punishment is possible, compared with the same game without it.
