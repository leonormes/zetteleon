Rejection Sensitive Dysphoria (RSD) is a specialized, intense emotional response common in ADHD, characterized by extreme sensitivity to perceived or actual rejection, criticism, or failure. It is a neurological event, not a character flaw; the brain's emotional processing centers perceive social disapproval as a direct physical threat, triggering a "fight or flight" response to social stimuli.

---

## 1. The Neurological Mechanism

RSD is rooted in the ADHD brain's dysregulated emotional processing (see [[MOC - Emotional Dysregulation]]):

- The Failed Filter: The brain fails to modulate the intensity of social signals. A minor critique is amplified into a "shout" that overrides logical evaluation.
- Physical Pain: The "dysphoria" (Greek for "hard to bear") is often described as physical pain, located in the chest or stomach, making it distinguishably different from typical social anxiety.
- Instantaneous Onset: Unlike the slow buildup of social anxiety, RSD triggers are near-instantaneous and can lead to immediate "Emotional Flooding."

## 2. Common Defense Mechanisms

To protect against the intense pain of RSD, individuals often adopt one of three "shields":

1. People-Pleasing: Becoming a "chameleon" to ensure constant approval and avoid even the possibility of a negative reaction.
2. Perfectionism: Working to an impossible standard so that no one can find a reason to criticize (linked to the "Shame-Procrastination Cycle" in [[SoT - ADHD Neurology & Core Concepts]]).
3. Withdrawal/Avoidance: Avoiding new situations, work sharing, or social interactions entirely to eliminate the risk of rejection.
4. Defensive Hypervigilance: Anxiously scanning one's own work for flaws before anyone else can find them, so that no criticism arrives unprepared for—see [[Defensive Hypervigilance]], which already names RSD as its underlying mechanism.

## 3. Impact on Mastery and Growth

RSD acts as a powerful amplifier for [[ADHD Emotional Reasoning]], where the _feeling_ of being disliked or failing is interpreted as an objective _fact_. This creates a "Capability Ceiling" by:

- Preventing the "stress-testing" of models (fear of being wrong).
- Derailing the "Point of Performance" through anticipatory anxiety.
- Reinforcing the belief that one is "broken" rather than "differently wired."
