## Shallow Recognition Signals Masquerade as Causal Understanding Until Tested

People infer understanding from shallow indirect signals—familiarity, recognition, belief alignment, surface explanation, exposure, fluency, and overconfidence—rather than auditing causal knowledge. None of these cues establishes whether a person can explain a mechanism in detail, predict novel behaviour, specify how parts interact, or troubleshoot failures. ^claim

### Scope & Conditions

Holds whenever a person evaluates their own understanding of a complex system without an external test. Seven shallow markers routinely produce a false feeling of understanding:

- **Familiarity**: encountering a concept often in daily life or headlines.
- **Recognition**: knowing the domain vocabulary without its underlying mechanics.
- **Belief alignment**: feeling that an idea is obviously true because it fits prior beliefs.
- **Surface explanation**: stating a high-level gist while omitting how components interact.
- **Exposure**: skimming articles or documentaries without self-testing.
- **Fluency**: speaking smoothly about a topic even when the explanation is thin or wrong.
- **Overconfidence**: projecting general confidence onto an unexamined domain.

The real test asks four questions: can you explain the mechanism in detail, predict how it behaves in a new situation, specify how its parts interact, and troubleshoot it when it breaks?

### Evidence

Grounded in [[Evidence - Sloman and Fernbach Find the Illusion of Understanding Is Robust Across Topics]], which reports that felt understanding routinely outruns explanatory competence across political, scientific, and personal-finance topics. The seven-signal list and four-part test are a vault synthesis operationalising how [[2026-07-25-heuristic-substitution-creates-ioed]] generates false positives; no direct primary-source quotation lists all seven signals verbatim.

### Implications

Self-assessment based on subjective ease or vocabulary recognition is unreliable for judging readiness to debug, teach, or build. Reliable calibration requires replacing passive review with generative tests—step-by-step explanation, novel prediction, or implementation.
