## The Traditional Definition of Knowledge is Justified True Belief

On the traditional analysis, a person knows a proposition if and only if it is true, the person believes it, and the person is justified in believing it. Each of the three conditions is claimed to be necessary, and together they are claimed to be sufficient. ^claim

### Scope & Conditions

- The note is built from three necessity claims, each held in its own note ([[Truth is a Necessary Condition for Knowledge]], [[Believing a Proposition Is a Necessary Condition for Knowing It]], [[Knowledge Requires Justification Because a True Belief Reached by Guesswork Is Not Knowledge]]), plus one further claim of its own: that the three together are enough. The challenges attack the parts separately, and the sufficiency claim is the one [[Gettier Problems Challenge the Traditional Definition of Knowledge]] targets.
- It defines propositional knowledge, knowing that something is the case. It does not cover knowing how or knowing a person.
- "Belief" here is the component of knowledge. It is not the provisional working model of [[Belief Between Fact and Faith]], which is a different use of the word.
- "Traditional" is a retrospective label. Plato's _Theaetetus_ examines the account and does not endorse it, and the explicit three-clause form is usually credited to Ayer and Chisholm in the 1950s. This reading is the note's own and is unchecked.
- The note is the vault's starting definition of knowledge. It states a position that many hold and that a larger literature challenges. It is not a settled verdict, and the wider map is [[MOC - From Information to Knowledge]].

### Evidence

One book states the analysis as the view of many epistemologists, which fits the standard textbook picture:

> "So it has become the view of many epistemologists that knowledge is justified true belief."
> (Martin 2014, ch. 3, PDF p. 33) · [open in Calibre](calibre://view-book/GCcalibreBooks/298/EPUB?open_at=toc:3%20Justification%20and%20Gettier%20problems)

The historical points rest on sources not yet quoted: Plato, _Theaetetus_ and _Meno_, Ayer (1956), Chisholm (1957) and Gettier, "Is Justified True Belief Knowledge?", _Analysis_ 23(6), 1963.

### Implications

- The analysis turns one question, what is knowledge, into attackable parts, so the later literature is organised by the part under attack: truth, belief, justification, or sufficiency.
- Justification carries the most weight. Internalists say the knower must reach it from the inside ([[Internalism Requires Conscious First-Person Access to Justification]]), and externalists say a reliable process can supply it ([[Goldman Reliabilism Grounds Knowledge in a Reliable Belief-Forming Mechanism]]).
- The usual repairs after Gettier add a fourth condition. Knowledge-first views reject the analysis itself ([[Knowledge-First Epistemology Treats Knowing as Basic and Believing as Derived]]).

### Falsifier

- A convincing case in which a person plainly knows a proposition while one of the three conditions fails, or a convincing argument that the three together can be met without knowledge and that no further condition can close the gap. The second has been argued since 1963.

### Tensions

- [[Popper Replaced Justification With Conjecture That Has Survived Criticism]]—the Popperian view treats theories as conjectures that survive criticism and never as positively justified, which this definition assumes.
- [[Probabilistic Thinking Treats Beliefs as Hypotheses With Confidence Levels]]—JTB treats belief as all-or-nothing, whereas graded confidence replaces it with a degree that moves with evidence.
