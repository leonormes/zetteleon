## The Traditional Definition of Knowledge is Justified True Belief

On the traditional analysis, a person knows a proposition if and only if three conditions hold: the proposition is true, the person believes it, and the person is justified in believing it. Each condition is claimed to be necessary and the three together are claimed to be sufficient. The analysis is traced to Plato and was the textbook default until Gettier's 1963 counterexamples.

### Scope & Conditions

- It defines propositional knowledge, knowing that something is the case. It says nothing about knowing how or knowing a person, which [[Aristotle Distinguished Between Episteme, Techne, and Phronesis]] and [[Types of Non-Linguistic Knowledge]] treat separately.
- "Necessary" and "sufficient" are separate claims. Gettier attacks only sufficiency. The necessity of each condition has been contested on its own: pragmatism disputes the truth leg ([[Pragmatic Truth Focuses on Utility Over Absolute Correctness]]), a person who answers correctly with no confidence has been argued to know without believing, and externalists drop the demand that the knower can state their justification ([[Nozick's Tracking Theory Grounds Knowledge in Truth-Tracking Across Counterfactuals]]).
- "Belief" here is the component of knowledge. It is not the provisional working model of [[Belief Between Fact and Faith]], which is a different use of the word.
- "Traditional" is a retrospective label. In Plato's _Theaetetus_ the account of knowledge as true belief with an account (logos) is examined and the dialogue ends without endorsing it, and the _Meno_ says true opinion becomes knowledge when it is tied down by reasoning out the cause. The explicit three-clause form is usually credited to mid-twentieth-century analytic epistemologists such as Ayer and Chisholm.
- This note is the vault's starting definition of knowledge and is flagged `axiom: true`. It is a map of the ground, not a settled verdict. Where the definition is extended or contested, the vault carries that in the linked notes below. The wider map is [[MOC - From Information to Knowledge]].

### Evidence

No verbatim quotation has been captured yet. The formulation above is the standard textbook statement and this note's own reconstruction. The historical points rest on these sources, to be quoted from the texts when they are next read:

- Plato, _Theaetetus_ (Stephanus 201c to 210) and _Meno_ (97a to 98a).
- A. J. Ayer, _The Problem of Knowledge_ (1956), and Roderick Chisholm, _Perceiving_ (1957), for the explicit three-condition form.
- Edmund Gettier, "Is Justified True Belief Knowledge?", _Analysis_ 23(6), 1963, pp. 121 to 123.

### Implications

- The analysis turns one question, what is knowledge, into three separately attackable ones, which is why the later literature is organised by the condition under attack: truth, belief or justification.
- The justification leg carries the most weight. A belief that is true by luck is not knowledge, so what the justification must do is connect the belief to the truth. Internalism says the knower must be able to reach that connection from the inside ([[Internalism Requires Conscious First-Person Access to Justification]]). Externalism says the connection can hold without the knower seeing it ([[Goldman's Reliabilism Grounds Knowledge in a Reliable Belief-Forming Mechanism]]).
- Gettier-style cases are possible because justification can fall short of guaranteeing truth. The usual repairs add a fourth condition: no false lemmas, a causal link, reliability, tracking, safety or defeasibility. A "knowledge-first" view (Williamson) reverses the order and takes knowledge as basic rather than analysing it.
- The definition separates knowing from merely holding information. The vault's contrast between the two ([[Comparison - Knowing vs Understanding]]) adds a further step beyond JTB, from knowing a fact to understanding why it holds.
- Most of what any person counts as known rests on testimony and trusted procedures, not on personal verification. Whether that counts as justification is the question raised by [[Trusting a Scientific Authority Feels Psychologically Identical to Trusting a False One]].

### Tensions

- [[Deutsch Rejects Empiricism for Conjecture-Led Theory Formation]]—tension, not contradiction: this definition assumes knowledge is a justified state of the knower, whereas the Popperian view the Deutsch notes follow treats theories as conjectures that survive criticism and never as positively justified.
- [[Probabilistic Thinking Treats Beliefs as Hypotheses With Confidence Levels]]—tension, not contradiction: JTB treats belief as all-or-nothing, whereas graded confidence replaces it with a degree that moves with evidence.
