## Neuroplasticity

Neuroplasticity is the capacity of a brain to alter its physical structure and functional organisation in response to experience, learning or injury. Defining the term states what plasticity means, not whether or how much a given brain exhibits it; the empirical claim that human brains retain this capacity across life sits in [[The Brain Changes Its Structure and Function in Response to Experience Throughout Life]]. ^def

### Scope & Conditions

- Encompasses the formation, strengthening, weakening and pruning of synaptic connections as well as larger-scale cortical remapping.
- Value-neutral: the term covers unhelpful habits and harmful conditioning just as much as deliberate skill acquisition.
- Distinct from any single mechanism within it, such as synaptic strengthening ([[Long-Term Potentiation (LTP) is a Key Cellular Mechanism for Learning]]), synaptic weakening ([[Long-Term Depression is a Mechanism for Breaking Habits]]), or memory reorganisation across brain regions ([[Systems Consolidation is the Gradual Reorganization of Memory in the Brain]]).
- Does not specify rate, magnitude or age limits; those are empirical questions, not parts of the definition.

### Evidence

Standard textbook usage defines the term directly as the capacity of the brain to change itself through learning:

> "The brain’s capacity to learn—and thus change itself—is called neuroplasticity"
> (Hanson 2015, ch. 4, PDF p. 75) · [open in Calibre](calibre://view-book/GCcalibreBooks/358/EPUB?open_at=toc:Chapter%204.%20Taking%20in%20the%20Good)

This establishes lexical usage rather than an empirical effect size.

### Implications

- Separates conceptual meaning from testable fact: even if adult brains were rigid, the word would still name the capacity to change.
- Supplies a shared umbrella concept connecting cellular mechanisms (Hebbian learning, LTP, LTD) to behavioural outcomes such as study, habit change and recovery.
