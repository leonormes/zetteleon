## Illusion of Explanatory Depth (IoED)

The illusion of explanatory depth is the idea that people tend to overestimate their understanding of concepts.

This is because we often judge our understanding based on signals, rather than direct assessment.

[Knowledge Aquisition and Zettelkasten](Knowledge%20Aquisition%20and%20Zettelkasten.md)

In this context, "based on signals" refers to cues or superficial indicators that give people a sense of understanding without fully testing or analyzing their actual knowledge. Signals are external or shallow markers that can make us _feel_ informed or competent about a subject, even if our grasp of it is not deep.

For example, we might feel we understand a concept because:

- It seems familiar (we've heard it discussed often).
- We can name parts or related ideas (we know some terminology or buzzwords).
- It aligns with our existing beliefs (we see it as intuitively correct).

These signals make us believe we understand a concept, even though we haven't done the hard work of explaining or teaching it, which would reveal gaps in our knowledge. This is at the core of the _illusion of explanatory depth_—we mistake these signals for real understanding.

Let me explain what "based on signals" means in the context of the Illusion of Explanatory Depth.

When we say people judge their understanding "based on signals," it means we rely on indirect indicators or cues rather than testing our actual detailed knowledge. Here are some examples:

1. Familiarity signals: "I see this concept mentioned often, so I must understand it"

- Like thinking you understand how a car works because you drive one daily
- Or believing you grasp economics because you regularly read financial news headlines

1. Recognition signals: "I recognize the terms involved, so I must understand the concept"

- Like thinking you understand photosynthesis because you know words like "chlorophyll" and "carbon dioxide"
- Or believing you grasp democracy because you're familiar with terms like "voting" and "representation"

1. Surface-level signals: "I can explain the basic idea, so I must understand it deeply"

- Like thinking you understand how a bicycle works because you can say "pedaling moves the chain which turns the wheel"
- But when asked to explain exactly how the gears interact or why the bike stays upright, you realize your understanding is superficial

The key insight is that these signals can make us feel confident in our understanding without actually testing whether we can:

- Explain the mechanism in detail
- Predict how the system would behave in new situations
- Identify how different parts interact
- Troubleshoot problems

Would you like me to provide some specific examples of how this plays out in particular domains?

In the context of the Illusion of Explanatory Depth (IoED), "based on signals" refers to the indirect cues and mental shortcuts people use to judge their understanding of a concept, instead of directly assessing their actual knowledge.

Here are some examples of these signals:

- Familiarity with the term: We often mistake familiarity with a concept's name or basic definition for a deep understanding of its underlying mechanisms or implications. For example, you might feel like you understand "democracy" because you hear the word frequently, but struggle to explain its various forms or how it functions in practice.
- Exposure to related information: Reading a few articles or watching documentaries on a topic can create an illusion of knowledge, even if we haven't actively engaged with the material or tested our understanding.
- Fluency in discussing the topic: Being able to talk about a concept in a casual setting can mislead us into thinking we have a strong grasp of it, even if our explanations are superficial or inaccurate.
- Confidence in our abilities: Overconfidence in our general knowledge or intelligence can lead us to overestimate our understanding of specific concepts.

Essentially, these signals provide us with a sense of familiarity and ease with a concept, which we often misinterpret as a deep understanding. However, when asked to explain the concept in detail, provide examples, or answer specific questions, the gaps in our knowledge become apparent.

This reliance on signals rather than direct assessment is a key factor contributing to the Illusion of Explanatory Depth. We tend to overestimate our understanding because we rely on these superficial cues instead of actively engaging with the material and testing our knowledge.
