## The Continuum Thought Experiment

Imagine a genuinely continuous quantity—one with no natural minimal units at all: an idealised length of line, or a volume of some substance with no atoms, molecules, or any other built-in stopping point for division. Ask the question: how many parts does it have?

There is no principled answer. The quantity can be divided in half, then in half again, indefinitely, and every division point is exactly as arbitrary as every other—nothing in the continuum itself marks out where one "part" ends and the next begins. Any number offered—two parts, ten, a trillion—is equally valid and equally arbitrary, because a true continuum supplies no natural seams to count along.

### Scope & Conditions

A purely conceptual limiting case: real physical substances are ultimately discrete at some scale (atoms, molecules, quanta), so this thought experiment describes an idealised, infinitely divisible continuum, not a claim about any actual material. Its purpose is to isolate what discreteness contributes to counting by imagining its complete absence.

### Relationship to the Sorites Paradox

This is the limiting case of the vagueness the Sorites Paradox explores. Sorites shows that a boundary can be *fuzzy*—a heap doesn't stop being a heap at one precise grain, but the grains themselves remain discrete, countable units throughout. The continuum thought experiment removes the boundary altogether: there is no fuzzy edge to disagree about, because there is no edge of any kind anywhere, until one is imposed on the continuum from outside it.

### Implications

- Counting is not merely easier with discrete objects—it is undefined without them. There is no "correct" count of the parts of a true continuum, only a count of the divisions someone chooses to impose on it.
- This is the thought-experiment form of the claim in [[Discreteness as a Precondition for Counting]]: rather than arguing directly that discreteness is required, it shows what happens in discreteness's total absence, and finds that quantity itself disappears along with it.
