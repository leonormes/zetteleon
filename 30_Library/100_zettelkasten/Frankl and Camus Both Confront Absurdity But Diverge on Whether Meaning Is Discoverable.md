## Frankl and Camus Both Confront Absurdity But Diverge on Whether Meaning Is Discoverable

Both Viktor Frankl and Albert Camus begin from the same premise: life carries no guaranteed, pre-given meaning in a universe that returns no answers to human demands. Their responses diverge fundamentally. Frankl holds that meaning is _discoverable_ within the absurd—detectable in the world, in relationships, and in one's stance toward suffering—and that hope grounded in this discovery is honest. Camus holds that hope is _philosophical suicide_—a leap of faith that evades the Absurd rather than confronting it—and that the only authentic response is revolt: living with full awareness of meaninglessness while refusing to despair of the present moment. Frankl's framework is therapeutic; Camus's is philosophical and rebellious.

### Scope & Conditions

A philosophical comparison of two existential responses to the same diagnosis. Choosing between them hinges on whether one treats meaning as intrinsic (detectable) or instrumental (created). In practice, the frameworks are not mutually exclusive as life strategies—both prescribe active engagement, connection, and presence—but they differ irreconcilably on the metaphysical status of meaning and the role of hope.

### Evidence

> "Frankl… insists meaning is discoverable within [absurdity]… Camus rejects traditional hope… one must accept the absurd and live authentically without illusions."

### Implications

- Frankl's framework provides a therapeutic scaffold for those who need hope as a motivational anchor; Camus's provides integrity for those who cannot accept hope without epistemic honesty.
- The practical prescriptions converge—both require engaged living, connection, and facing suffering—even where the metaphysical ground differs.
