## ADHD Working Memory Deficits Create a Compulsive Re-Planning Loop

Summary: Deficits in working memory foster a profound lack of self-trust in the ADHD brain, leading to a compulsive need to mentally re-check and re-verify plans, creating a debilitating "re-planning loop."

Details: The "out of sight, out of mind" nature of ADHD working memory means that steps in a plan can vanish from the mental workspace if not actively focused on. Acutely aware of this unreliability, the brain develops a cognitive insecurity and a fear of forgetting a critical detail. This anxiety fuels a cycle of cognitive rumination where the individual repeatedly re-reads instructions and mentally rehearses the plan, substituting the act of planning for the act of doing. This cycle is a primary engine for anxiety and negative self-talk.

### Overall Verdict

The statement that "ADHD working memory deficits create a compulsive re-planning loop" is conceptually supported in key phenomenological and neurobiological respects, but it requires critical qualification and distinction when evaluated against the sources.

While the sources strongly confirm that working memory deficits breed profound self-distrust [1, 2], repetitive checking behaviors ("pirouette syndrome") [3], and mental looping within the Default Mode Network (DMN) [3-5], they also emphasize that the primary, hallmark manifestation of ADHD is under-planning, impulsivity, and "time blindness," rather than excessive or compulsive planning [6-9]. Furthermore, what appears subjectively as a "re-planning loop" is classified by the sources not as actual executive planning, but as toxic emotional rumination and catastrophizing [3, 5, 10, 11].

### Supporting Evidence from the Sources

#### 1. Working Memory Deficits Breed Deep Self-Distrust

Both Russell Barkley and Edward Hallowell document that adults with ADHD suffer from significant impairments in both nonverbal working memory (the "mind's eye," which holds sensory imagery and sequences) and verbal working memory (the "mind's voice," which governs internal self-talk, rule extraction, and reflection) [12-14].

- Porous Immediate Memory: Immediate memory in ADHD can be so porous that intentions and instructions evaporate almost instantaneously [15].
- Accumulated Life Failure: Because of these lapses, adults with ADHD store up in their memory banks a lifetime of moments of failure, reprimands, embarrassment, and missed deadlines [2, 4].
- Loss of Trust in One's Instincts: Barkley specifically notes that adults with ADHD often lack faith in their own mental faculties [1]. For example, he presents the case of Kaye, an adult with ADHD who follows rigid workplace rules obsessively precisely _"because she doesn't trust her instincts"_ and uses external rules to keep anxiety from consuming her [1]. Hallowell similarly notes that individuals live with constant dread and panic, asking themselves: _"What did we forget? What might we say wrong? What didn't we do right lately?"_ [11].

#### 2. Compulsive Mental Checking and Re-Verifying: "Pirouette Syndrome"

The sources provide direct evidence of a compulsive checking cycle driven by working memory and attentional failures:

- In _ADHD 2.0_, Hallowell and Ratey introduce the concept of "pirouette syndrome", defining it as _"a circling back to make sure you've done something you've already done"_ (such as repeatedly checking if the front door is locked, the stove is turned off, or keys and sunglasses are lost) [3].
- Crucially, the authors explain the mechanism: because the individual was not actively engaged in their Task-Positive Network (TPN) at the exact moment they performed the action, the event was never properly registered in working memory [3]. This lack of internal encoding produces a _"niggling doubt [that] keeps you panicked until you can go back and check"_ [3].

#### 3. The Neurobiology of the Mental Loop (DMN Activation & Over-Synchronization)

The neurological mechanics in _ADHD 2.0_ and the Helsinki MEG study explain how this loop becomes self-reinforcing:

- The Glitchy Toggle Switch: In neurotypical brains, entering goal-directed task activity (TPN) turns off the Default Mode Network (DMN); in ADHD, the DMN fails to deactivate and competes directly with the TPN [16, 17].
- Rumination in the Frontal DMN: While the rear half of the DMN looks back into past personal history, the front part (medial prefrontal cortex) looks forward to plan and project [18]. Hallowell notes that when trapped in this front portion of the DMN, an individual _"ruminates on plans and make[s] lists,"_ going _"over and over and over"_ future scenarios, conversations, and contingencies because the brain projects humiliating failures happening again [5]. This trap is termed the _"infinite web of what-if"_ [19] or Chicken Little syndrome (endlessly obsessing over what could go wrong rather than executing) [10].
- Neural Over-Synchronization: The University of Helsinki study indicates that ADHD brain networks struggle with state-switching and show stronger long-range temporal correlations [20]. When neural circuits become overly synchronized, attention can become _"too tightly coupled or overly locked into rhythm,"_ providing a physiological explanation for why the brain gets _"stuck in a groove (hyperfocus, perseveration, or rumination)"_ [20, 21].

### Refuting and Nuancing Evidence from the Sources

While the _consequences_ of working memory failure can manifest as obsessive mental re-checking, the sources raise three major contradictions to characterizing ADHD as primarily defined by a "re-planning loop":

#### 1. The Core Trait of ADHD Is Impulsive Under-Planning, Not Over-Planning

Dr. Barkley argues extensively that ADHD is a disorder of "time blindness" and poor forethought, where behavior is dominated by the immediate "now" rather than preparation for the "later" [8, 9, 22]:

- Adults with ADHD typically do not plan ahead; they fail to activate mental representations of the future and arrive unprepared at deadlines [8, 22, 23].
- Barkley highlights that individuals with ADHD frequently make decisions impulsively, change plans on a whim, skip reading directions, and lunge into action without deliberating over alternatives [6, 7, 24].
- Thus, the typical presentation is failing to plan and acting without foresight, rather than being paralyzed by compulsive over-planning [7, 8, 25].

#### 2. DMN "Re-Planning" Is Emotional Rumination, Not Executive Planning

The sources explicitly distinguish between true executive planning and DMN rumination:

- Executive planning and problem-solving require activating the prefrontal cortex—holding multiple pieces of information in the "mind's playground," disassembling them, and systematically sequencing future actions [26, 27]. Because this process draws heavily on working memory and effortful mental control, adults with ADHD find formal planning slow, tedious, and difficult, and they frequently avoid it [7, 26, 28, 29].
- The endless circling over future plans described in the DMN is classified by Hallowell not as functional planning, but as toxic worry and catastrophic thinking [5, 10, 11]. In fact, Hallowell points out that individuals with ADHD often use worry and catastrophic mental loops as an unconscious form of self-stimulation—an attempt to generate adrenaline and pain to chemically awaken an under-aroused, dopamine-deficient cortex [30, 31].

#### 3. Internal Re-Checking Cannot Fix Working Memory

The sources refute the idea that internal mental re-verification is an effective or typical adaptive solution:

- Barkley stresses that because the internal "mind's eye" and "mind's voice" are compromised, attempting to resolve self-distrust through purely mental means will always fail [32-34].
- The only effective remedy is externalizing the executive functions at the "point of performance": offloading memory onto physical tools (pocket journals, alarms, smartphone reminders, and visual checklists) so the brain does not have to rely on internal working memory loops [32, 35-37].

What you are describing is one of the most recognizable patterns in the ADHD and VAST literature. In the programming world, it often looks like an irresistible urge to rewrite an entire codebase or build a new framework from scratch instead of reusing an existing module.

When evaluated through the lens of Dr. Edward Hallowell, Dr. John Ratey, and Dr. Russell Barkley, this behavior is not a character flaw or poor discipline. It is a predictable collision between neurochemical reward seeking, the acute intolerance of boredom, and the creative engine of the ADHD brain [1-3].

---

### 1. "Boredom Is Kryptonite": The Pain of Repetition

In _ADHD 2.0_ and _Delivered from Distraction_, Hallowell and Ratey emphasize that for an ADHD or VAST brain, boredom is not just a mild inconvenience—it is "kryptonite" [1]. Hallowell notes that being bored can feel almost like physical asphyxiation; the moment stimulation drops, the brain sounds an emergency alarm and reflexively seeks an immediate way to alter its internal state [1, 4].

- Why Old Solutions Feel Obsolete: Once you have already solved a problem, your brain has extracted all the novelty, learning, and puzzle-solving stimulation from it [5]. Taking that old solution and copying or adapting it to a similar task offers virtually zero dopamine payoff [3, 6].
- The Neurochemistry of RDS: People with ADHD frequently have what researchers call Reward Deficiency Syndrome (RDS)—blunted dopamine signaling or premature reuptake that makes ordinary, repetitive tasks feel flat and devoid of satisfaction [3, 7]. To an ADHD brain, reusing an old script or template feels like eating cold leftovers, whereas designing a clean, elegant, "better" architecture from scratch triggers an immediate surge of dopamine, engaging your focus and lighting up your executive circuits [2, 3, 8].

### 2. "I'll Take the Really Hard Way": Creating the "Right Difficult"

In Chapter 5 of _ADHD 2.0_, Hallowell describes a patient named Jon whose story mirrors this exact dynamic: Jon confessed that he could not bear working on ordinary tasks and was driven by a constant need to tackle _"impossibly difficult projects"_ because anything less left him restless and anxious [9, 10].

- Manufacturing Challenge: When a programmer with ADHD is assigned a task that feels too familiar, the brain perceives it as beneath its creative capacity [2]. Left as-is, you would likely procrastinate or struggle to start at all [2, 11].
- The Unconscious Pivot: By deciding that the old solution is outdated and that you must rebuild it _"better this time,"_ you unconsciously transform a routine chore into "the right difficult" [2, 9]. You turn what would have been a lethargic, unmotivating task into a high-stakes architectural puzzle that demands genuine problem-solving, thereby waking up your prefrontal cortex [2, 8].

### 3. High "Quick Start," Low "Follow Thru"

In _ADHD 2.0_, Hallowell discusses the Kolbe Index, a tool measuring innate problem-solving instincts [12]. Most adults with ADHD score exceptionally high in Quick Start (leaping into new challenges, innovating, and taking risks) and very low in Follow Thru (maintaining established systems, routines, and standard operating procedures) [12].

- Individuals with this profile naturally resist fixed processes and prefer to let solutions evolve dynamically while working [12].
- Reusing an existing script requires operating in "maintenance mode"—the exact operational mode the ADHD brain resists [6, 12]. Starting fresh allows you to stay in permanent "greenfield" mode, where your imagination and creative energy thrive [2, 12].

### 4. Barkley on the Stimulation-Seeking Engine

Dr. Russell Barkley adds a crucial perspective in _Taking Charge of Adult ADHD_. He notes that individuals with ADHD do not have trouble understanding information; rather, they struggle with sustaining activation and effort on unrewarding, repetitive tasks over time [6, 13].

- Barkley calls individuals with ADHD "stimulation seekers" whose internal fuel tanks run dry without continuous, immediate reinforcement [6, 14].
- If you attempt to simply maintain or re-apply an old tool, the lack of intrinsic feedback makes it almost impossible to maintain concentration [6, 13]. Redesigning the system from scratch provides continuous, immediate feedback loops (writing new logic, debugging fresh errors, watching a superior pattern emerge), which supplies the fuel your working memory needs to remain engaged [6, 14].

### Management Strategies Grounded in the Sources

If you want to harness this drive without letting it derail your productivity, the sources recommend several concrete boundaries:

1. Adopt Hallowell's "Well Enough" Standard:
   In _Delivered from Distraction_, Hallowell highlights the story of Sharon, a brilliant creative who succeeded by aiming to be "well enough organized" rather than pursuing impossible, exhaustive perfection [24, 25]. In code, this means defining a strict threshold for _functional completion_: ask yourself, _"Is this old solution clean enough to achieve the business goal, even if it isn't a masterpiece?"_ [25].
2. Channel the Refactoring Itch into a Dedicated Sandbox:
   Hallowell notes that ADHD minds always need a legitimate creative outlet so their energy doesn't sabotage routine obligations [26, 27]. Give yourself permission to build the "ideal" version, but timebox it or isolate it in a side branch or proof-of-concept project. Never make the core deliverable depend on a full rewrite unless the existing code is demonstrably broken [18, 28].
3. Externalize Time Limits (Barkley's Chunking Rule):
   Barkley emphasizes that internal time perception in ADHD cannot be trusted [29, 30]. If you feel the urge to rewrite a component, set a visible external timer (e.g., a 45-minute countdown clock) [28, 31]. Tell yourself: _"I have 45 minutes to optimize this. If it is not substantially working by then, I revert to adapting the existing solution"_ [28].
4. Partner or Delegate the "Follow Thru":
   Hallowell frequently advises surrounding yourself with people whose cognitive strengths complement yours [32, 33]. Pair-programming with a colleague who excels at pragmatic integration and maintenance can ground your visionary architectural impulses, allowing you to innovate where it counts while preventing endless restarts [12, 33].
