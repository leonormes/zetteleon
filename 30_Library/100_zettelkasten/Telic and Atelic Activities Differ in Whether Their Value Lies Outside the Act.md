title: Telic and Atelic Activities Differ in Whether Their Value Lies Outside the Act
type: concept
---

A telic activity is defined by a terminus: writing the report, passing the exam, getting the promotion. Its structure is exhaustive—every moment spent on it is a moment of not-yet-having-finished, and the moment it succeeds, it ceases to exist. An atelic activity has no such terminus. Walking, listening to music, thinking about a problem: you are not _getting them done_, you are simply doing them, and you are doing them completely at every instant.

The distinction is Aristotle's (_Nicomachean Ethics_ Book X, on _energeia_ versus _kinesis_) and is given its modern statement by Kieran Setiya in _Midlife: A Philosophical Guide_. Setiya's diagnosis is that a life composed exclusively of telic projects is structurally self-undermining: you are either not-yet-finished (frustrated) or finished (bereft). Atelic activity is the only kind that can be sufficient in the present tense.

The distinction is not about difficulty, seriousness, or effort. Atelic does not mean easy or unproductive—it means the value is not deferred to a state after the activity.

## Steelman

The telic/atelic line is analytically clean but empirically blurry: most real activities are describable either way depending on the frame the agent brings. Reading a paper to prepare for a meeting is telic; the same reading, same hour, becomes atelic if the meeting is cancelled and you keep going. If the classification depends entirely on the agent's stance rather than the activity, it may be doing less work than it appears.
