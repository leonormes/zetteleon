## Evil Requires Both Culpability and Foreseeability—Without These It Is Tragedy

For an act to be classified as evil, the harm must be culpably inflicted by an agent with the capacity for moral judgement who could reasonably foresee the consequences of their actions. Without culpability (the capacity to have acted otherwise) or foreseeability (the ability to anticipate the harm), the event is a tragedy or accident—morally significant but not evil. Evil requires both the capacity for moral judgement and the knowledge that makes its exercise obligatory.

### Scope & Conditions

Distinguishes evil from accidental harm, natural disasters, harm caused by those lacking moral capacity, and harm resulting from unforeseeable consequences. The standard is "reasonable foreseeability"—not perfect knowledge, but the knowledge available to a moral agent in the perpetrator's position.

### Evidence

> "the perpetrator must have the capacity to act otherwise and the ability to reasonably foresee the consequences."

### Implications

- Evil is a moral category, not a harm-severity category—catastrophic harm without culpability and foreseeability is tragedy, not evil.
- Culpability is tied to the possession of relevant information and the capacity to recognise likely outcomes; deprivation of either shifts the moral classification.

### Related

- [[Akrasia is Acting Against One's Better Judgment]]—tension: akrasia describes acting against one's own better judgement—it implies both culpability and foreseeability, making it a necessary but insufficient condition for evil; evil adds the further requirement of intolerable harm and (typically) power asymmetry.
