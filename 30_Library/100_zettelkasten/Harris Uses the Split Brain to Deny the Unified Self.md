## Harris Uses the Split Brain to Deny the Unified Self

Summary: Harris argues the split-brain phenomenon poses an insurmountable problem for a single indivisible self or immortal soul, since a surgeon could in principle divide a person into two centres with different beliefs and eternal destinies.

Details: If severing the corpus callosum produces two centres of experience, then the person was never a single indivisible thing to begin with. Harris presses the theological version: were a patient's left hemisphere to accept the divinity of Jesus while the right did not, doctrine would have to allocate two souls to one skull. The argument targets the _metaphysically simple_ self—a permanent Cartesian substance—not the everyday feeling of being one person. Recognising that narrower target is essential, because objections that defend a _process_ or _emergent_ self do not actually contradict Harris; he already treats the self that way.

### Evidence

> "if a split-brain patient's left hemisphere accepts the divinity of Jesus, but the right hemisphere does not, religious dogma would have to account for two souls facing different eternal destinies."

### Implications

- The claim only bites against an indivisible-substance self, not an emergent-process self.
- Critiques that "the self is a real emergent process" partly restate Harris rather than refute him.
