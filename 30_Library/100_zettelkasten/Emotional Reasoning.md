## Emotional Reasoning

Emotional reasoning is the cognitive distortion in which internal feeling states are treated as authoritative evidence about external reality. It substitutes subjective affect for empirical verification, concluding that because something feels alarming, humiliating, or insurmountable, the situation must objectively possess those properties.

### Scope & Conditions

- Governs epistemic evaluation: the misuse of emotion as proof of external facts or self-worth.
- Does not invalidate emotional experience itself; an affective response remains genuine bodily data even when its cognitive premise is false.
- Becomes acutely amplified under high emotional lability, sensory flooding, or executive delay (e.g. rejection-sensitive reactivity).

### Evidence

> "You take your emotions as evidence for the truth. Your logic: 'I feel like a dud, therefore I am a dud.' This kind of reasoning is misleading because your feelings reflect your thoughts and beliefs. If they are distorted—as is quite often the case—your emotions will have no validity."
> (David D. Burns, *Feeling Good: The New Mood Therapy*, Ch. 3)

### Implications

- The validity of an emotion is decoupled from the validity of the premise that generated it.
- Corrective cognitive hygiene requires transforming the implicit premise "X is true" into the explicit report "I feel X", creating the reflective gap necessary to test evidence.
- Deficits in emotional inhibition turn transient distress into spurious facts unless intercepted by deliberate metacognitive pausing.
