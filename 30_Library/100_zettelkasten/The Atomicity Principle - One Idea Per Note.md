## The Atomicity Principle - One Idea Per Note

The atomicity principle says that each note holds exactly one idea. A single idea can be linked, contested and reused on its own, which a note that bundles several ideas cannot.

### Scope & Conditions

It applies to main (permanent) notes. Fleeting notes and literature notes are exempt, because they are temporary or shaped by their source. The working test in this vault is the card forcing function: if the idea will not fit on a small index card, or needs a "but" or a "however", it is probably two notes. The word "atomicity" comes from Christian Tietze's 2013 essay, as Bob Doto reports, and Doto adds that the right level of atomicity is personal.

### Evidence

> "they embody one single idea per note (atomicity principle)"
> (Main Notes Are the Essential Building Blocks, on the traits of a main note)

> "Capture the idea as if it must fit on a small index card. If it requires divergent points or counterarguments, it is too large."
> (Atomic Signal Extractor prompt, the Card Forcing Function)

> "Write exactly one note for each idea and write as if you were writing for someone else"
> (Sönke Ahrens, How to Take Smart Notes, chapter "Everything You Need to Do")

> "atomization, the practice of reducing ideas to their essential points."
> (Bob Doto, A System for Writing, section "Connecting Your Ideas")

> "The use of the word "atomicity" in this context comes from Christian Tietze's 2013 essay"
> (Bob Doto, A System for Writing, endnotes)

### Implications

- A note that answers two questions should be split, so each answer can be linked to and disputed separately.
- Length is a symptom, not the rule. The rule is one idea, and a long note usually holds more than one.
- Because each note is one idea, an existing note can be linked instead of restated.
