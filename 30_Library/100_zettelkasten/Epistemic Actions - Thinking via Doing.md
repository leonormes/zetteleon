## Epistemic Actions - Thinking via Doing

Epistemic actions are physical actions performed not to alter the world (pragmatic actions), but to simplify a cognitive task. By manipulating the environment directly, the brain offloads complex internal simulations onto the fast, reliable feedback loop of the physical world—thinking by doing. ^claim

### Scope & Conditions

Applies to tasks involving spatial reasoning, combinatorial search, or working-memory bottlenecks where physical or visual manipulation is cheap and reversible. Unlike pragmatic actions—such as moving a glass in order to drink from it—epistemic actions do not advance the physical goal-state directly; they change the agent's informational state to make the next cognitive step easier.

### Evidence

This note synthesises David Kirsh and Paul Maglio's (1994) distinction between epistemic and pragmatic actions with everyday examples rather than quoting a primary source verbatim. Observed instances include rotating a Tetris piece physically to see if it fits rather than rotating it mentally, sketching on a whiteboard to understand a system, shuffling Scrabble tiles to spot words, and navigating an Obsidian Graph to surface patterns too complex for mental visualisation.

### Implications

- Interactive and spatial media—whiteboards, physical tiles, canvases, and node graphs—function as active computational tools during thinking, not merely as presentation surfaces after thinking is finished.
- Offloading mental simulation into physical manipulation reduces internal working-memory demand, freeing cognitive capacity for judgement and synthesis.
