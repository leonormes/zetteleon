## Beliefs Often Function as Belonging Cues to Secure Group Membership Rather Than as Truth-Seeking Theses

For many people, adopting a political or social opinion is a tribal signal that secures psychological safety and group membership, rather than a conclusion reached through rigorous analysis. ^claim

### Scope & Conditions

Applies to highly cohesive groups and polarised environments where holding a specific belief is a prerequisite for acceptance within a community or in-group. The claim is that belonging is a distinct driver of belief adoption, not that such beliefs are never also reasoned. It is falsified if in-group belief convergence is fully explained by shared information and environment, with no added effect from identity-signalling.

### Implications

- When someone holds a poorly thought-out position, it is often not a failure of intellect but an efficient survival mechanism for their specific social environment.
- Judging someone for not "doing the work" on their beliefs unfairly expects them to act as independent logic engines rather than social survival mechanisms.

### Steelman

Group-aligned belief need not be a belonging signal. Heywood and Laing note that "social-base explanations allow for rationality insofar as group interests may help to shape party allegiances" (2026, ch. 10, PDF p. 601; [open in Calibre](calibre://view-book/GCcalibreBooks/1681/EPUB?open_at=toc:10.%20REPRESENTATION%2C%20ELECTIONS%20AND%20VOTING)). Carroll holds that "the beliefs we choose to adopt are shaped as much, if not more, by the beliefs we already have than by correspondence with external reality" (2016, ch. 14, EPUB only; [open in Calibre](calibre://view-book/GCcalibreBooks/127/EPUB?open_at=toc:14.%20Planets%20of%20Belief)), a non-social route. Pinker also describes an "affective tipping point" where counterevidence topples opinions (2018, ch. 21, PDF p. 437). These limit how far "rather than" can go; none denies belonging as a driver.
