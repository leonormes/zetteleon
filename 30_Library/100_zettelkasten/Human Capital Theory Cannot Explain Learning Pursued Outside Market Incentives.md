Human Capital Theory (Schultz, Becker) models education as capital expenditure: time and money are invested, and the return is increased labour productivity, higher earnings and macroeconomic growth. As a model of _institutional resource allocation_ it is powerful and largely correct.

As a general philosophy of education it has a specific failure. Any learning with no market return is, within the model, waste—and yet such learning is pervasive, historically stable and pursued hardest by people with the least prospect of monetising it. The model does not merely disapprove of this behaviour; it has no representation for it, which is a stronger and more interesting problem.

The two frameworks are not symmetrically opposed. Human Capital Theory is a well-specified model with a bounded domain; the atelic account is a normative claim about what education is _for_. The conflict is real but it is a conflict between a description and a prescription.

## Steelman

Extended human capital models absorb much of this by treating learning as consumption rather than investment, and revealed-preference arguments can price intrinsic enjoyment directly. The charge of "cannot explain" may be aimed at a deliberately narrow reading of the theory rather than at how economists actually deploy it.
