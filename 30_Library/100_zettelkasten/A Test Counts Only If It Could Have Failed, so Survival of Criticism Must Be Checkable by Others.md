## A Test Counts Only If It Could Have Failed, so Survival of Criticism Must Be Checkable by Others

Saying a belief has survived criticism is always someone's judgement, and a believer makes the same judgement about their own belief. What separates an exposed belief from a protected one is whether a test could have failed, and others can check that from outside. A belief that could not have lost has not survived anything.

### Scope & Conditions

This is a secondhand report from an AI chat: the severity of a test and conventionalist stratagems are attributed to Popper, and the table of markers below is the chat's own synthesis. The chat also records its limit: severity is itself a judgement, so the regress returns at a finer grain, though one others can inspect. It does not answer Salmon's question of why not failing should count in a belief's favour.

### Evidence

> "A test only counts if it could have failed. Popper called this the severity of a test. A belief that couldn’t have lost hasn’t survived anything; it was never in the fight."
> (AI chat transcript pasted by Leon, 2026-10-23, section "Is “survived criticism” just a judgement?")

> "For Popper, objectivity isn’t a property of one person’s mind. It lies in claims being testable by others."
> (same section)

> "Severity is also a judgement, so the regress returns at a finer grain. The difference is that it becomes a judgement others can inspect, not a private conviction."
> (same section, Uncertainty)

### Implications

- Four outside-visible markers separate the two kinds: what would refute the belief is stated in advance, standards are fixed before testing, the same standard is applied to rival views, and there is a record of actually revising it.
- Agreeing with a criticism while leaving the belief unchanged means the criticism never landed, so the belief was protected and not tested.
- The line is not between believers and unbelievers: any belief, including an atheist's, can be exposed or protected.
