## Minimum Viable Understanding (MVU)

Mastery is not determined by the volume of information one possesses, but by the high-fidelity alignment between one's internal mental models and external reality. A flawed mental model acts as a "cognitive ceiling"—a systemic limit on performance where the output is bounded by the inaccuracies of the underlying simulation.

---

## 1. The "Bug in the Model" Pattern

A "Bug in the Model" is a fundamental error in internal logic that remains hidden as long as the system operates within specific, narrow parameters.

- The Hidden Foundation: Like [[Newtonian Physics as an Effective Theory]], a flawed model can appear perfectly functional at human scales, only failing ("crashing") when applied to edge cases or complex systems.
- The Fragility of Incorrect Assumptions: If a mental model assumes "The network is always reliable," the developer will write fragile code. No amount of "Clean Code" syntax can fix a foundational model error.
- Predictive Failure: Mastery is the ability to accurately simulate future states. Flawed models lead to "surprises" in production or learning plateaus where the same mistakes are repeated because the root cause is invisible to the observer.

## 2. Cognitive Biases as Defense Mechanisms

Flawed models are rarely abandoned easily because they are protected by a battery of cognitive biases (see [[MOC - Cognitive Biases]]):

- Confirmation Bias: Actively seeking data that fits the model while ignoring contradictory "leaks."
- The Illusion of Explanatory Depth (IoED): Confusing the ability to name a concept with the ability to simulate its mechanics.
- The Illusion of Fluency: Mistaking the ease of _reading_ (recognition) for the ability to _execute_ (recall/synthesis).
- Cognitive Dissonance: The mental discomfort felt when a model is challenged by reality. To alleviate this, we often reject the new data rather than incur the "toil" of rebuilding the model.
- The Dunning-Kruger Effect: Lacking the expertise required to even recognize that the model is flawed.

## 3. Breaking the Ceiling (The Recovery Protocol)

To achieve mastery, one must move from Instrumentalism ("it works for now") to Realism ("I understand the fundamental mechanics").

1. Stress-Test the Model: Intentionally seek out edge cases. Ask, "Where does this break?" (See: [[Leaky Abstractions]]).
2. Deconstruct to First Principles: Deconstruct abstractions until you reach the "Atoms" of the system. Read the source code; don't just rely on the documentation.
3. Explicit Externalisation: Use "Write to Think" or "Rubber Ducking" to force the model into linear text. This exposes the "logic gaps" that silent thinking hides.
4. Intellectual Humility: Accepting that all models are wrong, but some are useful allows for the iterative refinement necessary for true mastery.
