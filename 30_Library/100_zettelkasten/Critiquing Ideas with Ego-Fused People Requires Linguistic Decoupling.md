## Critiquing Ideas with Ego-Fused People Requires Linguistic Decoupling

When someone's belief is fused to their identity, a critique stays off the person only if it is worded with the claim, not the person, as its subject: no "you" or "your", and the claim put back on the table each time it slips. For example, "this policy misses the economic data" keeps the claim in view, while "your reasoning doesn't make sense" points at the person. ^claim

### Scope & Conditions

Applies when the belief is identity-bearing, so that a personal framing would be received as an attack ([[A Critique of a Belief That Is Fused to Identity Is Received as an Attack on the Person]]). "Requires" means the personal wording triggers that reception, and impersonal wording is necessary but not sufficient: it does not help if the person has already decided the critique is aimed at them. It changes the wording and not the substance, and the critique of the claim stays as strong as before.

### Evidence

The claim is the author's own reasoning. It first appeared as wording from an AI conversation (Gemini, 2026-07-13) captured in a HEAD note no longer found under that name, so that origin is unverified. No source in the library tests whether impersonal wording lowers defensiveness.

### Implications

- State the problem about the claim, the data or the policy, and avoid "you" and "your" when naming it.
- When the discussion slips back to the person, restate the claim rather than defending yourself.
- The wording is one step of a longer approach, set out in [[How to Critique an Idea With Someone Whose Identity Is Fused to It]].

### Falsifier

- Evidence that impersonal wording produces as much defensiveness as personal wording with identity-fused beliefs, or that it is read as evasive and raises the defence.
