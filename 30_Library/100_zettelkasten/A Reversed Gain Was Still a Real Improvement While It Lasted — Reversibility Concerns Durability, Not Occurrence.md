## A Reversed Gain Was Still a Real Improvement While It Lasted—Reversibility Concerns Durability, Not Occurrence

Treating the possibility of reversal as evidence against progress having occurred is a logical overreach—a gain can be real even if it is later lost, because reversibility is a question about how long an improvement lasts, not about whether the improvement happened at all; a country that became freer for a period and then authoritarian again was still actually freer during that period.

### Scope & Conditions

Corrects a specific inferential error (reversibility → the gain wasn't real), not a claim that reversed gains don't matter—the relevant follow-up questions are how large the gain was, who benefited, how durable it was, and what caused its reversal.

### Evidence

> "Gray treats the possibility of reversal as evidence against progress. That inference is too strong. A gain can be real even if it is later lost; reversibility concerns durability, not whether the improvement occurred."

### Implications

- Citing a later reversal to retroactively deny an earlier gain ever happened is a distinct, checkable fallacy worth naming when it appears in political argument.
