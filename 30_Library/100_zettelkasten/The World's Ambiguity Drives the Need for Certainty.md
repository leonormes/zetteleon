## The World's Ambiguity Drives the Need for Certainty

The inherent complexity and ambiguity of the world create a state of uncertainty. This uncertainty is often psychologically uncomfortable (the avoidance mechanics are set out in [[SoT - Cognitive Ambiguity and Deep Thinking]]). The discomfort drives a powerful need to find clear, stable beliefs and a sense of "truth" to navigate reality.

### Scope & Conditions

- It is a general tendency and not a law. The three nearby constructs are distinct and a person can be high on one without the others: wanting a firm answer, finding unclear future outcomes hard to bear, and enjoying effortful thought ([[Need for Cognition, Need for Cognitive Closure, and Intolerance of Uncertainty Are Related but Distinct Constructs]]).
- The discomfort is not a fixed property of uncertainty. It grows when the world is hard to predict and being wrong seems costly ([[Predictive-Processing Accounts Frame Discomfort With Uncertainty as a Prediction-Error Problem]]).
- The pressure is social as well as private. Observers reward confidence and penalise open admissions of doubt, so the demand for certainty is partly set by other people (see the Kahneman passage under Evidence).
- It describes the motive for wanting certainty. It says nothing about whether the certainty reached is warranted.

### Evidence

> "How much one needs a faith in order to flourish, how much that is “firm” and that one does not wish to be shaken because one clings to it, that is a measure of the degree of one’s strength (or, to put the point more clearly, of one’s weakness)."
> (Friedrich Nietzsche, *The Gay Science*, §347 "Believers and their need to believe", Knopf Doubleday edition as held in the library; footnote markers omitted)

> "An unbiased appreciation of uncertainty is a cornerstone of rationality—but it is not what people and organizations want. Extreme uncertainty is paralyzing under dangerous circumstances, and the admission that one is merely guessing is especially unacceptable when the stakes are high."
> (Daniel Kahneman, *Thinking, Fast and Slow*, ch. 24 "The Engine of Capitalism", p. 314)

> "We get only one try at any given decision—one flip of the coin—and that puts great pressure on us to feel we have to be certain before acting, a certainty that necessarily will overlook the influences of hidden information and luck."
> (Annie Duke, *Thinking in Bets*, ch. 1, "“I’m not sure”: using uncertainty to our advantage")

### Implications

- Felt certainty is a relief and is not evidence that a belief is true. A belief can feel settled because the discomfort has gone and not because the case for it has improved. [[Confirmation as a Perceived Shortcut to Truth]] describes the same relief arriving from outside validation.
- A belief held for comfort is then protected. Once a belief is in place the mind guards it against contrary evidence, which is why this need sits upstream of the problems in [[Human Cognition Evolved for Social Survival, Not for Tracking Objective Truth]].
- The need is partly an economy of effort, not only a weakness. Beliefs work as prediction shortcuts because conscious processing is limited ([[The Brain Uses Beliefs as Prediction Shortcuts Because Conscious Processing Is Limited]]).
- What reduces the need is an available response and a habit of grading confidence, not certainty itself ([[A Sense of Control Is Belief in an Available Response, Not a Guarantee Against Bad Outcomes]]).

### Further Reading

- [The Gay Science, Friedrich Nietzsche, §347](calibre://view-book/GCcalibreBooks/1678/EPUB)—*corroborates and goes further: reads the need for something "firm" as a measure of weakness and names the "impetuous demand for certainty" that now takes a scientific-positivistic form. The evaluative reading is Nietzsche's and this note does not adopt it.*
- [Thinking, Fast and Slow, Daniel Kahneman, ch. 24, p. 314](calibre://view-book/GCcalibreBooks/53/EPUB)—*corroborates the social side: clients prefer confident experts, so "acting on pretended knowledge is often the preferred solution".*
- [Thinking in Bets, Annie Duke, ch. 1](calibre://view-book/GCcalibreBooks/55/EPUB)—*corroborates the pressure to be certain before acting, and offers the counter-habit of getting comfortable with "I'm not sure".*
- [The Big Picture, Sean Carroll, ch. 14 "Planets of Belief"](calibre://view-book/GCcalibreBooks/127/EPUB)—*extends: our preference for preserving existing beliefs, with confirmation bias and the backfire effect; follows from the need for settled belief.*
- [Black Box Thinking, Matthew Syed, ch. 5 "Intellectual Contortions"](calibre://view-book/GCcalibreBooks/1490/EPUB)—*extends: cognitive dissonance as the way settled beliefs are defended against inconvenient evidence.*
