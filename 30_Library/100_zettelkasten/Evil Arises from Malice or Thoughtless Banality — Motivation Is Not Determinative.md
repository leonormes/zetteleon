## Evil Arises from Malice or Thoughtless Banality—Motivation Is Not Determinative

Evil can arise from two structurally distinct motivational sources: deliberate malicious intent (the Kantian "perverse will"—choosing wrong for its own sake) or "thoughtlessness"—the bureaucratic banality in which immense harm is produced by a perpetrator entirely absorbed in executing a role without engaging moral reflection. Hannah Arendt's analysis of Eichmann demonstrated the second pathway. Both are sufficient for evil; neither is required.

### Scope & Conditions

References the Arendtian concept of the "banality of evil" as applied to bureaucratic, systemic, or institutional perpetrators. The key variable is not the psychological state of the perpetrator but whether the harm produced was reasonably foreseeable. Malicious intent is a sufficient condition, not a necessary one.

### Evidence

> "Hannah Arendt's analysis of Adolf Eichmann revealed that profound evil can arise from 'thoughtlessness' and bureaucratic banality rather than monstrous motivation."

### Implications

- Diagnosing evil by searching for monsters misses the far more common pathway—institutional or systemic processes in which individuals participate without engaging moral reflection.
- Foreseeable harm magnitude is the more reliable criterion than perpetrator psychology; the question "could this person have known?" matters more than "did they intend this?".
