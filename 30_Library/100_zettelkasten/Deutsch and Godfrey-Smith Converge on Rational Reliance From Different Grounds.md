## Deutsch and Godfrey-Smith Converge on Rational Reliance From Different Grounds

Interestingly, Deutsch explicitly addresses a dilemma identical to Godfrey-Smith's bridge example, using a scenario where a person considers jumping off a tower. Deutsch argues that we are justified in relying on the prevailing theory of gravity not because past observations "confirmed" it, but because it is the only rationally tenable theory remaining that provides a good explanation, whereas alternative theories provide bad or unexplained assertions.

### Scope & Conditions

Directly ties Deutsch's tower-jumping example to Godfrey-Smith's bridge-building example as two answers to the same underlying question: why is it rational to act on a well-tested theory?

### Evidence

> "Deutsch argues that we are justified in relying on the prevailing theory of gravity not because past observations 'confirmed' it, but because it is the only rationally tenable theory remaining that provides a good explanation, whereas alternative theories provide bad or unexplained assertions"

### Implications

- The two thinkers reach practically compatible conclusions (rational reliance on tested theories) via incompatible justifications: best-remaining-explanation (Deutsch) versus procedural confirmation (Godfrey-Smith).
- This is a genuine, unadjudicated tension, not a contradiction: both survive once you drop the shared assumption that rational reliance requires positive confirmation.

### Related

- [[A Good Explanation Is Hard to Vary]]—Deutsch's side of this tension rests entirely on this criterion: a theory is trustworthy because rival explanations are bad or unexplained, not because it was confirmed.
