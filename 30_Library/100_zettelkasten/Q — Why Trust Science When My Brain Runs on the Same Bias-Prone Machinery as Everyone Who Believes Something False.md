> [!question] Central question
> Why do I trust that science is a valid way of learning the truth about reality, when I don't personally do the experiments or verify the evidence, and I'm using the same fallible cognitive machinery as people who are just as confident and just as wrong?

This is not a question about whether science is right on any particular finding. It is about what, if anything, justifies preferring it over a rival belief system, given that the feeling of being right is not itself evidence of being right.

## The Resolution, in Two Parts

1. [[Science Is Trustworthy Because Its Structure Corrects for Bias, Not Because Scientists Are Less Biased]]—not because I or any scientist reasons more objectively than a person who rejects science, but because the method is structurally falsifiable, evidence-based, and never treated as final, which lets a community of equally biased people still self-correct.
2. [[Preferring Science Is Itself a Falsifiable, Evidence-Based Belief, Not a Dogmatic One]]—this preference is not exempted from the same standard. I could change my mind, given evidence that the structural properties in part 1 had stopped holding.

## Why the Question Arises at All

- [[Human Cognition Evolved for Social Survival, Not for Tracking Objective Truth]]—the premise behind the question: if cognition were built for objective accuracy, this puzzle would not arise, because everyone's confidence would track reality equally well. It doesn't, so confidence alone cannot be the answer.
- [[Trusting a Scientific Authority Feels Psychologically Identical to Trusting a False One]]—the sharpest version of the puzzle: trusting a physicist because their explanation "makes sense" feels, from the inside, exactly like a flat-earther trusting a false authority for the same reason.
- [[Falsifiability Distinguishes Science from Dogma]]—the property that ultimately breaks the symmetry between the two cases, once the answer moves from "who feels more certain" to "which structure is built to catch its own errors".
