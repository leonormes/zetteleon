## Whether Anger Can Be Virtuous Is a Genuinely Contested Philosophical Split, Not Settled Science

One tradition (Aristotle's "good temper," Plato's thumos, modern philosophers like Martha Nussbaum's "transition anger") holds that anger can be a fitting, proportionate, politically useful response to genuine injustice when strictly governed by reason and stripped of a desire for petty revenge; Stoic and Buddhist traditions counter that anger inherently corrupts reason, making any attempt at "measured" anger dangerous and prone to escalation. Neither side has a decisive empirical answer.

### Scope & Conditions

Presented as a live philosophical disagreement, not a resolved question—treating either side as simply correct overstates the state of the debate.

### Evidence

> "Anger can be a fitting, proportionate, and politically useful response to genuine injustice, provided it is strictly governed by reason and stripped of the desire for petty revenge… Stoics and Buddhists counter this by arguing that anger inherently corrupts reason, making any attempt to wield 'measured' anger highly dangerous and prone to escalation."

### Implications

- Citing "righteous anger" as an unqualified virtue, or dismissing all anger as always corrupting, both skip past a genuine, unresolved philosophical dispute.
