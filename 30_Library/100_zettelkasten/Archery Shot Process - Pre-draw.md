## Archery Shot Process - Pre-draw

Pre-draw acts as a checklist step to verify posture, front shoulder position, and shoulder alignment by rotating through the core.

### The Steps

1. Torso Rotation (Core Alignment & Thoracic Rotation) & "Front Alignment First": Prioritize getting a straight, solid connection from your draw elbow, through your draw hand, and straight into your bow hand _before_ aligning your shoulders to the target. Establish this distance in front of you, and _then_ rotate your upper body/chest toward the target to bring that alignment into your body's center (without twisting the hips/feet). True shoulder alignment must be achieved strictly by rotating the thoracic spine around a vertical axis, never by twisting the hips or displacing the upright T-posture forward. In an angular draw kinematic, this core rotation brings the bow from an open setup angle into a "half-closed" position relative to the target line. Attempting to perfectly align to the target first and then squeezing your arms into position causes severe tension. Imagine your core is a towel being twisted tightly—this coiling action locks the body in place and creates immense stability.
   - *No-Pull Alignment:* Rotating the trunk brings the drawing hand naturally closer to the face without any active arm-pulling. Do not begin actively pulling the string with arm muscles during this alignment phase; let the rotational movement alone shorten the distance.
   - _Purpose:_ Rotating the thoracic spine uses the large, strong trunk muscles to bring the shoulders into line, instead of squeezing the arms into place, which causes severe tension. The hips stay locked so the core remains a stable platform.
2. The "Barrel of the Gun": Strive for perfect skeletal alignment before the draw begins. The center of your bow wrist, bow shoulder, and draw shoulder must form a perfectly straight line, mimicking a long rifle barrel. Stacking these bones minimizes the muscular effort needed to hold heavy draw weights.
   - _Purpose:_ Stacked bones carry the load through the skeleton, so less muscle effort is needed to hold the bow.
3. Draw Hand and Arm Placement: Set the angle between your upper draw arm and shoulders at roughly 90 degrees (slightly further back is fine; avoid placing it too far forward to prevent impingement). Your draw hand wrist should end up roughly in line with the front of your chin. Most importantly, ensure your draw elbow is positioned on or slightly _behind_ the arrow line.
   - _Purpose:_ Keeping the upper arm near 90 degrees and not too far forward avoids shoulder impingement. An elbow on or behind the arrow line leaves room to swing it round with the back rather than pulling with the arm (see Short Pre-Draw under Faults). The elbow-position reason is partly inferred.
4. Visual & Tension Setup (Pre-Aiming): Apply a slight amount of tension to the string. Do not aim yet; keep the arrow and stabilizer offset (pointing safely to the left of the target face for a right-handed archer). Establish a quick visual reference without aiming through the sight.
   - _Purpose:_ A quick visual reference means you don't have to fight for the sight picture during the draw.
5. The Final Setup Check: Pause briefly after rotating to run a rapid mental checklist before pulling back: Did the hook and grip change? Are you still squeezing the finger spacer? Is the bow arm tension good? Did you reach toward the target? Is the "barrel of the gun" aligned?
   - _Purpose:_ Pre-draw sets about 85% of the shot's structural alignment, so this is the last cheap chance to catch a changed hook, grip or tension before loading.
6. Verification: Verify that the posture is tall, the front shoulder remains set down (check for the "acromion divot" on top of the shoulder to confirm it is properly depressed and the deltoid is engaged), correct shoulder-line alignment is established, and the head is set in its final, immovable position before loading.
   - _Purpose:_ A locked front shoulder is one of the quickest ways to stabilise draw length and anchor point. A collapse at full draw gives erratic draw length and a floating anchor.

### Why It Works

- Pre-draw sets up the majority (85%) of the shot's overall structural alignment.
- Rotating the core engages large, strong trunk muscles to draw the bow efficiently.
- Establishing a quick visual reference prevents you from having to fight for the sight picture during the draw.
- Getting the front shoulder position locked in is one of the quickest ways to stabilise variables like draw length and anchor point.

### Faults & Diagnostics

- Bicep Tendon Impingement (Linear Draw Penalty): Forcing a strict linear draw requires over-reaching forward to hold the bow continuously on target, creating an overly acute angle at the front shoulder joint. This significantly elevates the risk of impingement and inflammation of the long head of the biceps tendon, particularly for broader-shouldered archers.
- The "Straight Push-Pull" Illusion: Trying to push the bow hand perfectly straight to the target while pulling the draw hand straight back to the face causes the bow hand to come too close to the body, kinking the wrist. Instead, feel an outward expansion—directing the left thumb slightly to the left, and the draw hand slightly to the right (away from the body). This internal feeling of "opening the bow" aligns the shoulders, even if the physical movement is still visually straight.
- Forced Shoulder Alignment: Attempting to get into line by simply pushing your bow shoulder forward without rotating your upper body. This causes the shoulder to roll over, creating a highly unstable joint position without actually improving skeletal alignment. True alignment comes from rotating the entire torso and upper thoracic spine.
- Pelvic Twisting vs. Thoracic Rotation: Twisting the hips or displacing the T-posture forward in an attempt to align the shoulders compromises foundational core stability. True shoulder alignment is created strictly by rotating the thoracic spine around the central vertical axis of the body while hips remain locked parallel to the feet.
- Biomechanical Warning: Drawing purely with upper-body strength or dropping into alignment from a high raise strains the shoulder extensors, leading to early fatigue and reduced long-term consistency.
- The Setup Trap (Allowing the Bow to Pull You Forward): During this pre-draw phase, there is a natural slowing down of movement. Do not let the mechanical weight and tension of the bow pull your drawing shoulder or elbow forward. This puts the shoulder in an overly extended, loaded position, creating unnecessary pressure on the joint. Keep the draw shoulder feeling "neutral," free, and easy to move (like your arms resting by your sides). Do not forcefully yank the shoulder down either.
- Short Pre-Draw (Hand Too Close): If you start your draw with your draw hand squeezed too close to your bow hand/shoulder, it severely compresses the shoulder joint. This closed angle makes it incredibly inefficient to draw the string back, forcing you to use arm muscles rather than properly swinging the elbow around with your back.
- Shoulder Collapse at Full Draw: A front shoulder collapse occurs when the bow-side shoulder yields or gives backward while you are holding at full draw. This contributes to erratic draw length and a floating anchor point.
