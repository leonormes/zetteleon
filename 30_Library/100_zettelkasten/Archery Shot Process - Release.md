## Archery Shot Process - Release

Summary:

Release is the act of letting go of the string, which should be an involuntary, natural reaction to the clicker when the preceding steps are executed correctly.

Details:

- Passive Relaxation vs. Active Opening: A clean release occurs when you relax the hand's flexor muscles, allowing the bowstring to push the fingers out of the way, rather than actively using your extensor muscles to "claw" or force the hand open. Slowly relax your fingers while continuously pulling backward with your shoulder tension.
- **The Danger of Tension:** If there is too much tension in the hand, the string cannot push cleanly through the fingers and must go *around* them, causing side-to-side oscillation and inconsistent arrow flight.
- Involuntary Action: Treat the release as a reaction to the clicker rather than a conscious opening of the fingers.
- The Tension Drop Mindset (The 40% Rule):
  - Avoid thinking of the release as going from 100% hold to 0% by actively opening the fingers. Consciously opening your fingers causes a wide, "flappy" release where the hand flies outwards away from the face.
  - Frame the release as reducing finger tension down to around 40%. The draw weight of the bow will naturally force the string through your fingers, after which the fingertips instantly curl back into a relaxed state as your hand travels rearward.
- Letting the String Cut Through: Do not consciously try to open or straighten your fingers during the release. High-speed video shows human fingers cannot open fast enough to clear a moving string; attempting to force them open sends unnecessary nerve signals from your brain that disrupt your directional line of tension. Keep your fingers relaxed and allow the string to push or cut past them.
- **Preserve Shape in the Follow-Through:** Many archers actively force their fingers straight open upon release, introducing new inconsistencies. Instead, practice releasing by relaxing the hand while keeping the fingers curled in their original hook shape during the follow-through. The hand should finish looking the same as it did on the string, without splaying fingers outward. Verify with a mirror or delayed video app.
- Squeezing the Finger Spacer: Maintain continuous pressure against the tab's finger spacer with your fingers throughout the entire release and follow-through cycle to prevent your tab from shifting.
- Cut Rope Visualization: Imagine a taut string tied to the draw elbow, pulling it straight back and wrapping around behind your head like a rope.
- The Two-Sided Release:
  - A release is a two-sided action. An outward or collapsed release on the draw side is almost always mirrored by a bow-arm drop or sight twitch on the bow side.
  - Maintain active front-side structure and push through the release just as hard as the draw-side elbow continues pulling back.
  - *Mental Cue:* Imagine your draw elbow exploding *"70 metres away from the target"* while your sight pin drives *"70 metres straight into the target"*.
- **The Pull-Up Bar Analogy:** Imagine hanging from a pull-up bar by just your fingers. You wouldn't bend your wrists at extreme angles — you rely on your natural skeletal structure to hang. Apply this same natural, relaxed structural approach to holding the bowstring, removing tension from the arms.
- **Release as the Beginning of the Shot (Sprinter Analogy):** Do not treat the release as the final event of the shot cycle. If the mind relaxes the instant the clicker drops, back tension collapses and the hand flies outward. Instead, mentally frame the release as the *beginning* of the shot — like a sprinter running *through* the finish line rather than stopping on it. Pull strongly through execution to achieve a well-directed follow-through.
- Poundage & Tension:
  - Ensure bow poundage is manageable. Over-bowing forces a clenched hook ("string grabbing") to prevent the string from slipping, making a clean, relaxed release mechanically impossible.
  - Relaxation is relative to grip strength; drop bow weight if hand tension remains high.
  - Kim Woo-jin's advice: Master expansion and release movements with a lightweight setup first before gradually adding weight.
- Release Flaws to Avoid:
  - *Static Release:* Avoid trying to freeze your hand at anchor upon release, as this alters bow-arm tension timing and causes inconsistent target impact under pressure.
  - *The Pluck:* The draw hand pulls outward away from the face upon release, like waving goodbye to the arrow. Caused by excess tension in the hand, forearm, and biceps.
  - *The Collapse:* The draw hand moves forward toward the target upon release. Caused by stopping the pulling motion right before the shot, or by poor skeletal alignment (shoulders not pointed straight, elbow out of line with the arrow).
  - *Premature Shot Ending:* A shot cycle does not end when the clicker drops or when the string leaves your fingers; it ends only when you hit a full static finish.
  - *"Jumpy" Release (Fatigue & Clicker Struggle):* A jumpy release occurs when an archer lacks muscular endurance and is fatigued, struggling to pull smoothly through the clicker. This causes sudden, jolting movements that throw the bow off target upon release. The release must remain explosive yet completely balanced across the body.
- The Ultimate Purpose of Technical Foundations: While top archers display widely diverse draw styles, stances, shoulder alignments, and release executions, the three universal technical foundations—postural stability, consistent full-draw anchor, and smooth expansion—exist to serve a single ultimate purpose: creating a consistent, repeatable release and execution on every single shot.

### Elite Variant — Aggressive Punchy Execution

- **The Smoothness Trap:** Trying to force a perfectly smooth, effortless-looking release like Kim Woo-jin's can inadvertently lead to soft execution and forward collapses for intermediate archers who lack his volume of practice.
- **Emulate Aggressive Power:** Intermediate archers often benefit from emulating the high-intensity execution seen in elite archers like Yuni Jin. She anchors forcefully against her face and uses an aggressive, powerful drive from the elbow to "break through" the string upon release.
- A punchy, assertive expansion acts as a shield against forward creeping and inconsistent arrow groups. Favour a dynamic, firm, aggressive release with strong elbow continuation (also covering An San or Yun Mi-jin) pulling firmly into the face, rather than attempting an ultra-soft execution.

### Training Drills

#### Two-Handed Finger Slip-Off Drill

- Purpose: Isolates clean finger relaxation without needing to manage bow weight.
- How to do it:
  1. Hook the three fingers of your draw hand directly into the fingers of your opposite hand (or a training partner's hand) in front of your chest.
  2. Create tension by pulling both elbows apart using your back muscles.
  3. Maintain draw-elbow structure and relax only the fingertips of your draw hand, allowing them to slip cleanly away.
- **Variation — Finger-Slip Sensitivity:** Clasp the hooking fingers of both hands together, pull outward from both elbows to simulate full draw, and practice relaxing only the tips of one hand to let them slide free while the rest of the draw hand remains structurally quiet.

#### The Carrier Bag Drill

- Purpose: Trains finger relaxation without splaying or opening the hand.
- How to do it:
  1. Hold a small weighted bag by the handles at your side with your drawing fingers hooked.
  2. Slowly drop your finger pressure (towards the 40% tension mark) until the handles slip out naturally.
  3. Keep your hand structured and relaxed; do not splay your fingers open as the bag drops.

#### Progressive Relaxation Drill

- Purpose: Builds the kinesthetic feeling of passive hand relaxation during release using a lightweight beginner bow close to a target face (2–3 meters).
- How to do it:
  1. Low Draw (Stomach Level): Draw the string low to your midsection, watch your draw hand, minimize hand tension, and relax the hand so the string pulls past your fingers.
  2. Half Draw (Chest Level): Draw with a bent bow arm to chest level. Focus entirely on the internal physical sensation of relaxing your hand rather than watching it.
  3. Full Draw: Step into your standard full-draw posture and practice the same passive hand relaxation.

#### Thumb & Pinky Wiggle

- Purpose: Detects tension buildup in the hand during the draw cycle. Keeps hand tension consistent from setup through release.
- How to do it:
  1. Draw your bow to full anchor normally.
  2. Continuously move your thumb and little finger while at full draw.
  3. If your hand accidentally clenches or bends to grip the string harder, the thumb and pinky will lock up and stop moving. This gives immediate physical feedback that tension has crept in.

#### Shot Trainer Drill

- Purpose: Teaches a relaxed, passive hook by temporarily removing the fingers from the string entirely.
- How to do it:
  1. Use an Astra Archery Shot Trainer (or Formaster) strapped directly to your draw elbow.
  2. Draw the bow using only the elbow and back muscles — no finger connection to the string.
  3. Once the feeling of back-muscle-only drawing is learned, place the fingers back on the string as a passive hook without re-engaging the wrist and forearm.

#### The Neck Scratch (Empty Hand Drill)

- Purpose: Trains a tight, inline release path behind the ear without bow weight.
- How to do it:
  1. Draw your empty hand backward, keeping fingertips lightly skimming along the jawline and neck throughout execution.
  2. Train a tight, inline path straight backward behind the ear, bypassing the risk of using the bicep to snap the fingers.
