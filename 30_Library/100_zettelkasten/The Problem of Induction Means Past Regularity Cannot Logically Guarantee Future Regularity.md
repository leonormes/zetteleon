## The Problem of Induction Means Past Regularity Cannot Logically Guarantee Future Regularity

Science relies heavily on induction: generalising from a set of observed cases to a rule that should hold for unobserved cases. This move powers most of empirical science, but no number of confirming instances can logically prove the generalisation will keep holding—the sun rising every day so far does not logically entail it will rise tomorrow. This is a standing limit on how much certainty induction alone can deliver, distinct from any particular failure of method.

### Scope & Conditions

Applies to any inference from observed instances to a general rule or to unobserved instances—this is a logical limit, not a practical one that better instruments or bigger samples remove.

### Implications

- Confidence built purely on repeated observation is provisional by nature, not a gap to be closed with more of the same evidence.
- This is part of why falsifiability and risky prediction matter: they test a generalisation against cases it could fail, rather than accumulating more cases it could merely confirm.
