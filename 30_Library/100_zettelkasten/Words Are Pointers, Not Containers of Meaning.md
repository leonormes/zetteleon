## Words Are Pointers, Not Containers of Meaning

Words point towards meaning held in the lived experience of each person rather than containing meaning inside themselves. Because a word carries no intrinsic semantic payload, communication only succeeds to the extent that speaker and listener share overlapping experiential referents, context, and inferential habits.

### Scope & Conditions

- Applies whenever natural language is used to coordinate action, explain ideas, or resolve interpersonal disagreement.
- Dictionary definitions stabilise shared conventions, not personal connotations or embodied understanding; two people can agree on a definition and still point to incompatible lived realities.

### Evidence

> "Words are pointers, not containers of meaning. Their effectiveness relies on a complex interplay of factors that go far beyond the dictionary definition."

Source: [[beyond words]] (line 181).

### Implications

- **Reconstruction over transfer**: Listening is an act of rebuilding meaning from local memory and schemas, not unpacking a parcel sent by the speaker ([[Knowledge Transmission Is Reconstruction, Not Copying]]).
- **Why repeating yourself fails**: When a pointer misses its target, saying the same words louder or more logically does not create the missing referent ([[The Information Deficit Model is a Fallacy]]).
- **Design for shared referents**: Effective communication requires checking what the listener's pointer actually resolves to—via examples, shared artefacts, and environment design rather than bare assertions.
