## Affective Polarization

Affective polarization is the tendency of partisans to feel escalating negative emotion toward opposing partisans and positive emotion toward co-partisans, measured independently of how far apart the two sides actually stand on policy.

### Scope & Conditions

Distinct from ideological polarization, which measures policy-position distance. Documented via implicit association tests and behavioural economic games; found in some studies to now exceed other forms of entrenched social prejudice (race, ethnicity, religion).

### Evidence

> "The escalating tendency of partisans to view opposing partisans negatively and co-partisans positively, irrespective of actual policy disagreements."

### Implications

- Two people with nearly identical policy views can still be maximally polarized against each other's party affiliation.
- Reducing policy disagreement does not automatically reduce affective polarization.

### Related

- [[Claim - Beliefs often function as belonging cues to secure group membership rather than as truth-seeking theses]]—extends: affective polarization is the emotional register of the same tribal-signalling dynamic that note describes for individual belief adoption.

### Library Sources

- _Enlightenment Now: The Case for Reason, Science, Humanism, and Progress_ (Steven Pinker), Ch. 21—draws the direct analogy: "political partisanship is like sports fandom: testosterone levels rise or fall on election night just as they do on Super Bowl Sunday," and notes partisans watching the same footage each see more infractions by the opposing side—an in-group/out-group perception split independent of the actual facts.
- _Think Again_ (Adam Grant), Ch. 1 "So Close and Yet So Far"—distinguishes harmless preference-based polarization (his stinky-tofu example) from the identity-driven kind that generates real conflict, i.e. the boundary between ideological and affective polarization this atom draws.
