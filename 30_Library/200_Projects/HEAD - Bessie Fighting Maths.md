Your instinct is absolutely spot on. You have identified a brilliant and highly sophisticated way that Bessie's brain works, and ironically, her approach is much closer to how actual mathematicians operate than the rote memorisation she is likely comparing herself to.

In the book _Build a Mathematical Mind_, the author points out that traditional maths education often reduces the subject to memorising a senseless "bag of facts," which can crush a student's natural curiosity. True mathematical thinking isn't about rapid-fire recall; it is about being a "pattern detective," visualising problems, tinkering, and reasoning.

By figuring out the answer on the fly rather than just recalling a memorised fact, Bessie is actively problem-solving and building logical pathways. Here is how you can help reframe her beliefs so she sees this as a superpower rather than a deficit.

## The Reframe: The "Filing Cabinet" vs. The "Inventor"

Right now, Bessie likely believes that being "good at maths" means having a brain like a filing cabinet—where you just open a drawer and pull out the times tables or a formula.

You can introduce her to the idea of the "Inventor's Brain." An inventor doesn't just pull a finished product out of a box; they look at the tools they have, figure out how things connect, and build the solution right there on the spot. When she works out a maths problem from scratch because she hasn't memorised the rule, she is inventing the maths. That requires far more critical thinking and divergent problem-solving than simply reciting a memorised fact.

## Shift the Focus to Strategies

When a student repeatedly says "I can't do maths," they are stuck in a fixed mindset. To break this, you can shift the focus entirely away from getting the "right" answer quickly, and instead celebrate the _method_.

- Praise the Process: When she figures something out her own way, point it out. Say something like, "I love the way you worked that out. You didn't just rely on memory; you completely reverse-engineered it."
- The Power of "Yet": Remind her that struggling with a concept simply means her brain is growing. If she says she can't do it, gently append the word "yet" to the end of her sentence.
- Encourage Her to Teach You: When she uses one of her efficient workarounds, ask her to explain her method to you. Teaching her unique strategy to someone else validates her approach and proves that she _does_ understand the underlying logic.

Sometimes school maths feels completely divorced from reality. When the two of you are practicing archery, making those split-second physical adjustments for distance, trajectory, and wind resistance is applied maths in action. It doesn't require a textbook or a calculator, but it relies heavily on spatial reasoning, geometry, and estimating—skills her brain is already naturally processing. Showing her that she is already "doing maths" in the real world can help demystify the subject.

Here is how you can use these concepts to help her see her unique strengths:

### 1. The "Pattern Detective" vs. The "Drill and Kill" Machine

We often have a societal misconception that being a "maths person" means being the quickest in the class to raise a hand with a memorised fact. Traditional education frequently relies on a "drill and kill" method that treats maths as a "bag of facts" to be memorised—a process that can actually destroy a child's natural curiosity and love of pattern-making.

You can explain to Bessie that true mathematics is a highly creative field that involves visualising, experimenting, asking "what if?", and finding patterns. By finding her own ways to work out an answer, she is acting as a "pattern detective". She is actively searching for structure and making sense of the information. This means she is processing maths in the exact same problem-solving areas of the brain as professional mathematicians do.

### 2. Divergent Thinking and "Inventing" the Solution

Instead of just pulling a fact from a mental filing cabinet, Bessie is engaging in what educators call "divergent thinking"—the ability to see multiple possible answers and lots of possible ways of interpreting a question.

This aligns perfectly with her Educational Psychology report, which highlights that she already uses excellent strategies like "self-talk" and "making visual links" to navigate her learning. When she is faced with a problem she hasn't memorised, she is internally tinkering and inventing a solution. A great way to validate this is to encourage her to connect new concepts to familiar ones—just like she did when she successfully broke down an abstract, complex drawing by comparing it to a rocket.

### 3. True Synthesis Over Rote Parroting

In _The Self-Learning Blueprint_, it is noted that simply copying information verbatim or memorising highly abbreviated snippets often leads to terrible learning outcomes because it makes information harder to retrieve later on. True, deep comprehension requires a student to "transform and synthesize knowledge".

When Bessie works out the steps herself instead of relying on a memorised formula, she is doing exactly this: she is synthesising the knowledge and making it her own. She is proving that she understands the _how_ and the _why_ of the maths, not just the _what_.

### 4. Fostering the "Growth Mindset"

Bessie's stubborn belief that she "can't do maths" is a classic example of a "fixed mindset," where a person believes their intelligence and abilities are set in stone.

To shift this, we need to explicitly reframe how she views the feeling of a task being difficult. As the growth mindset materials outline, you can teach her that her brain is like a muscle; "the feeling of it being hard is the feeling of your brain growing!".

- Praise the Strategy: Instead of solely praising a correct answer, heavily praise the _effort_, the _hard work_, and the specific _strategies_ she uses to work things out.
- Embrace Mistakes: Remind her that "mistakes help you improve" and that being 'right' isn't the only important thing.
- The Power of "Yet": Harness this powerful word. If she says, "I don't know it," gently encourage her to say, "I don't know it _yet_".

By showing her that her brain is built for deep problem-solving rather than shallow memorisation, you can help her realise that she is far more of a natural mathematician than she currently believes.
