title: Foundation (basic conversion and standard arithmetic
type: note
---

Start by setting aside the formal GCSE terminology and exam papers for the first session. When a student has missed schooling and feels negative about maths, jumping straight into abstract index notation can trigger anxiety.

Instead, tap into what educators call "tinkering" and "pattern sniffing"—letting her play with numbers she already knows to discover the underlying structure.

Step 1: The "Money & Friendly Numbers" Hook Connect standard form to friendly numbers (multiples of 10 and 100) and money, where she already has intuition.

- Ask her: _"If you have four £10 notes, how much is that?"_ ($4 \times 10 = 40$)
- _"What about four £100 notes?"_ ($4 \times 100 = 400$)
- Lay out three calculations:
    - $5 \times 10 = 50$
    - $5 \times 100 = 500$
    - $5 \times 1{,}000 = 5{,}000$
- The confidence win: Ask her what $5 \times 10{,}000$ would be. When she gets $50{,}000$, celebrate that she already understands how powers of 10 scale numbers up.

Step 2: Play the "Shortcut" Game (Powers of 10)

Show her that mathematicians are actually quite lazy—they look for shorter ways to write things down so they do less work:

- Remind her (or introduce gently) that:
    - $10 = 10^1$
    - $10 \times 10 = 100 = 10^2$ (2 tens multiplied together)
    - $10 \times 10 \times 10 = 1{,}000 = 10^3$ (3 tens multiplied together)
- Let her notice the pattern: the small power simply tells you how many times 10 has been multiplied in.
- Revisit Step 1 using the shorthand:
    - $500 = 5 \times 10^2$
    - $5{,}000 = 5 \times 10^3$
- Give her a couple of quick, low-stakes wins: _"How would we write $7{,}000$ using our 10 power shorthand?"_ ($7 \times 10^3$).

Step 3: Introduce the "One Rule Club" ($1 \le A < 10$) Now introduce the only official rule of standard form without overwhelming her with formal algebra:

> _"To be in the Standard Form Club, the front number must look like a normal single-digit number—between 1 and 9.99…"_

- Give her a fun sorting activity: "In the Club" or "Not in the Club"?
    - $3.2 \times 10^4 \rightarrow$ In (3.2 is between 1 and 10)
    - $45 \times 10^2 \rightarrow$ Not in (45 is too big)
    - $0.8 \times 10^5 \rightarrow$ Not in (0.8 is too small)
- This builds early mastery through simple recognition before she has to create the expressions herself.

Step 4: Use the Calculator as a Safety Net On Foundation Paper 2 and Paper 3, scientific calculators are allowed. Showing her how the machine handles standard form removes the fear of getting stuck during arithmetic:

- Type $864{,}000$ into the calculator and press `=`.
- Show her the $\times 10^x$ button (or `EXP` button) at the bottom.
- Show her how pressing the `ENG` or `S<=>D` key changes numbers into powers of 10.
- Knowing the calculator can verify her answers takes the pressure off her memory and turns it into a tool she can control.

Keep First Sessions Short Aim for 15-minute bursts focused purely on positive whole numbers (like turning $400$ or $6{,}000$ into standard form). Hold off completely on decimals and negative powers until positive numbers feel like second nature.
