## Resistance Band Routine for Recurve Archery

For a late starter (52) who wants to train away from the bow and tune draw biomechanics.

### Kit

- Light and medium loop bands (or a long therapy band, e.g. Theraband red/green)
- An archery-specific "form strip" (e.g. an Elastic Formaster/AER band) if you have one; otherwise a light band held at full draw length works
- A door anchor or a sturdy post at chest height
- A mirror or phone camera (side-on and from behind) for checking form

### How Often

- Daily (10 min): Warm-up + SPT form drill—use on any non-shooting day and before every shooting session
- 3x per week (25–30 min): Full routine A + B
- Rest: At least one full day off a week. At 52, tendons adapt more slowly than muscle—progress load every 2–3 weeks, not every session.

### Core Principles

1. Back tension, not arm pull. The draw should come from the rhomboids and lower trapezius pulling the draw-side shoulder blade towards the spine. The hand and forearm are just hooks.
2. Shoulders down. Every rep starts by setting the shoulders low and away from the ears. Shrugging is the commonest cause of shoulder pain in archers.
3. Alignment. At full draw, the draw elbow should be roughly in line with the arrow (or slightly behind it when viewed from above).
4. Slow and controlled. 2 seconds out, 2–3 second hold, 3 seconds back. Never let the band snap back.
5. Quality over load. If you can't hold perfect form, use a lighter band.

### Warm-Up (5 Min)

| Exercise | Reps | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Arm circles (small to large) | 10 each way | Loosen the shoulder joint |
| Band pull-aparts | 15 | Arms straight at shoulder height; squeeze shoulder blades |
| Shoulder dislocates (wide grip, light band) | 10 | Over the head and behind; widen grip if it pinches |
| Thoracic rotations | 8 each side | Seated or kneeling; rotate through the upper back |
| Scapular wall slides | 10 | Back flat against the wall; slide arms up keeping contact |

### Routine A—Shoulder Health and Stability

The foundation. Protects the rotator cuff, which takes the most load in archery.

| Exercise | Sets x Reps | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| External rotation (elbow at side, towel under arm) | 2 x 15 each arm | Rotator cuff; keep the elbow tucked |
| Internal rotation | 2 x 15 each arm | Balance with external rotation |
| External rotation at 90° (elbow at shoulder height, "goalpost") | 2 x 12 each arm | Mimics the draw-arm position |
| Face pulls (anchor at head height) | 3 x 12 | Pull to the face, elbows high, rotate hands back |
| Y-T-W raises (light band) | 2 x 8 each letter | Lower trap and mid-back endurance |
| Serratus punches | 2 x 12 each arm | Protract the shoulder blade; key for a stable bow shoulder |

### Routine B—Archery-Specific Strength

| Exercise | Sets x Reps | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| Bow-arm push hold | 3 x 20–30 sec each side | Band anchored behind you; push straight out as if holding the bow; bow shoulder low, elbow rotated slightly out |
| Seated or standing single-arm rows | 3 x 12 each arm | Lead with the elbow; finish by squeezing the shoulder blade back |
| Reverse flyes | 3 x 12 | Rear deltoid; slight bend in elbows |
| Simulated draw (SPT) | 3 x 10 each side | See drill below |
| Reverse-side draws | 1 x 10 | Draw on your non-dominant side to keep the body balanced |
| Pallof press (anti-rotation) | 2 x 10 each side | Core stability for a still stance |
| Plank hold | 2 x 30–45 sec | Torso stability |

### SPT Form Drill (The Key Exercise)

SPT = Specific Physical Training. This is the main tool for refining biomechanics away from the bow.

Setup: Hold the band with the bow hand at arm's length and hook it with the draw fingers as if it were a string. Choose a band that gives about 50–70% of your bow's draw weight at your draw length.

Sequence (each rep):

1. Stance—feet shoulder-width apart, weight 60/40 towards the balls of the feet, hips level.
2. Set—bow shoulder low, chest down slightly, head turned to the target.
3. Set-up—raise both arms, bow arm extended, draw elbow high.
4. Draw—bring the hand to anchor using the back; feel the shoulder blade move.
5. Anchor—hand under the jaw, string (band) touching the chin and nose.
6. Transfer and hold—shift the load from arms to back; hold 3–5 seconds while gently "expanding".
7. Release—relax the fingers _while still pulling back_; the draw hand should travel back along the neck. (Only do this with an archery-specific form strip, or a band anchored to the bow hand so it cannot snap.)
8. Follow-through—hold the position for 2 seconds after release.

Progression:

- Weeks 1–2: 3 x 10 reps, 3-second holds
- Weeks 3–4: 3 x 10 reps, 5-second holds
- Weeks 5–6: 3 x 8 reps, 8–10 second holds
- After that: increase band resistance or build up to 20–30 second holds for endurance

Mirror checklist:

- Is the draw shoulder low, or creeping up?
- Is the draw elbow in line with the arrow line, or pointing out to the side?
- Is the bow shoulder collapsed in or pushed up?
- Is the head still, or moving forward to meet the hand?
- Does the release hand stay on the neck, or flare out?

### Notes for a Late Starter

- Expect gradual progress. Tendons and ligaments need 6–12 weeks to adapt. Don't increase draw weight on the bow just because the bands feel easy.
- Pain rules: Muscle fatigue is fine. Sharp pain, pinching at the front or top of the shoulder, or elbow pain means stop and regress.
- Prioritise Routine A. Rotator cuff and lower-trap work is the best protection for the shoulder over years of shooting.
- Video yourself every 2 weeks. Compare side-on and from behind to see real changes.
- Ask your coach to check your SPT form once—it is easy to rehearse a fault without noticing.
- If you have existing shoulder, neck or blood-pressure issues, check with a GP or physio before doing long holds.
The best way is to learn the shot in pieces: practise each step on its own, then link them together one at a time. Here's a method using the band.

### Break the Shot into Steps

Use a fixed list of steps and give each one a one-word cue you can say in your head:

1. Stance–"Feet"
2. Hook and grip–"Hook"
3. Set (shoulders low, head turned to the target)–"Set"
4. Set-up (raise the arms, bow arm out, draw elbow high)–"Up"
5. Draw (pull with your back, not your arm)–"Draw"
6. Anchor–"Anchor"
7. Transfer and hold (the load moves from your arm into your back)–"Load"
8. Expand and release–"Expand"
9. Follow-through–"Finish"

Say the cues the same way every time. In the end they become your shot routine on the range.

### Stage 1: Practise Each step Alone (Weeks 1–2)

Do one step at a time, then stop and check it:

- Get into the position and hold it for 5 seconds.
- Check yourself in a mirror or on a phone video.
- Fix anything that's off, relax, and repeat. Do about 5 good reps per step.

Spend the most time on Set, Draw and Load, because that's where most faults start.

### Stage 2: Link the Steps together (Weeks 2–4)

Build the chain up a step at a time:

- First do Stance and Hook, then pause.
- Then Stance, Hook and Set, then pause.
- Keep adding one step until you can do the whole sequence.

If one step goes wrong, go back to the start of the chain. Don't patch it halfway through, or you'll learn the mistake.

Backward chaining also helps. Start at full draw and practise just Expand and Finish until they feel automatic. Then start one step earlier each time. Release is the hardest part to learn, so this way it gets the most practice.

### Stage 3: Same Rhythm Every time (Weeks 4–6)

Do the full shot with a steady count, for example:

- Set-up: 1–2
- Draw: 3–4
- Anchor and Load: 5–6
- Expand and release: 7–8

Aim for the same timing on every rep. A shot that's consistent in the band drill is more likely to be consistent on the range.

### Stage 4: Draw Drill with Your Eyes Closed

Once you can run the full shot smoothly, close your eyes at anchor. Feel where your:

- draw-side shoulder blade is
- string hand sits against your jaw
- bow shoulder is

Then open your eyes and check them in the mirror. This builds the body awareness you'll use with a real bow.

### Tips

- Band strength: for practising the steps, use a light band, about 30–50% of your draw weight. Save heavier bands for strength work.
- Release: only let go if you're using an archery training band or one tied to your bow hand.
- Little and often: 10 careful minutes a day teaches you more than one long session.
- Write down your focus: pick one step each session (for example, "Set this week") and note it, so your progress shows up in your training log.

## Master the Draw A Beginner's Resistance Band & Blank Bale Training Progression

### 1. Introduction: Why Train with Resistance Bands First?

For an aspiring archer, introducing a heavy bow too early often introduces physical limits, excessive structural tension, and visual aiming anxiety. A rigid bow handle and heavy draw weight naturally mask underlying execution flaws, forcing the brain to default to inefficient muscle recruitment just to manage the load. Training with a light resistance band (such as a red or yellow stretch band) strips away equipment resistance and isolates motor skill acquisition.

By using a light band, you leverage proprioception—your body's subconscious awareness of its position, weight, and resistance in space—to build a rapid, high-fidelity brain-to-body connection. The conscious mind can process only one instructional thought at a time, but the subconscious executes complex, multi-joint movements simultaneously through imprinted "motor programs." Because every movement performed leaves a permanent neurological footprint, practicing with a stretch band ensures that correct movement schemas are established without the anxious distraction of trying to hit a target face or struggling against heavy poundage.

💡 The 3 Core Masteries

1. Passive Finger Hook & Wrist Control: Isolating the finger flexors while eliminating destructive forearm and wrist tension.
2. Dynamic Back Alignment & Elbow Path: Engaging the scapular retractors and posterior cuff through proper rotational mechanics.
3. Smooth Passive Release Mechanics: Relaxing the draw hand smoothly under continuous back tension without plucking the string.

Before attempting to isolate fine motor control in the hands and back, you must first prepare the central nervous system and shoulder girdle through a dynamic warm-up.

### 2. Phase 0: The Dynamic Band Warm-Up Routine

A proper dynamic warm-up increases core body temperature, draws blood out of the digestive system into working muscles, widens blood vessels to optimize oxygen delivery, and breaks up tight fascial adhesions. Performing these exercises with a light stretch band wakes up the central nervous system and loosens shoulder fascia before form isolation begins.

|   |   |   |
|---|---|---|
|Exercise Name|Target Area & Purpose|Execution Cues|
|Band Passovers|Shoulder joint fascia & chest|Hold the band in front with fully locked elbows. Pass the band smoothly overhead all the way down to the lower back, then return to the front.|
|Band Halos|Shoulder girdle & thoracic mobility|Maintain fully locked elbows and move the band overhead in large, continuous circular halo motions in both directions.|
|Front 'T' Retractions|Rhomboids & mid-trapezius|Hold the band straight out at chest height. Squeeze shoulder blades to pull arms horizontally apart into a 'T' shape; hold for 2 seconds before relaxing.|
|Overhead 'T' Retractions|Latissimus dorsi & lower trapezius|Start with the band directly overhead. Pull downward and apart, dropping your arms into a 'T' shape behind the head.|
|External Rotator Pulls|Rotator cuff (_infraspinatus_ & _teres minor_)|Keep one elbow anchored tightly against your ribs at 90 degrees, holding the band. With the other hand holding the opposite end extended, rotate the working forearm outward away from the body while keeping the elbow pinned to the side.|

Now that the shoulder joints are mobile and blood circulation is optimized, the central nervous system is prepped to isolate fine motor control in the finger hook.

### 3. Phase 1: Step-by-Step Biomechanical Drill Progression

This progressive drill sequence breaks the archery shot cycle down into grokkable, isolated movements. Replicate each drill using a light stretch band before introducing actual bow weight.

#### 3.1 Drill 1: The Passive Finger Hook & Release (Finger Slip Drill)

- Objective: Internalize the feeling of a passive release while maintaining structural relaxation in the drawing wrist and forearm.
- Biomechanical Focus: Isolating the _Flexor Digitorum Superficialis_ (FDS)—which flexes the proximal interphalangeal (PIP) joints—and the _Flexor Digitorum Profundus_ (FDP)—which flexes the distal interphalangeal (DIP) joints—to maintain the hook while keeping the wrist and forearm flexors completely relaxed.
- Step-by-Step Execution:
    1. Stand upright and hook the finger pads of both hands directly together (index, middle, and ring fingers).
    2. Bring both hands up to your neck at anchor level.
    3. Apply expanding linear tension between your arms, maintaining a straight, relaxed wrist joint.
    4. Maintain "Jelly Fingers" in the drawing hand—ensuring the forearm flexors remain utterly soft once tension is established.
    5. Focus on maintaining active back engagement while allowing the finger pads to smoothly slip apart naturally without any active muscle effort in the hand.
- Common Faults to Avoid:
    - Active "plucking" of the fingers outward away from the neck.
    - "White-knuckle" gripping or over-engaging forearm flexors, which kinks the wrist joint and blocks back connection.

#### 3.2 Drill 2: Front Shoulder Set & Core Alignment

- Objective: Stabilize the front bow shoulder low in its socket while maintaining a solid lower body pillar.
- Biomechanical Focus: Lowering the center of gravity and engaging the _serratus anterior_, _subscapularis_, and latissimus dorsi to pack the glenohumeral joint and shoulder girdle downward under pre-tension.
- Step-by-Step Execution:
    1. Establish a square stance with feet shoulder-width apart, parallel to the shooting line.
    2. Engage your core: crunch the abdominals, keep your chest down, tilt the ribcage inward, and tuck the hips into a neutral position.
    3. Hold the stretch band in front of you with a light pre-tension (approximately 1 to 2 inches of stretch).
    4. Internally rotate the front bow arm before applying full tension to ensure proper string clearance on the arm.
    5. Raise both hands together to eye level, engaging the lats to pack the bow shoulder down into its socket as you raise.
- Common Faults to Avoid:
    - Initiating the raise from below horizontal, which forces the front deltoid to pull the bow shoulder upward.
    - Arching the lower back or flaring the chest/sternum upward during the raise.

#### 3.3 Drill 3: Scapular Draw & Elbow Path (The Execution Draw Drill)

- Objective: Train back tension as continuous movement by bypassing hand tension and pulling directly with the scapular retractors.
- Biomechanical Focus: Engagement of the scapular retractors and posterior cuff (_LAN2_ / posterior deltoid area) while eliminating active drawing hand force.
- Pedagogical Rationale: Focusing directly on flexing specific back muscles often causes co-contraction—simultaneously flexing opposing chest and arm muscles—which locks the body in place and stops the draw. By focusing instead on the movement of the _LAN2_ point (the posterior deltoid) in a continuous arc around behind the head, the central nervous system automatically recruits the correct scapular retractors without creating anti-developmental tension.
- Step-by-Step Execution:
    1. Loop a Thera-band directly over the point of your drawing elbow, leaving your drawing hand completely free without gripping the band.
    2. Establish posture and raise both arms to eye level.
    3. Draw by rotating the _LAN2_ point and draw elbow in a continuous arc straight around behind your head, letting the relaxed hand simply follow the elbow's path.
    4. Continue drawing through the anchor position into a full follow-through squeeze, holding the squeeze for 5 seconds to prime scapular retraction.
    5. Execute a controlled, slow eccentric let-down, returning fluidly backward directly through the correct anchor position to the pre-draw position.
- Common Faults to Avoid:
    - Pulling inward with the hand, which causes the band to open up into a triangle shape and breaks linear alignment.
    - Dropping the draw elbow below the force line during the draw or eccentric let-down.

#### 3.4 Drill 4: Finding Anchor & Reference Points (The "Fake Anchor" Drill)

- Objective: Establish repeatable, multi-point facial contact without altering head posture.
- Biomechanical Focus: Maintaining a still, vertical head ("central pillar") while bringing the drawing hand inward to achieve bone-to-bone contact.
- Step-by-Step Execution:
    1. Draw the band toward your collarbone or lower neck first to confirm complete back muscle transfer.
    2. Slide the drawing hand upward and inward along the neck until it meets the jawline.
    3. Lock in the 3 Non-Negotiable Reference Points:
        - Top of the drawing hand pressed firmly along the jawline.
        - Band/string touching the tip or center of the nose.
        - Thumb tucked deeply into the natural muscular pocket of the neck.
    4. Keep the chin slightly elevated to provide a flat shelf along the jawline.
    5. Micro-Head Tilt Protocol: If string blur or band alignment is slightly off upon reaching anchor, never shift your anchor hand across your face or allow the hand to float. Keep the hand locked to the jawline and perform a subtle micro-head tilt until the string blur aligns perfectly with your visual reference.
- Common Faults to Avoid:
    - Tucking the chin downward or dipping the head forward to meet the band.
    - Allowing the anchor hand to "float" in open space without firm facial contact.

With isolated movement patterns established, you can now transition into building physical holding capacity and muscular endurance.

### 4. Phase 2: Building Archery Endurance–The 20:40 SPT Protocol

Specific Physical Training (SPT) hold routines build static holding strength, skeletal alignment, and neuromuscular efficiency. Sustaining isometric holds teaches the central nervous system which muscles require active tension and which must stay relaxed.

#### Overview & Protocol Structure

- Work-to-Rest Ratio: 20 seconds static hold at full draw / 40 seconds rest.
- Volume: 10 repetitions total (~10 minutes total workout time).
- Equipment: Light resistance band or ultra-light bow setup paired with a full-length mirror.
- Mirror Practice Visual Setup: Stand perpendicular to (pointing your body away from) the full-length mirror, turning only your eyes toward the reflection. This visual setup allows real-time verification of shoulder height, elbow path, and anchor contact without twisting your head or neck out of proper shooting alignment.

#### The 3 Non-Negotiable Mirror Alignment Checks

While holding at full draw in front of the mirror, continuously monitor your reflection for these structural baselines:

1. Front Shoulder: Packed down low into its socket and internally rotated (lats and _serratus anterior_ engaged).
2. Draw Elbow: Elevated slightly above the arrow force line, actively rotating around behind the back.
3. Posture: Straight vertical "T-shape" with the chest pressed down, abs crunched, and hips neutral.

⚠️ The Golden Rule of Fatigue Execute every single second of the hold with pristine technique. If fatigue causes your posture, shoulder position, or core alignment to break down at any point during a hold, let down immediately. Muscling through bad form under fatigue imprints flawed movement habits permanently into your subconscious motor programs.

Once static alignment and holding capacity are ingrained, you are ready to apply these motor patterns to close-range target work with an actual bow.

### 5. Phase 3: Transitioning to Close-Range Blank Bale Practice

Blank bale shooting serves as the bridge between resistance band drills and active bow handling. Positioning a large, plain target butt at a close distance of 2 to 3 yards (5 to 10 meters) with no target face or bullseye attached completely eliminates target panic and visual aiming anxiety, allowing 100% of your conscious bandwidth to focus strictly on physical sensations and motor execution.

#### Blank Bale Execution Checklist

1. Establish Stance: Step onto the line with a square stance, feet parallel and shoulder-width apart. Plant your feet firmly and do not shuffle between shots.
2. Set Hook & Grip (The Lifeline Rule):
    - Set the finger hook first: place the string in the first groove of the middle finger, wrapping the index and ring fingers naturally, with the pinky curled inward. Apply light upward pressure toward the chin.
    - Place the bow hand into the grip using The Lifeline Rule: contact must reside solely on the meaty pad of the thumb (_thenar eminence_), ensuring no part of the riser crosses over the central "lifeline" of the palm, which prevents hand torque. Maintain soft "Jelly Fingers" in the bow hand.
3. Raise & Draw: Apply 1 to 2 inches of pre-tension. Raise both hands to eye level while internally rotating the front arm and packing the front shoulder down with the lats. Initiate the draw by driving the draw elbow around behind your back using scapular retractors.
4. Anchor Contact & Alignment: Bring the hand to the jawline without moving the head from its central pillar position. Confirm the top of the hand along the jaw, string on the nose tip, and thumb tucked in the neck pocket. Execute a micro-head tilt if necessary to align string blur without moving the hand.
5. Passive Release: Keep back muscles actively contracting backward. Maintain "Jelly Fingers" in the drawing hand—relax the _flexor digitorum_ muscles (FDS/FDP) in the fingers, allowing the string to slip free naturally without active hand opening or plucking outward.
6. Fluid Follow-Through: Keep the bow arm extended toward the target. Allow the draw hand fingernails to brush lightly along the jawline toward the ear in a smooth, continuous line backward, maintaining posture until the arrow strikes the target.

To ensure long-term skill acquisition and prevent technical regression, structure your ongoing practice around core operational guidelines.

### 6. The Golden Rules of Form & Band Practice

1. Alternate Band and Bow: Avoid single-discipline marathons. Alternate 3–6 repetitions on the resistance band with 3–6 shots on the bow during a practice session to ensure perfect motor transfer.
2. Maintain Realistic Speed: Because resistance bands are light, it is easy to draw them too rapidly. Synchronize your hands and perform band drills at the exact same draw cadence you use when shooting a bow.
3. Prioritize Quality Over Volume: Fine motor skills rely on focused repetition. Executing 30 hyper-focused repetitions with pristine mechanics builds superior subconscious motor programs compared to 150 mindless, fatigued repetitions.
4. Use Immediate Visual Feedback: Never guess your alignment. Always practice in front of a full-length mirror (looking with eyes only while perpendicular) or use video recording to verify and adjust your shoulder, spine, and elbow positions in real time.
5. Process Over Target: Measure the success of a shot entirely by how the movement felt within your body, never by target outcomes. Focus on controlling the physical process to build true execution confidence.

The anchor point (referred to in technical coaching manuals as "facial marks" or the "draw reference point") serves as the fixed rear sight for your shot alignment. Within the broader context of shot execution, it bridges the transition from drawing the bow to expanding and executing a clean release.

---

## 1. Definition and Function as the "Rear Sight"

- Spatial Alignment: The anchor establishes the precise spatial position of the arrow nock relative to your dominant aiming eye, creating the rear reference required for consistent elevation and horizontal group consistency.
- "Facial Marks" vs. "Anchor": Modern coaching frameworks often prefer the terms "facial marks" or "draw reference points" over "anchor" because the word "anchor" implies that motion completely stops. In proper shot execution, the draw transitions smoothly into expansion without a static freeze.

---

## 2. Anatomical Reference Points for a Solid Connection

A repeatable target recurve anchor relies on a three-point contact system:

1. Jawline Contact: The top of the drawing hand (or the shelf of the finger tab) presses firmly along and under the lower jawbone.
2. Facial/String Contact: The bowstring touches the center or tip of the nose and the corner of the chin.
3. Neck Pocket: The thumb stretches down and tucks securely into the natural muscular pocket along the neck (the sternocleidomastoid muscle).

For barebow, field, or longbow shooting, high anchors (such as the index finger under the cheekbone or at the corner of the mouth) are commonly used to alter the arrow's initial line of sight.

---

## 3. Key Shot Execution Mechanics at Anchor

- "String to Face, Never Face to String": Your head must act as an unmoving central pillar locked in position before the draw begins. Leaning the neck or reaching the chin forward to meet the string disrupts posture and draw length. Lifting the chin slightly creates a clean, flat shelf along the jawline for the hand to seat against.
- Bone-to-Bone Stability: The drawing hand must press past soft, shifting skin to establish firm bone-to-bone contact against the jaw. A "floating anchor" (holding in open space near the face) is a primary cause of erratic group dispersion downrange.
- Anchoring "Deep" via the Draw Elbow: Moving into anchor is achieved by bringing the hand _inward_ toward the neck rather than pulling further back past the face. Leading this movement with the drawing elbow allows the hand and wrist to remain flat and relaxed without kinking.
- Maintaining Face Pressure Through Expansion: Once established, facial contact and pressure must remain continuous as you transition into expansion and pull through the clicker. If string picture alignment is slightly off when reaching anchor, you should make a microscopic head tilt rather than moving the anchor hand across the jaw.

---

## 4. Integration with Release and Follow-Through

- The "Back End" Path: Upon release, the drawing hand must remain passive and slide straight backward along the jawline and neck toward the ear or shoulder (a region referred to as the "back end").
- Avoiding Plucking and Collapsing: If the hand pops outward away from the face (plucking) or creeps forward upon release (collapsing), it indicates that back tension was lost at the anchor point.

---

Building a rock-solid, repeatable anchor point (often referred to as establishing your facial marks or draw reference point) requires creating an unyielding physical connection between your hand, face, and neck. Because your anchor acts as the rear sight for your shot, even a tiny shift in position will scatter your arrows downrange.

---

### 1. Set Your Head and Posture First

- Head as a Still Pillar: Set your head in a level, erect position facing the target _before_ you begin your draw.
- "String to Face, Never Face to String": Bring the bowstring directly to your stationary face. Leaning your neck forward or dropping your chin to meet the string collapses your posture and alters your draw length.
- Lift the Chin Slightly: If your chin is tucked too low, you cannot form a clean, stable connection along your jawline. Raising your chin slightly creates a flat, straight shelf for your drawing hand to seat against.

---

### 2. Establish the Three Primary Reference Contacts ("Bone-to-Bone")

Avoid "floating" your hand in open space or relying on soft skin that slides. Press past soft tissue to establish firm, repeatable bone-to-bone contact across three distinct points:

1. The Jawline Contact: Press the top of your drawing hand (or the shelf of your finger tab) firmly along and underneath your lower jawbone.
2. The String Contact: Touch the bowstring lightly to the center or tip of your nose and the corner/center of your chin.
3. The Neck Pocket Contact: Tuck your draw hand thumb deeply into the natural muscular pocket along the side of your neck.

---

### 3. Anchor "Inward" Using Your Elbow (Not Hand Muscles)

- Pull Inward, Not Past: Anchoring "deep" does not mean dragging your hand further back past your ear. It means pulling your hand inward toward your neck.
- Lead with the Draw Elbow: Initiate and guide the anchoring movement using your draw elbow rather than pulling with your forearm or bicep muscles.
- Elbow Height: Keeping your drawing elbow slightly higher than the horizontal line of the arrow allows your draw hand to settle completely relaxed onto the jawbone without putting unwanted tension into your wrist or fingers.

---

### 4. Refine Alignment and Train Tactile Feedback

- Use Micro-Head Tilts for Alignment: If you reach full draw and notice your peripheral string picture is slightly off, do not slide your anchor hand across your jaw. Keep your hand locked in place and perform a microscopic tilt of your head to bring the string blur into alignment.
- Train by Feel with a Mirror: Practice drawing with a lightweight resistance band or an elastic string in front of a mirror. Once you visually confirm your reference points in the mirror, close your eyes to concentrate purely on the tactile sensation against your face, neck, and jaw until the placement becomes automatic.
