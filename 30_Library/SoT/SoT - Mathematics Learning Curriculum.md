## Minimum Viable Understanding (MVU)

Mastering the field of mathematics requires a progressive, structured learning plan starting from foundational arithmetic and logic, moving through analysis and algebra, and finally branching into specialised fields like topology, combinatorics, and applied mathematics.

## Working Knowledge

The progression of mathematical study can be structured into eight core phases:

1. Foundations: Basic arithmetic, algebra sequence (intermediate, college, pre-calculus), proof writing (e.g., _How to Prove It_ by Velleman), and basic set/number theory.
2. Analysis & Calculus: The mathematics of change. Calculus 1-3, differential equations (ODEs/PDEs), real analysis (rigorous, proof-based calculus), and complex/numerical analysis.
3. Algebra and Structures: Abstraction of standard algebra. Linear algebra (computational then proof-based), abstract algebra (groups, rings, fields), and advanced structures like Galois Theory.
4. Geometry & Topology: Spatial properties and continuous functions. Euclidean geometry to differential geometry, general topology, and algebraic topology. Requires heavy prerequisites.
5. Discrete Mathematics & Combinatorics: Essential for computer science. Covers logic, binary relations, induction, graph theory, and combinatorial counting.
6. Probability & Statistics: Data inference. Basic probability/statistics moving to rigorous mathematical statistics requiring heavy calculus.
7. Applied Mathematics & Modelling: Applying abstract math to reality. Physics/engineering math (modeling physical systems) and cryptography.
8. Advanced Topics & Frontiers: Niche research fields (e.g., combinatorial topology). A comprehensive review text like _All the Math You Missed_ by Garrity is useful here.

## Current Understanding

The structured approach highlights a critical turning point in mathematical education: the shift from computation to proof writing. Foundational logic and rigorous proofs (as introduced in phase 1 and mastered in phase 2 real analysis) form the essential bridge between early mathematical computation and the ability to grasp higher-order abstract structures in topology, abstract algebra, and advanced mathematical statistics.
