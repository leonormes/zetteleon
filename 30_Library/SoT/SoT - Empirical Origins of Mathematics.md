## Minimum Viable Understanding (MVU)

Historically, mathematics often did not begin as an abstract pursuit, but rather emerged as a necessary tool to solve practical, physical, and commercial problems in the real world.

## Working Knowledge

- Commerce and Numeral Systems: Mathematical tools like Arabic numerals and the concept of zero were introduced to Europe (e.g., by Fibonacci) driven by the practical needs of merchants and bankers for complex financial calculations.
- Physical Measurement and Calculus: Johannes Kepler developed the foundations of integral calculus by solving the highly practical problem of measuring the volume of three-dimensional wine barrels (conceptually slicing them into infinitely thin planes).
- Newton's Empirical Mathematics: Isaac Newton's mathematical theories were heavily grounded in real-world experiments, including jumping to measure wind force, building sundials for timekeeping, testing fluid resistance with pendulums, and applying geometry to optics (measuring thin films of air with convex lenses).

## Current Understanding

The abstract purity of modern mathematics often obscures its deeply empirical and practical origins. From Fibonacci's commercial arithmetic to Kepler's cask volumes and Newton's rigorous physical experiments, the most profound mathematical leaps were frequently born from a direct need to measure, model, or manipulate the physical world. This aligns with the broader methodological view that mathematical abstraction is fundamentally about "stripping to essence" from lived, empirical reality.
