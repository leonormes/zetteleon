## 2. Ontology of Money

Money is a complex entity that defies simple categorization as purely concrete or abstract. Its nature is fundamentally institutional.

### 2.1 Nature and Functions

- Money is often mistakenly viewed as merely a concrete object (coins, paper). However, with the advent of electronic money, it is more accurately understood as an **abstract object** or a **property of an agent**.
- **John Searle's theory** posits that institutional statuses, like money, are imposed on objects. Electronic money further challenges this, suggesting money's status as a collection of causal powers within a larger institutional structure.

### 2.2 Institutional Reality

- Money's existence is sustained by **collective acceptance or belief**. It is a social fact that persists as long as there is general consensus about its value and function.
- Its value is not intrinsic but is granted by its embeddedness in societal agreements and regulatory frameworks.

### 2.3 Abstract and Concrete Dualism

- **Concrete Money:** Physical currency (coins, notes) represents a tangible form.
- **Abstract Money:** Electronic funds, digital currencies, and balances in accounts are abstract.
- This dualism highlights money's adaptable nature as a social construct.

## 3. Ontology of Wealth

Wealth is a more expansive concept than money, encompassing a wide array of valuable possessions and capabilities.

### 3.1 Wealth as a Broader Concept

- Wealth includes all valuable resources and assets, both tangible and intangible. This extends beyond mere currency to include:
  - **Tangible Assets:** Real estate, commodities, physical goods.
  - **Intangible Assets:** Stocks, bonds, intellectual property, human capital (skills, knowledge).

### 3.2 Economic and Social Dimensions

- **Economically:** Wealth is quantitatively measured as the total value of assets minus liabilities.
- **Socially:** Wealth confers status, power, and influence within a society, reflecting its perceived value and distribution. These social dimensions are not always directly quantifiable in monetary terms.

### 3.3 Institutional and Conventional Aspects

- The definition and valuation of wealth are also shaped by **social conventions and institutional frameworks**.
- Market conditions, legal regulations, and collective beliefs significantly influence how different forms of wealth are recognized and valued.

---

## 4. Relationship Between Money and Wealth

Money and wealth share an ontological interdependence, with money often serving as a key mechanism for the accumulation, representation, and exchange of wealth.

### 4.1 Money as a Tool for Wealth

- **Medium of Exchange:** Money facilitates the exchange of goods and services, enabling the transfer and aggregation of value that contributes to wealth.
- **Store of Value:** Money can retain its purchasing power over time, making it a convenient way to hold and accumulate wealth.

### 4.2 Ontological Interdependence

- Money's status as an institutionally sustained entity makes it a crucial component of modern wealth systems. Without money, the measurement and exchange of wealth would be far more rudimentary and complex.
- Conversely, the concept of wealth provides money with its purpose; money is primarily valuable because it can acquire and represent various forms of wealth.

### 4.3 Abstract and Concrete Wealth

- Just as with money, wealth manifests in both abstract (e.g., financial instruments) and concrete (e.g., land) forms.
- The institutional frameworks underpin both money and wealth, ensuring their recognized value and function within society.

---

## 5. Summary

From an ontological viewpoint, money is a social and institutional construct that serves as a tool for economic interaction, while wealth is the aggregate of all valuable possessions and resources. Money is a *component* and *facilitator* of wealth, but not synonymous with it. Both are deeply rooted in collective belief and social structures.
