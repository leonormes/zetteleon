Paper Review: Synthea and the Generation of Synthetic Healthcare Records

The provided paper outlines the development of Synthea, an open-source software system designed to generate Realistic Synthetic Electronic Health Records (RS-EHRs) [1, 2]. The authors tackle a major problem in healthcare IT and research: the lack of accessible, high-quality data due to strict privacy, security, and legal restrictions [3, 4].

The paper points out that traditional anonymization techniques still carry high risks of patient re-identification, and many existing synthetic data generation methods rely on real patient records as inputs, thus inheriting privacy liabilities [5-7]. To solve this, Synthea was built using the PADARSER framework (Publicly Available Data Approach to the Realistic Synthetic EHR), which assumes access to real records is impossible [8]. Instead, it generates fully synthetic, "cradle-to-grave" patient histories using publicly available demographic statistics, clinical practice guidelines, and disease prevalence rates [9-12].

Ideas to Adopt for Your Own Data Generation Project

Based on Synthea's architecture and methodologies, here are several highly effective strategies you can adopt for your own synthetic data generation project, regardless of your specific industry:

1. Eliminate Real-Data Dependencies for Absolute Privacy

If your domain has strict data privacy constraints, avoid using real data as a seed or training set, which can lead to accidental data leakage or re-identification [13]. Instead, adopt Synthea's PADARSER approach: build your simulation entirely from publicly available aggregate statistics, rules, and guidelines [8-10]. This guarantees the output is fully synthetic and free of legal and privacy restrictions [1, 9].

2. Use State-Transition Machines to Model Behavior

To create complex, realistic data, Synthea models disease progression and patient journeys using state-transition machines encoded in JSON [14]. You can build a similar generic module framework by defining:

- States: Separate your logic into _Control States_ (e.g., initial, terminal, delay, or counter states that track attributes) and _Output States_ (e.g., events that write data to your final record, like "Encounter" or "ConditionOnset") [15-18].
- Transitions: Move between states using diverse logic paths:
    - _Direct:_ Moves from A to B [19].
    - _Distributed (Probabilistic):_ Moves based on percentage distributions (e.g., an 80% chance of path A, 20% chance of path B) [19, 20].
    - _Conditional:_ Moves based on specific attributes or historical data (e.g., if the generated user is over age 50, transition to a specific state) [20, 21].

3. Implement Longitudinal "Lifespan" Simulations

Instead of generating isolated, static data points, simulate a timeline. Synthea tracks synthetic patients from birth to death by computing state transitions for every person at configurable timesteps (e.g., every 7 days) [11, 14, 22, 23]. This continuous simulation enables the creation of highly cohesive, historically accurate data sequences.

4. Design for Modularity and Open Formats

Synthea stores its state-machine models in standard JSON format, allowing the models to be easily inspected, modified, and expanded upon by domain experts [14, 22]. Designing your data generation rules as standalone, readable modules will make it much easier to scale horizontally and allow collaborators to contribute new scenarios [22, 24].

5. Output to Strict Industry Standards

Your generated data's value relies heavily on its interoperability. Synthea ensures its simulated patient records are immediately useful to developers by formatting the output into widely accepted industry standards, such as HL7 FHIR and C-CDA [25, 26]. Identify the standard data formats for your target use case and build exporters that map your simulation output directly into those formats [15, 27].

6. Validate and Tune Using Aggregate Statistics

Because this approach uses a "roll of the dice" probability model at each timestep, the simulation will not perfectly map to specific outputs right away [28, 29]. To validate your generator, run large batches of synthetic data and compare the resulting population-level statistics against real-world aggregate data [30, 31]. If there is a variance (e.g., Synthea initially generated too many young patients with diabetes), you can easily tune the transition probabilities in your state machines until the aggregate outputs mirror real-world prevalence [32-35].

The OMOP Standardized Vocabularies are a foundational component of the Observational Health Data Sciences and Informatics (OHDSI) Common Data Model (CDM) that provide a common repository for standardizing and mapping medical terminologies [1, 2]. Rather than being a single, standalone terminology, the OMOP vocabulary acts as an aggregator of existing public vocabularies (such as SNOMED, LOINC, RxNorm, and ICD) and fills in terminology gaps where necessary [3]. By encoding data at a semantic layer, it enables disparate health records to be mapped to standard concepts, ensuring interoperability across different systems and countries [4, 5]. Currently, the vocabulary maps to over 150 different terminologies and contains over 11 million concepts [6, 7].

When They Are Updated

The Standardized Vocabularies are not a static dataset; they are updated on a regular cadence, typically twice a year (with major community-wide releases often occurring in February and August) [3, 7-9]. These regular updates are necessary because the upstream source vocabularies (such as ICD or SNOMED) are frequently updated by their respective governing bodies, which creates a downstream effect requiring the OMOP vocabularies to update as well [3]. The community converged on two major releases per year to make it easier for decentralized data partners to stay in sync for network research [9].

The Update Process

The process of building, maintaining, and updating the vocabularies is managed by the OHDSI Vocabulary Team and relies heavily on community input:

- Data Ingestion and Pruning: The Vocabulary Team continuously pulls in updates from upstream sources, such as the Unified Medical Language System (UMLS) [10, 11]. Because these source systems can contain a lot of noise, the team prunes unnecessary data and enhances the mappings using historical work and established conventions [10].
- Formatting via the Pallas System: All of the disparate vocabularies are consolidated into a single, common format using an internal system called Pallas [2]. This automated ingestion relieves researchers from needing to manage the differing formats, structures, and lifecycles of individual source vocabularies [2].
- Community Issue Tracking: The update process is highly community-driven. If researchers or ontologists find missing vocabularies, mismatched mappings, or orphan codes during their work, they submit an issue on the OHDSI GitHub repository [11-13]. The Vocabulary Team reviews these issues, creates or adjusts the mappings, and incorporates these fixes into the next mainstream release cycle [11, 12].
- Distribution via ATHENA: Once the vocabularies are built, users do not need to compile the vocabulary database themselves. Instead, they can search for and download the finalized, updated vocabulary files directly through a web-based OHDSI tool called ATHENA [14, 15].
- Local ETL Maintenance: When a new vocabulary version drops, local data stewards must review the changes to see how it impacts their data [16]. They can use tools like the _Tantalus_ package to run a "diff" between the previous and new versions of the vocabulary to identify which codes have changed or become deprecated [17]. Because vocabulary updates alter concept mappings, developers often have to rerun their entire Extract, Transform, Load (ETL) process to ensure their local CDM reflects the newest standard concepts [18].

While the OMOP Standardized Vocabularies contain millions of mappings, OMOP is much more than just a terminology dictionary. At its core, the Observational Medical Outcomes Partnership (OMOP) is a Common Data Model (CDM) [1].

While the vocabulary mappings provide the _semantic_ standardization (translating disparate local codes into a common language), the CDM provides the _structural_ standardization [2-4]. It organizes healthcare data into a standardized, patient-centric relational database format divided into distinct clinical domains, such as conditions, drug exposures, measurements, and procedures [5-7].

By standardizing both the structure and semantics of the data, the OMOP CDM serves several highly powerful purposes:

1. Enabling Distributed Network Research

Traditionally, conducting a multi-site study required creating custom scripts for every individual institution's unique data structure [8]. Because OMOP harmonizes data into a uniform format, researchers can write a single analysis script and execute it across hundreds of disparate databases worldwide (such as electronic health records, claims data, and clinical registries) [9-11]. This allows for massive, global observational research without sensitive patient data ever leaving an institution's firewall [11, 12].

2. Standardizing Advanced Analytics

The OHDSI (Observational Health Data Sciences and Informatics) community uses the OMOP CDM to power three primary clinical analytics use cases:

- Clinical Characterization: Answering _"What happened to them?"_ by summarizing population demographics, computing disease incidence rates, and visualizing treatment pathways (e.g., mapping the typical sequence of drugs given to hypertension patients) [13-15].
- Population-Level Estimation: Answering _"What are the causal effects?"_ by utilizing advanced epidemiological methods to perform safety surveillance and comparative effectiveness studies (e.g., determining if drug A causes more major bleeding events than drug B) [14, 16].
- Patient-Level Prediction: Answering _"What will happen to me?"_ by applying machine learning algorithms to historical data to predict individual outcomes, such as estimating the probability a specific patient will attempt suicide in the year following a depression diagnosis [17, 18].

3. Powering an Open-Source Software Ecosystem

Because the data structure is predictable, the OMOP CDM is backed by over 6 million lines of open-source software [19]. This includes powerful tools like ATLAS (a web-based platform for defining patient cohorts and designing analyses without writing code), ACHILLES (a tool for visual data characterization), and the Data Quality Dashboard (a tool that runs thousands of automated checks to validate data integrity) [19-22].

4. Streamlining Health System Operations

Beyond academic research, health systems use OMOP internally. It drastically reduces the labor required to submit data to external clinical registries, because the data only needs to be mapped to OMOP once to satisfy multiple registries [23, 24]. It is also being leveraged for operational intelligence and learning health systems, such as tracking antimicrobial stewardship, monitoring sepsis, or building clinical decision support applications right at the point of care [25-27].
