### Atom 001: Contrast Effect in Sequential Political Judgment

- Kind: mechanism
- Statement: When an evaluator is exposed to an extreme, norm-violating political figure, that figure sets an anchor so low that subsequent evaluations of a moderately flawed politician are pulled upward, because the two are compared against each other rather than against an absolute standard.
- Scope & Conditions: Applies to sequential evaluation generally (documented in non-political domains like speed dating); strongest when the anchor is highly salient and the comparison is made close in time. Rooted in the general psychological "contrast effect" (perception of a stimulus exaggerated by juxtaposition with a differing baseline).
- Evidence: "If an individual is exposed to a highly negative, extreme, or norm-violating stimulus, subsequent stimuli of moderate or even poor quality are perceived much more positively than they would be in strict isolation."
- Implications:
    - Voters can rate a routinely dishonest politician as trustworthy purely through comparison, without any change in that politician's actual behaviour.
    - The effect is a property of the evaluation sequence, not of the object being evaluated.

### Atom 002: Social Judgment Theory's Latitude Recalibration Under an Extreme Anchor

- Kind: mechanism
- Statement: Social Judgment Theory holds that people sort incoming political information into a latitude of acceptance, a latitude of noncommitment, and a latitude of rejection relative to their existing attitudes; an extreme anchor figure stretches the whole scale, broadening the latitude of acceptance to absorb behaviour that would otherwise fall into noncommitment or rejection.
- Scope & Conditions: Pioneered by Muzafer Sherif. Applies when the extreme figure is judged to fall deep within the evaluator's latitude of rejection, which is what stretches the rest of the scale. The latitude of noncommitment shrinks correspondingly, forcing more binary in-group/out-group categorisation.
- Evidence: "When an individual encounters an extreme political figure whose actions fall deep within their latitude of rejection, it triggers an intense contrast effect… the voter's internal latitude of acceptance undergoes a structural broadening."
- Implications:
    - Neutrality becomes psychologically harder to sustain as the latitude of noncommitment shrinks.
    - Routine manipulation by an in-group politician can migrate from "unacceptable" to "acceptable" purely via this recalibration, without the politician's behaviour changing.

### Atom 003: The Inclusion/Exclusion Model Explains Why an Extreme Leader Becomes an Isolated Benchmark

- Kind: mechanism
- Statement: Per Schwarz and Bless's Inclusion/Exclusion Model, when a political figure dominates accessible memory through extreme, norm-breaking behaviour, evaluators exclude that figure from the general category "politicians" and instead treat them as a separate, isolated comparison benchmark—so other politicians are judged relative to that one extreme case rather than as members of the same class.
- Scope & Conditions: Requires the extreme figure to be highly cognitively accessible (e.g. dominating the news cycle). Distinguishes this from a "contagion" reading where one bad actor taints trust in the whole category.
- Evidence: "They exclude the extreme leader, treating them as a distinct, isolated, and overwhelmingly negative benchmark… other politicians who lie, manipulate the media, or engage in standard political maneuvering are viewed not as corrupt actors degrading the system, but as the comparatively benign, safe alternative."
- Implications:
    - Explains why ordinary political dishonesty stops being classified as "what politicians do" and starts being read as "not as bad as the real threat."

### Atom 004: Affective Polarization Is Emotional Animosity Independent of Policy Distance

- Kind: definition
- Statement: Affective polarization is the tendency of partisans to feel escalating negative emotion toward opposing partisans and positive emotion toward co-partisans, measured independently of how far apart the two sides actually stand on policy.
- Scope & Conditions: Distinct from ideological polarization (which measures policy-position distance). Documented via implicit association tests and behavioural economic games; found to now exceed other forms of entrenched social prejudice (race, ethnicity, religion) in some studies.
- Evidence: "The escalating tendency of partisans to view opposing partisans negatively and co-partisans positively, irrespective of actual policy disagreements."
- Implications:
    - Two people with nearly identical policy views can still be maximally polarized against each other's party affiliation.
    - Reducing policy disagreement does not automatically reduce affective polarization.

### Atom 005: Negative Partisanship Redefines Political Identity Around Opposition, Not Affiliation

- Kind: definition
- Statement: Negative partisanship is a mode of political identity in which a voter's allegiance is driven more by fear, hatred, and distrust of the opposing party than by genuine affection for their own party's platform or positions.
- Scope & Conditions: Downstream consequence of affective polarization; becomes the dominant driver of voting behaviour under high polarization, overshadowing assessments of candidate quality or competence.
- Evidence: "A voter's political identity is driven significantly more by an intense hatred, fear, and distrust of the opposing party than by any genuine affection for their own party's platform."
- Implications:
    - Voting becomes a defensive act against the out-group rather than an endorsement of the in-group's platform.

### Atom 006: Perceived Out-Group Threat Suppresses In-Group Scrutiny via a Halo Effect

- Kind: mechanism
- Statement: When voters perceive an out-group leader as an existential threat, their cognitive resources shift to defence, which shuts down critical scrutiny of their own side's leaders—not because those leaders are seen as virtuous, but because they are framed as the necessary bulwark against the threat.
- Scope & Conditions: Requires high threat perception, typically stoked by framing the opposing figure as radical or dangerous rather than merely disagreeable. Produces a halo effect that is functional (instrumental to defeating the threat) rather than evaluative (based on the leader's actual conduct).
- Evidence: "The in-group leaders are shielded by a psychological halo effect—not because they are inherently virtuous, but because they are perceived as the necessary, urgent bulwark against a catastrophic threat."
- Implications:
    - In-group leaders can act with reduced accountability during periods of high perceived external threat.
    - The halo effect is contingent on threat perception, not on any change in the shielded leader's behaviour.

### Atom 007: High Affective Polarization Suppresses the Normal Negativity Bias Toward In-Group Lies

- Kind: constraint
- Statement: Under ordinary conditions a single immoral act (e.g. lying) severely and disproportionately damages a person's reputation (negativity bias); under high affective polarization this bias is suppressed for in-group politicians, whose lies are reclassified from moral failings into instrumental competence needed to win an existential contest.
- Scope & Conditions: A boundary condition on general negativity bias—this atom describes when and why the normally powerful asymmetry (bad news outweighs good news) stops applying to a specific target (one's own side) under a specific condition (high polarization + high out-group threat).
- Evidence: "The negativity bias, which normally causes voters to aggressively punish lying, is suppressed. Because the extreme anchor is generating massive amounts of moral outrage, the in-group politician's routine lies are reclassified not as moral failings, but as instrumental competence."
- Implications:
    - Ordinary accountability mechanisms for dishonesty can be switched off selectively, by in-group membership, rather than uniformly.

### Atom 008: Voters Trade Off Democratic Norms and Honesty for Partisan Victory Under High Polarization

- Kind: claim
- Statement: A significant portion of the electorate will knowingly choose a candidate who shares their partisan goals but behaves dishonestly or undemocratically over an opposing candidate who is honest and adheres to democratic norms, and this trade-off intensifies as affective polarization rises.
- Scope & Conditions: Based on Milan Svolik's experimental research on democratic backsliding, using forced-choice designs. Voters are not shown to forget or fail to notice the dishonesty—they consciously demote honesty to a secondary priority.
- Evidence: "A highly significant portion of the electorate is perfectly willing to trade off democratic principles and basic honesty for partisan interests… When affective polarization is high, the perceived cost of letting the opposing party win vastly outweighs the moral cost of voting for a manipulative, dishonest, or corrupt in-group candidate."
- Implications:
    - Democratic backsliding can proceed through informed, deliberate voter choice rather than deception or ignorance.

### Atom 009: Scandal Spillover Splits Into Contagion or Contrast Depending on Polarization Level

- Kind: distinction
- Statement: A political scandal produces one of two opposite trust effects on other politicians: in low-polarization environments it spreads distrust to the whole political class (contagion), while in highly polarized, rivalrous, multilevel environments it can instead increase trust in the scandal-maker's opponents (contrast).
- Scope & Conditions: Demonstrated experimentally using the UK's "Partygate" scandal and its effect on trust in rival Scottish Parliament politicians—Westminster's norm violation increased, rather than contaminated, trust in the opposing tier of government.
- Evidence: "Severe norm violations at one level of government did not cause a contagion of distrust toward the rival politicians. Instead, because the Westminster leader was acting so egregiously, trust and approval for the opposing Scottish politicians actively increased."
- Implications:
    - The same scandal can either damage or repair the reputations of unrelated politicians depending purely on the surrounding polarization level, not on anything those other politicians did.

### Atom 010: Collective Moral Licensing Lowers the Ethical Bar Applied to One's Own Political Side

- Kind: mechanism
- Statement: Voters who identify as the moral resistance to a perceived extreme threat acquire a collective sense of moral superiority ("moral credentials") that then licenses them to tolerate lies, manipulation, or corruption from their own representatives that they would otherwise condemn.
- Scope & Conditions: An application of individual-level moral licensing (where a past good deed licenses a later ethical lapse) at the collective, partisan-identity level. Documented as providing cover even for "apex corruption"—malfeasance at the highest levels of government.
- Evidence: "Because the voters feel their overarching goal—defeating the extreme leader—is indisputably righteous, they extend moral licensing to their own politicians, tolerating lies, manipulation, clientelism, or hypocrisy that they would otherwise fiercely condemn."
- Implications:
    - Moral self-certainty about the righteousness of a cause can itself become the mechanism that permits corruption within that cause.

### Atom 011: Political Elites Strategically Deploy Negative Representation Because the Ecosystem Rewards It

- Kind: mechanism
- Statement: Elected officials increasingly practise "negative representation"—defining themselves by opposing and attacking the rival party rather than by substantive policy work—because media coverage, small-donor fundraising, and elite peer pressure all reward attack-focused positioning even though the general public says it prefers substantive debate.
- Scope & Conditions: Term and research from Mia Costa's "How Politicians Polarize." The incentive is structural (media/donor/elite ecosystem), not a simple mismatch between what politicians want and what voters want—voters dislike the rhetoric but the ecosystem rewards it anyway.
- Evidence: "While the mass public actually dislikes negative, attacking rhetoric and strongly prefers politicians to focus on substantive policy solutions, politicians continue to polarize because the broader political ecosystem heavily and predictably rewards negative representation."
- Implications:
    - Politicians can rationally keep polarizing even while accurately believing voters dislike it, because the electoral and financial incentives point the other way.

### Atom 012: Contrast-Driven Voter Forgiveness Erodes Elections as a Mechanism of Policy Accountability

- Kind: claim
- Statement: Because politicians can rely on voters excusing lies and policy failures when contrasted against a frightening alternative, elections lose their function as referenda on governing performance and become tribal roll calls instead, removing the incentive to govern honestly or effectively.
- Scope & Conditions: Presented as the downstream, systemic consequence of the contrast effect, affective polarization, and moral licensing operating together over repeated election cycles—not a standalone mechanism.
- Evidence: "Because politicians know definitively that voters will excuse their lies, manipulations, and legislative failures as long as they are contrasted against a terrifying alternative, there is little incentive to govern effectively or honestly."
- Implications:
    - The erosion is self-reinforcing: less policy accountability produces more room for elite negative-representation strategies, which further entrenches contrast-driven forgiveness.
