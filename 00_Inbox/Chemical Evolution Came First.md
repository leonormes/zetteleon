It was a continuous process of evolution, not a later spark acting on a static object. The moment self-replicating systems emerged, they were subject to selection and began evolving. This chemical evolution led directly and seamlessly into the biological evolution of life as we recognise it.

## **Chemical Evolution Came First 🧪**

Before true cells, there was a period of **chemical evolution**. In the primordial soup, various molecules formed, but only some were stable. Among these, certain systems (like RNA, according to the "RNA World" hypothesis) became capable of making copies of themselves.  
This kicked off a form of natural selection. Molecules that were more stable and replicated faster and more accurately, outcompeting others for the necessary building blocks, became more common. This wasn't life, but it was evolution in action on a chemical level.

## **The Protocell Transition 🌱**

The formation of a **protocell**—enclosing that self-replicating chemistry within a membrane—was the critical transition from chemical to **biological evolution**. This wasn't a single, miraculous event. It was the result of the physical properties of molecules like lipids, which naturally form spheres in water.  
From the moment the first protocell managed to replicate itself and pass its traits to its offspring, it was evolving. The billions of years weren't a waiting period; they *were* the period of evolution. Early protocells competed fiercely:

* Any cell with a slightly better membrane for grabbing resources would thrive.  
* Any cell that developed a more efficient way to use energy would replicate faster.  
* Any cell that could withstand environmental stress better would survive.

This relentless process of variation and selection over hundreds of millions of years gradually refined these simple protocells, leading to the emergence of the **Last Universal Common Ancestor (LUCA)**, the organism from which all life on Earth today descends. The "spark" wasn't a separate event; it was the entire, continuous process of self-replication meeting natural selection.
