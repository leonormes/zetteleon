## Cooperation Thesis—What's Left Over

%%[refines:: [[LLM - Cooperation Thesis]]]%%

%%[relates:: [[Society]]]%%

> [!question] Research question
> How much of politics and law is explained by cooperation problems—and what's left over?

> [!summary] Short answer
> Cooperation problems explain the functional origin and necessity of legal order and administrative governance. What's left over, and what drives much of actual historical turbulence, is the struggle for zero-sum power, the evolved drive for social status, and the pursuit of justice as a moral end in itself.

---

### 1. What the Cooperation Lens Explains (Public Goods Baseline)

- Suppressing free-riding and establishing order. Early groups used norms, gossip and collective punishment ("reverse dominance hierarchies") to restrain bullies and free-riders. Hart's primary rules (bans on murder, theft, violence) do the same job in law.
- Coordination at scale. Shapiro's Planning Theory of Law: law as a system of generalised plans for large, complex societies. Hart's secondary rules (recognition, change, adjudication) are the machinery that lets strangers coordinate.
- Conflict resolution by compromise. Crick and Heywood: politics as conciliating group interests through dialogue, bargaining and institutions rather than force.

---

### 2. What's Left Over (Blind Spots)

If politics and law were only about cooperation, societies would look like positive-sum engineering projects. Four domains resist that picture.

#### A. Power, Domination and Coercion (Zero-sum)

- Law as consolidated force (Ian Dunt): law often legitimised brute force—land grabs became titles, enslavement became property.
- Who gets what, when, how (Lasswell). Marxists: law as superstructure enforcing ruling-class interests. Radical feminists: "the personal is political".
- Critical Legal Studies and Foucault: law is not neutral; power works through a "microphysics" of disciplinary institutions (prisons, hospitals, schools).
- Weber: the state is defined by its monopoly on legitimate force, not by cooperative intent.

#### B. Status Competition and Machiavellian Drives

- Machiavellian Intelligence hypothesis (Simler & Hanson, _The Elephant in the Brain_): big brains evolved partly to compete for status, prestige and dominance.
- Hidden motives: much political discourse is status signalling and coalition jockeying, where one person's gain is another's loss.

#### C. Norms, Rights and Moral Integrity

- Rights as trumps (Dworkin, "law as integrity"): rights cannot be overridden just to advance collective goals.
- Justice prior to efficiency (Rawls, "justice as fairness"). Lord Denning: the aims of law—order and justice—often conflict.

#### D. Ideology, Sacrality and Us Vs Them

- Moral foundations (Haidt): loyalty, authority, sanctity. Symbols and doctrines get sacralised; movements become Durkheimian rituals.
- Charismatic and totalitarian politics (Minogue, Kershaw): "political moralism" and charismatic authority turn politics into an all-encompassing crusade that erases the private/state boundary.
