The article explains executive function: the set of mental skills used to set goals, plan, solve problems, manage emotions, sustain attention and adapt when circumstances change. It identifies three core skills: working memory, cognitive flexibility and inhibitory control. [my.clevelandclinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/articles/executive-function)

## The Three Skills

- Working memory is holding and integrating information over time—for example, updating your view of a news story as new details emerge. The article says it develops through childhood and adolescence, peaks around the early 30s, and can begin declining from about age 35. [my.clevelandclinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/articles/executive-function)
- Cognitive flexibility is switching approaches, tasks or perspectives when needed. It supports multitasking, empathy and abandoning a plan that is not working. [my.clevelandclinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/articles/executive-function)
- Inhibitory control is managing attention, thoughts, emotions and impulses—such as ignoring office noise during a conversation or pausing rather than reacting in anger. [my.clevelandclinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/articles/executive-function)

## What Can Affect it

Executive functions are associated with the brain's frontal lobe. Difficulties are referred to as executive dysfunction and can occur with ADHD and autism, as well as brain injury or degenerative conditions such as dementia and Alzheimer's disease. [my.clevelandclinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/articles/executive-function)

The page also notes that everyday factors—including stress, loneliness, inadequate sleep and insufficient exercise—can impair executive functioning, even without an underlying neurological diagnosis. [my.clevelandclinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/articles/executive-function)

## Assessment and Improvement

A clinician, often a neurologist, may assess executive functioning through a neurological examination and targeted tasks; the Stroop test is one example used to measure inhibitory control. [my.clevelandclinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/articles/executive-function)

Potential supports discussed include:

- Computerised cognitive training, with evidence of benefit for some children with learning disabilities
- Neurofeedback, where early research suggests possible flexibility benefits
- Mindfulness or meditation, which may help inhibitory control
- Regular activity that combines physical movement with real-time thinking and decision-making, such as team sports [my.clevelandclinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/articles/executive-function)

The important practical caveat is that benefits from training tend to fade after the activity stops, so ongoing practice and sustainable routines matter more than a short-term "fix." [my.clevelandclinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/articles/executive-function)
