## Cooperation Thesis—Origins of Politics, Law and Society

%%[refines:: [[Society]]]%%

> [!summary] Verdict
> "Politics, law and society trace back to solving the cooperation problem" is a strong lens but a weak thesis. Treat it as a research question, not a conclusion.
>
> Working question: _How much of politics and law is explained by cooperation problems—and what's left over?_

---

### 1. Starting Point: the "Society" Note

The [[Society]] note builds a chain of four steps:

1. Tragedy of the Commons: individual interest ("Me") versus collective interest ("Us").
2. Moral machinery: emotions such as empathy, guilt and indignation stabilise cooperation before formal law exists (Greene).
3. Law and the state: property disputes need an impartial adjudicator, which leads to a social contract (Locke and Hobbes).
4. Money: a technology for peaceful exchange between strangers.
5. The modern tragedy: tribal moralities clash ("Us vs Them"), which calls for a "metamorality" and a shift to manual-mode reasoning (Greene).

#### Audit of the Note

| Claim | Status | Issue |
|---|---|---|
| Greene's Me/Us, Us/Them, automatic vs manual mode | ✅ Faithful |—|
| Social norms cheaper than money (Ariely) | ✅ Fair |—|
| Locke _and_ Hobbes argued for the impartial judge | ❌ Conflated | The impartial judge and pre-existing property are Locke. The lawless, insecure state of nature is Hobbes, for whom property only exists once the sovereign creates it. They disagree on the very point cited. |
| Need for a judge "gave rise to" government | ⚠️ Category error | The social contract is a justification for government, not a historical account of its origins. Evidence on early states points more to surplus, taxation and coercion (Scott). States monopolise force (Weber) rather than replacing it. |
| Money emerged for trade between strangers | ⚠️ Contested | This is the "barter myth". The anthropological record (Graeber, _Debt_) suggests credit came first, and that money was often created by states for tax. "Trade instead of fighting" is Montesquieu's _doux commerce_ idea, which is plausible but disputed. |
| Metamorality presented as neutral, pragmatic reasoning | ⚠️ Omission | Greene's manual mode is specifically utilitarianism ("deep pragmatism"). This is the most contested part of his argument (critics include Berker and Haidt). "Inherently tribal" overstates the evidence. |
| Tragedy of the Commons implies ruin without formal institutions | ⚠️ Qualified | Ostrom showed that communities often govern commons successfully without the state or privatisation. This _supports_ the moral-machinery point but undercuts the idea that ruin is inevitable. Hardin's case is really an open-access resource that nobody manages. |

### 2. Institutional Redesign across Moral Frameworks

Position taken in the conversation:

- Use Ostrom rather than Greene as the design basis. Nested, many-centred governance with locally made rules manages moral disagreement without first requiring everyone to accept one shared moral currency.
- Deliberative mini-publics. Ireland's citizens' assemblies are the strongest evidence that people can reach principled compromise across moral camps.
- Agree on process, not values. Agreement on evidence standards, appeal routes and sunset clauses is achievable; agreement on values is not.

> [!warning] Uncertainty
> Mini-publics work well on specific questions, but their recommendations are weakly and inconsistently adopted by governments.

### 3. Thesis Pressure-test

#### Why the Lens is Legitimate

It's an established tradition, not a pop-science frame:

- Hobbes, and later game theory (Axelrod, Binmore): states as solutions to prisoner's dilemmas.
- Ostrom: empirical governance of shared resources.
- Henrich, Bowles & Gintis: cultural evolution of norms, punishment and institutions.

#### Why It's Risky as a Thesis

1. It can't be disproved as stated. Almost anything can be described as a cooperation problem after the fact.
2. Rival explanations:
   - Domination: institutions built for extraction (Scott; Olson's "stationary bandit").
   - War between groups: competition between groups forges cooperation within them (Turchin).
   - Religion: moralising "Big Gods" come before large-scale cooperation (Norenzayan).
   - Contingency: early societies experimented freely, with no single driving problem (Graeber & Wengrow).
3. Cooperation for whom? Cartels, mafias and apartheid are all effective cooperation. Solving cooperation says nothing about whether the result is just.

#### Falsification Condition

> This thesis would be wrong if the foundational institutions of early societies functioned primarily not to facilitate mutual benefit, but to systematically extract wealth and consolidate power for a ruling elite.

Changes from the first draft:

- "Designed" became "functioned primarily", because intent can't be recovered from the record and most institutions aren't designed.
- "Primarily" adds a threshold, because of the stationary-bandit objection: an elite that extracts over the long term has a reason to supply order and public goods, so finding extraction doesn't by itself disprove cooperation.

### 4. Case Study: Taxation and the Domesday Book

Leon's argument: taxation fits the domination model better than the cooperation model. The Domesday Book (1086) was a wealth inventory for taxing a newly conquered population, so taxation was imposed from above rather than agreed.

Critique:

1. It isn't an origin. Domesday is a conquest state taking over an existing state. It reused Anglo-Saxon tax machinery: the geld, assessed in hides (a unit of taxable land) through shires and hundreds (administrative districts). That system grew from payments to Viking raiders and to fund mercenaries. It tests conquest, not origins.
2. Its purpose was mixed. Historians debate whether it was mainly for tax, for recording who held land after the Norman takeovers (dispute resolution), or for funding defence against a feared Danish invasion (it was commissioned in 1085). ⚠️ _Specifics need checking against the literature._
3. Key insight: if Domesday was partly a record of land tenure, the same document is both an extraction tool and Locke's impartial judge. Dual function is a better finding than "taxation was domination".

Better evidence for origins: Mesopotamian and Egyptian grain taxation (Scott, _Against the Grain_). Charles Tilly, _War Making and State Making as Organized Crime_, argues that extraction becomes state-building.

## Verdict: Accept it as a Consistency Check, not as Confirmation

That verification shows my answer matches your sources. It doesn't show the sources are right. If "Society" was built from Greene, Haidt and Boehm, and I drew on the same books, then two agreeing LLMs amount to one source checked twice. The verifier is also uniformly positive ("highly accurate", "maps perfectly", "exact progression"), which is itself a flag.

### Where it Overstates

1. "Exact progression." My five layers were a teaching sequence, not an established historical order. Reputation and punishment likely evolved together, and kinship and reciprocity overlap. Don't record them as a strict timeline.
2. Costly punishment "dramatically increases cooperation." That's true in Western lab games (Fehr & Gächter). But cross-cultural studies (Herrmann, Thöni & Gächter, 2008) found "antisocial punishment" in some societies, where people punish _cooperators_, and cooperation gains shrink or vanish. The layer is real; the word "dramatically" is culture-dependent.
3. "Sensitive to being watched." Reputation effects are solid. But the popular "watching eyes" findings, where pictures of eyes increase honesty, have replicated poorly. Don't let that evidence carry the claim.
4. Liberty/oppression foundation. This comes from Haidt's Moral Foundations Theory, which builds on Boehm, not from Boehm himself. The theory's measurement and cross-cultural validity are actively contested. Treat it as useful but provisional.
5. "Thoroughly supported." Boehm is influential, but critics argue forager egalitarianism varied more than he allows. I flagged this last time, and the verifier dropped the caveat rather than checking it.

- The free-rider framing and the Tragedy of the Commons as one instance of it: solid.
- The weapons-and-coalitions mechanism for reverse dominance: a fair summary of Boehm, via Haidt.
- Dominance as a separate kernel from cheating: supported, and this is the part most worth keeping.

## Verdict: Nearly. Swap the Label, Keep the Idea

As a layman's shorthand, "Tragedy of the Commons" is defensible. But it's one _example_ of the kernel, not the kernel itself. The more accurate first principle is the free-rider problem, also called the cooperation problem:

> Working together creates a surplus, and that surplus makes it tempting to take the benefit without paying the cost.

### Why the Swap Matters

- The Tragedy of the Commons is one narrow case: overusing a shared resource that nobody manages.
- The free-rider problem covers the whole family: dodging the hunt but eating the meat, skipping sentry duty, not paying tax, breaking a promise. The commons is just the most famous illustration.
- From an evolutionary perspective, this is the actual puzzle biologists ask: why would a creature pay a cost to help others, when cheats who don't pay seem to do better?

### How Evolution Solved It, in Layers

Each layer is a stronger anti-cheating device than the one before:

1. Kinship. Help relatives, because they share your genes.
2. Reciprocity. "I'll help you if you help me."
3. Reputation. Others are watching, and gossip spreads.
4. Punishment. Cheats get shamed, excluded or hit, which is where righteous indignation comes from.
5. Institutions. Law, money and the state make punishment and reputation work among strangers, at scale.

That's your "solidified around" story, and it's fairly well supported.

### One addition for First Principles

There's a second evolutionary kernel that the cooperation story alone misses: dominance. Our ape relatives live in bully-led hierarchies. Anthropologist Christopher Boehm argues that early humans were egalitarian only because the group formed coalitions to suppress would-be bullies.

So a fuller first principle is two tensions, not one:

- Me vs Us: free-riding.
- Strong vs Many: domination.

Much of politics sits where these meet. Your Domesday example is really the second tension, not the first.

How confident I am: the free-rider framing is mainstream. Boehm's argument is influential but still debated.
