## Shuhari—synthesis Of Six Video Distillations

> [!warning] Provenance
> Third-hand material: video → Gemini distillation → Claude synthesis. Nobody in this chain checked the transcripts directly. Gemini's _Status_ labels and signal-to-noise ratings are its own judgements, not evidence—treat this page as a map of what the sources claim, not of what is true.

### Verdict

- One idea survives across all six: automate the fundamentals under a trusted model _before_ personalising them. Everything else—timelines, repetition counts, neuroscience citations—is decoration of very uneven quality.
- Worth keeping: V5 (practitioner account, 85/15) and V6 (structured, 80/20). V4 is skimmable. V1–V3 add nothing that V5 and V6 don't already cover.
- The shared blind spot: every one of the six distillations flags the same dependency—the model being copied must be correct, and the learner must be able to tell when a repetition is wrong. None of the videos explains how to secure either.

### The Model, Briefly

| Stage | Kanji | Literal sense | What the learner does |
|---|---|---|---|
| Shu | 守 | protect / obey | Reproduce the form exactly; no personal variation |
| Ha | 破 | break / detach | Vary, compare against other styles, extract the principles beneath the forms |
| Ri | 離 | leave / separate | Act from internalised principle; new forms emerge rather than being planned |

Origins (not covered accurately by the videos): the phrasing is usually attributed to a verse by the 16th-century tea master Sen no Rikyū and was popularised by the Edo-period tea master Kawakami Fuhaku; martial arts (notably aikido) adopted it later, and Alistair Cockburn carried it into agile software circles. V1's "originating in Japanese martial arts" is therefore loose.

Traditional geometry: often drawn as concentric circles—Shu inside Ha, both inside Ri—rather than as a staircase. That framing sides with V5's non-linear reading against the timeline-driven videos.

### Where the Sources Converge

- Fundamentals before innovation—all six.
- Premature innovation cements bad habits—V2 ([04:23](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Y66uMiInbnQ&t=263)), V4 ([02:21](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9pTUN_NH2SM&t=141)), V6 ([02:41](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vqfVpoOqByE&t=161)); V3 gives the cultural version (speed, visibility, burnout).
- Ha is understanding, not rejection—reached through contrast and variation: other styles (V2 [11:45](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Y66uMiInbnQ&t=705), V6 [04:40](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vqfVpoOqByE&t=280)) or deliberately varied timing, rhythm and context (V4 [03:59](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9pTUN_NH2SM&t=239)).
- Ri emerges; it is not targeted—V6 ([08:17](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vqfVpoOqByE&t=497)), with V2 and V3 attaching it to flow-state neuroscience.
- Knowing ≠ having—V2's "eternal student trap" ([10:07](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Y66uMiInbnQ&t=607)) and V5's _karada de oboeru_, learning with the body ([04:14](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0d6fndfodxU&t=254)), make the same point from opposite ends: collecting techniques without internalising them produces the illusion of mastery.

### Where They Diverge

#### Linear Staircase Vs Cycle

- Sequential, time-boxed stages: V2, V4, V6.
- Cyclical / non-linear: V1—masters return to Shu for each new sub-skill ([02:34](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mnSEoonKffY&t=154)); V5—practitioners cycle constantly, and the neat three-step doctrine is largely a Western systematisation of something that happens unconsciously in Japanese practice ([01:08](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0d6fndfodxU&t=68), [02:58](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0d6fndfodxU&t=178)).

#### Timelines—the Disagreement _is_ the Finding

| Source | Prescribed Shu duration |
|---|---|
| V4 | 2–8 weeks (then 1–6 months of Ha) |
| V1 | ≥ 6 months with one teacher/system, unmodified |
| V6 | 6–12 months minimum |
| V2 | "Years, not months"; cites 1–3 years imitation, then 3–7 years experimentation |
| V5 | Rejects discrete phases |
| V3 | None given |

The same stage spans roughly two weeks to three years. That spread is evidence the durations are narrative devices. The real variables—skill complexity and feedback quality—are the ones none of the sources quantifies.

#### Domain Scope

- V5 is explicit that its argument concerns procedural / motor skill; it leaves abstract cognitive work unaddressed.
- V6's own caveat: rote copying suits motor skills better than abstract problem-solving.
- V2 treats martial arts and business as having identical learning curves (Gemini flags this as inaccurate).

#### Gemini's Labels Are not Consistent

The same claim receives different _Status_ labels in different distillations—Ri is "Plausible" in V1 but "Established" in V4; Ha is "Established" in V1, V4 and V6 but "Plausible" in V2 and V3. The labels were assigned per video, not cross-checked. Do not import them as confidence levels.

### Gaps Every Source Shares

1. Model selection—how to choose who or what to copy. Flagged in V1, V3 and V6; noted by Gemini as an unexamined assumption in V2 and V5.
2. Feedback during Shu—how to detect a wrong repetition before it is automated. Flagged explicitly in V2, V4 and V6.
3. Transition criterion—how to tell Shu is finished. Flagged in V3 and V4.
4. The motivational cliff—Shu is where most learners quit from boredom or frustration. Flagged in V1 only, though it applies to all.

### Pressure-test (Claude's addition—not from the Videos)

The videos converge on strict, invariant repetition in Shu, and Gemini repeatedly marks this as consistent with the deliberate-practice literature. That support is weaker than it looks:

- Ericsson cuts against the videos, not for them. Deliberate practice (Ericsson, Krampe & Tesch-Römer, 1993) means teacher-designed activities _with immediate feedback_—exactly the component the videos omit. "Copy an online expert" is closer to naive practice.
- Deliberate practice explains less than the genre implies. Macnamara et al.'s 2014 meta-analysis attributed roughly 12% of performance variance to deliberate practice overall, with large differences by domain (high in games and music, very low in professions).
- Invariant repetition may be the wrong default. The contextual interference effect (Shea & Morgan, 1979) and variability-of-practice work (Schmidt's schema theory, 1975) suggest blocked repetition improves _in-session_ performance while varied or interleaved practice improves _retention and transfer_. Massed practice feels effective—a fluency illusion. Schöllhorn's group goes further, arguing that involuntary variation in technique may itself drive learning ([Aragones et al.](https://bibliographie.ub.uni-mainz.de/catalog/publication-104353)).
- Possible reconciliation: the Challenge Point Framework (Guadagnoli & Lee, 2004) holds that optimal difficulty depends on the learner's level—novices benefit from _lower_ interference, more advanced learners from more. On that reading, Shu → Ha is a dial, not a gate.
- Parallel model: Fitts & Posner's stages (cognitive → associative → autonomous, 1967) describe roughly the same arc as V4's declarative → procedural → creative mapping. The difference is that Fitts & Posner _describe_ what happens to the learner; Shuhari _prescribes_ what the learner should do ("don't vary yet"). The prescription is the contestable part.
