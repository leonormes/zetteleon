Reasoning:

These strategies are based on an understanding of how the ADHD brain functions, and recognises the paradoxical tendencies associated with the condition, which were outlined in the previous response. For example, individuals with ADHD often:

- Struggle with executive functions, such as planning, prioritising and organising.
- Have difficulty maintaining focus and attention.
- Experience time differently, often lacking a sense of the passage of time.
- Are prone to boredom and seek out novelty and stimulation.
- Are often highly creative and have a tendency toward intuitive problem solving.
- Can hyperfocus on areas of interest.

By adapting GTD to suit these tendencies, and leveraging their strengths, it becomes possible to create a system that truly works with the individual, rather than against them.

Next Steps:

It sounds like you're experiencing anticipatory resistance, where your brain builds up the act of starting as a mental hurdle. This is often a mix of perfectionism, fear of failure, or even resistance to discomfort, which can lead to procrastination. Here's what's likely happening in your mind:

1. Pomodoro as a Trigger: The Pomodoro technique has become associated with the discomfort of focusing on tasks, even though it helps once you start. Your brain is trying to avoid that discomfort, magnifying the effort it thinks is involved.
2. Fear of Commitment: Starting a Pomodoro may feel like committing to "serious work," which can spark a sense of pressure. You may feel overwhelmed by the expectation of productivity.
3. All-or-Nothing Thinking: Your brain might be framing starting a Pomodoro as an all-or-nothing decision. It assumes you'll need to be perfect or fully engaged, which feels heavy.
4. Task Aversion: If you're avoiding a task itself, the Pomodoro becomes guilty by association. Starting it reminds you of the task you'd rather not do.
