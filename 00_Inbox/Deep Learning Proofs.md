The "Direct-Then-Drill" approach is a method for integrating direct practice and drills by using them as alternating stages in a larger cycle of learning. It addresses the limitations of both approaches when used in isolation. Direct practice involves working on a whole skill in the context in which it will eventually be used. Drills involve isolating and practicing specific components of a skill.

The Limitations of Isolated Direct Practice and Drills:

- Isolated Direct Practice: Direct practice alone may not sufficiently improve specific areas of a skill, as it does not allow for targeted improvement. You may not be able to address specific weaknesses or components of the skill, because your attention is spread across many different aspects of the task.
- Isolated Drills: Drills in isolation can lead to a disconnect from the overall skill. The individual components may improve, but the application of those components may not transfer well to real-world situations. There may be limited context for how these components relate to each other or the overall goal.

The Direct-Then-Drill Approach as an Iterative Cycle:

The Direct-Then-Drill approach resolves this by creating an iterative cycle that alternates between the two. The three stages are:

1. Direct Practice: The first step is to engage in direct practice by working on the whole skill in the context in which it will be used. This establishes the overall goal and identifies the areas that need improvement. It connects your learning activities to the context in which the skills will eventually be used.

- In the context of mathematical proofs, this would involve attempting to write complete proofs, tackling problems from the "Book of Proof". It involves attempting to apply the knowledge you've gained from reading, which allows you to see where your understanding is incomplete, and where you need to target more focused practice.

1. Analysis and Isolation (Drill): This is where you isolate specific subskills or areas that are either rate-determining steps in your performance, or components that are difficult to improve during direct practice. Drills allow you to simplify a complex skill, enabling you to focus your cognitive resources on a single aspect.

- In the case of mathematical proofs, this could involve focusing on a specific proof technique (e.g., direct proof, proof by contrapositive), focusing on understanding and using definitions, or specific skills like algebraic manipulations within a proof. It allows you to break down complex tasks into more manageable parts, and master those parts.

1. Integration: You then return to direct practice and integrate what you have learned. You apply the improved components in the broader context of the skill, allowing you to build connections between the separate parts. This step also serves as a check to determine whether your drill was useful. If the drill is not effective, then this can be identified and addressed, allowing for continuous improvement.

- For mathematical proofs, you would work on problems that require using your improved skills in combination with other concepts, testing whether the isolated practice translates into improved overall performance.

Key Benefits of the Direct-Then-Drill Approach:

- Contextualised Learning: By beginning with direct practice, you keep your learning activities tied to the context where you will be using the skills. You avoid the problem of abstract learning that does not transfer to practical situations.
- Targeted Improvement: By identifying specific weaknesses, you are able to apply drills to the areas where you need the most improvement, leading to greater overall effectiveness. This accelerates learning by focusing on the rate-determining steps and subskills.
- Efficient Use of Cognitive Resources: Drills allow you to dedicate cognitive resources to single aspects of a complex skill, rather than spreading your effort too thinly.
- Improved Transfer: By integrating the improved subskills back into the larger skill, you develop a more cohesive understanding of the overall process. This prevents the problem of isolated improvements that cannot be applied in a real situation.
- Iterative Refinement: The approach is iterative, which means you can continuously evaluate and improve the drills you use. It lets you identify what isn't working as a drill, and iterate on it until you find a way that helps you.
- Flexibility: The frequency of the cycle can be adjusted as needed. In the early stages of learning, you can cycle between direct practice and drills more frequently, and in the later stages, the cycle can be longer, as your skills are more developed.
- Mindful Practice: Drills move from being "meaningless drudgery" to being purposeful and important. When they are directed by the learner, the drills allow you to enhance the learning process by accelerating the specific aspects that you find difficult.

Application to Learning Mathematical Proofs:

1. Direct Practice: Attempt to write a complete mathematical proof.
2. Analysis and Isolation (Drill): Identify a rate-determining step in your proof (e.g., understanding logical connectives, applying definitions, using a specific proof technique, algebraic manipulation). Create a drill to focus on that weakness (e.g., create a time slice where you focus on doing algebraic manipulations, or work through problems that focus only on using definitions).
3. Integration: Return to the direct practice of attempting to write complete proofs, and apply the improved component to the larger problem.
4. Evaluate: Evaluate your success to see if this drill was the correct one for the bottleneck. If you are still struggling, then you should go back to analysis and isolation and find a different rate-determining step.

Conclusion:

The Direct-Then-Drill approach provides a balanced and effective way to integrate direct practice and drills. It ensures that your learning activities remain connected to the desired outcome while also allowing you to develop the specific skills necessary for mastery. By alternating between these two stages, you can improve your overall skill level much more effectively than if you were to use either approach in isolation. This method is particularly useful for complex skills, such as mathematical proofs, where both context and targeted improvement are important for success.

The declarative and procedural learning systems are two distinct pathways the brain uses to store information in long-term memory, each with unique characteristics and functions. They operate differently and contribute to a more comprehensive understanding of learned material when used together.

Declarative Learning System:

- Conscious and Explicit: The declarative system is closely linked to working memory, making you aware of what you are learning. You can "declare" the information you are acquiring. It involves the conscious, intentional memorisation of facts, concepts, and rules.
- Step-by-Step Learning: This system learns best through step-by-step explanations and logical procedures. It is well-suited for understanding definitions, facts, and sequences of steps in problem-solving.
- Explicit Recall: Information learned through the declarative system is retrieved through explicit recall. You must consciously access the information to use it. It is the more humdrum of the two systems.
- Examples of Declarative Learning:
  - Memorising historical dates.
  - Learning a new vocabulary word.
  - Understanding the rules of grammar in a new language.
  - Following a step-by-step procedure to solve a mathematical problem.
  - Learning the steps to serve a tennis ball before practice begins.
- Limitations: While the declarative system is effective for initial learning, it is less efficient for automatic, intuitive execution of skills, and more susceptible to being forgotten without retrieval. It is slower to retrieve from, and lacks the natural fluency that comes from procedural memory.

Procedural Learning System:

- Unconscious and Implicit: The procedural system operates largely outside conscious awareness. You are often not consciously aware of when you are learning through this system. It focuses on the development of skills and habits through repetition and practice.
- Intuitive Learning: The procedural system learns by intuitively feeling out patterns rather than through explicit, step-by-step explanations. It is about developing a feel for what works, and this is built through practice. It excels at pattern recognition.
- Automatic Execution: Information stored procedurally results in automatic and smooth execution of skills and tasks. Once a skill is proceduralised, it requires less conscious effort and becomes more natural and fluent.
- Examples of Procedural Learning:
  - Riding a bicycle.
  - Serving a tennis ball after repeated practice.
  - Typing on a keyboard without looking.
  - Speaking a language fluently.
  - Quickly solving a Rubik's Cube.
  - Instinctively knowing that 7 and 5 sum to 12\.
  - Quickly assessing a situation and responding to it.
- Strengths: Procedural learning is robust and is retained much longer than declarative knowledge, and also allows for fast reactions in stressful situations. It is less prone to forgetting.
- Limitations: It can be difficult to explain how one performs a skill learned procedurally. It also does not involve conscious memorisation of individual steps, so some aspects can be difficult to verbalise.

Interplay Between the Two Systems:

- Complementary Roles: Both systems work together, with the declarative system often initiating a skill and the procedural system taking over as it becomes more automatic. The declarative system is important for getting started, while the procedural system is what helps you execute with ease and intuition.
- Enhanced Learning: Learning through both systems results in richer and deeper understanding of the material. When both are employed, there are stronger neural links formed in long-term memory.
- Example of Integrated Learning:
  - When learning a new language, the declarative system helps in learning vocabulary and grammar rules. The procedural system develops fluency and automaticity through practice and conversation.
  - In learning to serve a tennis ball, the declarative system guides the initial movements and steps, and the procedural system takes over after practice, resulting in smooth, natural serves.
  - In solving mathematical problems, declarative knowledge of rules is combined with procedural knowledge of how to execute those steps. Procedural intuition helps in choosing which formulas and techniques to apply, which can help with problem-solving speed.
  - In math and science, the declarative system is used initially, by following step-by-step procedures, and then key examples are internalized to develop fast procedural skills and intuition.

Key Differences Summarised:

| Feature | Declarative System | Procedural System |
|:---- |:---- |:---- |
| Consciousness | Conscious and explicit | Unconscious and implicit |
| Learning Style | Step-by-step explanations | Intuitive pattern recognition |
| Recall | Explicit recall | Automatic execution |
| Memory | Can be more quickly forgotten | More robust and long-lasting |
| Focus | Facts, concepts, rules | Skills, habits, automatic procedures |
| Speed | Slower retrieval | Fast and efficient |
| Verbalisation | Can be easily articulated | Difficult to explain |

Implications for Learning:

- Utilise Both Pathways: It is beneficial to use both declarative and procedural systems when learning something new. This approach allows for a deep and robust knowledge base.
- Practice for Procedural Skills: Skills that require automatic and intuitive performance are best developed through repeated practice, which strengthens the procedural system.
- Start Declaratively: Begin with understanding the facts, concepts, and procedures declaratively, and then transition to procedural learning through practice and application.
- Internalise Key Examples: Internalising key examples can help the development of procedural intuition.
- Focus on application: Ensure that the skills being learned are applied in a practical context, so that the declarative can be translated into procedural learning.
- Balance Theory and Practice: In learning complex topics, a balance of theoretical knowledge and practical application should be maintained. This can lead to a more complete understanding that encompasses knowledge of both "what" and "how".
- Real-World application: Ensure there is a connection between learning activities and the context where the skills will eventually be applied.
- Be mindful of the black box: Be aware that procedural learning happens largely outside of conscious awareness, and that the process cannot be directly influenced.

Conclusion:

The declarative and procedural learning systems are complementary and vital for effective learning. While the declarative system helps in acquiring and understanding new information explicitly, the procedural system enables the automatic and intuitive application of that information, and the ability to execute skills without conscious thought. By understanding the distinct characteristics and interplay of these two systems, learners can optimise their study habits, engage in more effective practice, and develop a deeper and more comprehensive understanding of whatever they are learning.
