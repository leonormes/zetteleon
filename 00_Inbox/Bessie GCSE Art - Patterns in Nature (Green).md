## Patterns in Nature—GCSE Art Research (Green Colour Study)

### Why Green?

Bessie has chosen green as her colour focus because humans can perceive more distinct shades of green than any other colour—a strong, examiner-friendly rationale to open her sketchbook with.

- The retina has three types of cone cells (S, M, L), and the M-cones (medium wavelength) and L-cones (long wavelength) both peak close together in the green–yellow-green region of the spectrum (~530–580nm), creating heavy overlap and finer discrimination in that range.
- This gives the visual cortex a stronger, more differentiated signal for green/yellow-green hues than for blue or red, meaning we can tell subtly different greens apart more easily than subtly different blues or reds.
- It's also evolutionary: early primate ancestors lived in dense forest/foliage environments, so being able to spot ripe fruit, edible young leaves, or camouflaged predators/insects against a green backdrop was a survival advantage—this trichromatic colour vision developed alongside a shift to daytime, plant-and-fruit-based diets.
- Green also sits in the middle of the visible spectrum, between blue and red; this positioning is part of why night-vision displays and instrument panels traditionally use green.

Sketchbook tip: this scientific fact makes a great annotation/mind-map on her first research page—it directly justifies the colour choice with evidence, which AQA examiners like to see (linking personal choices to reasoning, not just "I like green").

### What Counts as a "Pattern in Nature" (AQA Context)

AQA's Art & Design pattern topic asks students to explore where shapes, lines, or colours repeat in nature, built from a repeated element (a "motif").

- A motif is the single repeating unit; patterns can be regular (grid-like, tessellating) or organic/irregular (like a paisley shape, itself derived from plant forms).
- Organic shapes (as opposed to geometric shapes) are irregular, curved, and imperfect—think leaves, petals, pebbles, shells—and using them makes artwork feel natural and alive.
- BBC Bitesize's AQA-specific Art and Design revision pages on "Pattern" and "Shape" are worth Bessie reading directly for keywords and examples to quote in her annotations.

### Key Mathematical/Natural Patterns to Draw From

These are the classic, exam-friendly "patterns in nature" themes—all lend themselves beautifully to green plant subjects:

- Fibonacci spirals—proportions following the golden ratio, visible in pinecone scales, unfurling fern fronds, snail shells, and the seed-head spirals of sunflowers.
- Fractals—self-repeating patterns at different scales (zoom in and the pattern looks similar); classic examples are Romanesco cauliflower, fern leaves (each frond looks like a mini version of the whole plant), tree branching, and river deltas.
- Symmetry and radial repetition—seen in flower petal arrangements, leaf venation, and segmented plant structures; useful for compositions built around a central motif.
- Tessellation and cellular repetition—leaf surfaces, moss, lichen, and honeycomb-like plant cell structures under magnification.

Practical drawing subjects (all strongly green): fern fronds (fractal branching), unrolling bracken/fiddleheads (Fibonacci spiral), succulent rosettes (radial symmetry + fractal), cabbage/kale leaves (organic pattern + strong veining), moss and lichen macro textures, ivy and climbing plant tangles, bamboo groves (repetition/rhythm), seaweed and algae forms.

### Artists and Sources to Reference

A strong AQA sketchbook needs artist research alongside primary observational drawing—these give Bessie named practitioners to analyse and link to her own work:

- Ernst Haeckel—19th-century biologist whose lithograph collection _Kunstformen der Natur_ ("Art Forms in Nature", 1899–1904) depicted the symmetry and repeating structure of organisms (radiolarians, jellyfish, plants); hugely influential on Art Nouveau design and a go-to reference for pattern-in-nature GCSE projects.
- Karl Blossfeldt—botanical photographer who took extreme close-up (macro) black-and-white photographs of plant stems, leaves, and buds, revealing architectural, almost sculptural patterns; excellent reference for how magnification reveals pattern.
- Henry Moore—sculptor whose organic, flowing forms echo natural pebbles, bones, and shells; commonly paired with natural-forms sketchbook units at GCSE for shape/form studies.
- Piet Mondrian, Henrique Oliveira, Levi van Veluw—other artists referenced in natural-forms GCSE resource booklets, useful for showing how artists abstract or reconstruct organic forms.
- Look at William Morris-style repeat patterns and paisley motifs too—both are historically derived directly from plant/seed shapes and are textbook examples of organic repeat pattern in design.

### Suggested Sketchbook Structure

1. Mind-map page: "Why Green?"—include the cone-cell/evolution science above as an annotated diagram.
2. Primary observational drawings: ferns, succulents, cabbage leaves, moss—pencil, biro continuous-line, and ink studies, focusing on line, texture, and repeated structure.
3. Macro photography: close-up photos of leaf veins, moss, lichen textures (can be edited/filtered to emphasise green tonal range).
4. Artist research page: Haeckel and Blossfeldt studies with short written analysis, plus a copy/response drawing in her own style.
5. Colour-mixing experiments: mix as many distinct greens as possible (paint/coloured pencil swatches) to visually demonstrate the "humans see more greens" concept—a lovely tie-back to her rationale.
6. Pattern development: take one motif (e.g., a fern frond or fractal spiral) and develop it into a repeating tile/pattern design, moving from observation to design.
7. Final piece planning: combine observational drawing + developed pattern + green colour theory into a final composition concept.
