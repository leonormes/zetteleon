## The New Opposition: Countering Authoritarianism in a Multipolar World

### Part I: Deconstructing the Premise \- Defining the Ideological Landscape

#### Section 1: Introduction

The assertion that both the United States and the Russian Federation are fascist states, and the subsequent question of who, then, opposes a rising global fascist establishment, serves as a powerful entry point into one of the most critical issues of our time: the nature of contemporary authoritarianism and the forces arrayed against it. This query, while resting on premises that require rigorous examination, correctly identifies a palpable sense of democratic backsliding and the rise of illiberal, nationalist politics across the globe. The old certainties of the Cold War, a bipolar ideological struggle between liberal democracy and communism, have given way to a more complex and fragmented geopolitical landscape. Understanding this new era demands a clear-eyed, evidence-based analysis, moving beyond polemical labels to grasp the intricate realities of modern political systems.

This report will address the core of the query by undertaking a three-part investigation. First, it will establish a precise, scholarly framework for understanding fascism. This is a necessary prerequisite, as the term is frequently used as a political pejorative, and a disciplined analysis requires a stable definition grounded in historical and political science scholarship. Second, this report will apply this framework in two detailed case studies, examining the political systems of the United States and the Russian Federation. This analysis will assess the extent to which each nation exhibits characteristics of fascism, authoritarianism, or other forms of governance, thereby testing the foundational assumptions of the initial query. Finally, having clarified the nature of these major powers, the report will map the contours of the actual global opposition to authoritarianism. This involves moving beyond the Cold War paradigm of a single opponent (the USA) to identify the diverse and multi-layered coalition of state and non-state actors that constitute the contemporary counterweights to illiberalism. Through this structured approach, this report aims to provide a comprehensive and nuanced answer, transforming the initial question into an opportunity for a deeper exploration of the global struggle for democracy in the 21st century.

#### Section 2: Fascism \- A Scholarly Framework

To analyze the political character of modern states, one must first establish a clear and rigorous definition of the ideology in question. Fascism, in particular, presents a significant analytical challenge. Its historical manifestations were varied, and in contemporary discourse, the term is often deployed more as a rhetorical weapon to delegitimize opponents than as a precise analytical category.1 This section will navigate these complexities to build a functional, scholarly framework for understanding fascism, distinguishing it from related but distinct political phenomena.

##### 2.1 The Challenge of a Singular Definition

Defining fascism has proven uniquely difficult compared to other major ideologies like liberalism or communism, which have foundational thinkers and canonical texts.3 The term itself has a complex history, originating from the Italian word

_fascio_ (meaning "bundle" or "group") and adopted by Benito Mussolini, though it was already in political circulation before his rise.4 The debate over its meaning is lengthy and unsettled, with some scholars, like Gilbert Allardyce, going so far as to declare fascism an "illusion" and a word that "means virtually nothing".3 This academic skepticism stems from the tendency for definitions to be either too vague to be useful or so specific to the cases of Italy and Germany that they exclude other relevant movements.3

The problem is compounded by the term's political utility as a pejorative. As many scholars note, "fascism" is often used as a "meaningless term of abuse" or a slogan, rarely as a carefully informed argument.1 This is evident in the contemporary geopolitical landscape, where the label is strategically weaponized. For example, the Russian government, which defines fascism as being synonymous with Nazism, leverages its historical victory in World War II to brand itself as the world's preeminent anti-fascist power, while labeling its opponents in Ukraine and elsewhere as "fascists".5 Simultaneously, a growing number of Western scholars and commentators are applying the fascist label to Russia itself, creating what analyst Marlene Laruelle calls a "mirror game" where the term's meaning is fluid and instrumental.7 A similar dynamic exists in the United States, where the debate over applying the "fascist" label to political movements has become a central feature of the political conflict itself, with the choice of definition often reflecting a deeper strategic or philosophical position.2

Despite these challenges, a "new consensus" has emerged among historians and political scientists, moving away from checklists of superficial traits and toward an understanding of fascism's core ideological motivations.3 This evolution in scholarship allows for a more disciplined analysis. It requires distinguishing between the

_substance_ of an ideology and its _form_. Early definitions often focused on the outward expressions of fascism—the colored shirts, the street gangs, the specific symbols.3 Modern scholarship, however, prioritizes the underlying ideological project. The crucial question is not merely whether a movement has a paramilitary wing, but whether that paramilitary wing is dedicated to a revolutionary project of national rebirth. This focus on the ideological core provides a stable benchmark against which contemporary movements can be measured.

##### 2.2 Core Tenets of Historical Fascism

Synthesizing the work of leading scholars such as Robert Paxton, Roger Griffin, and Benito Mussolini himself, a coherent picture of historical fascism's ideological core emerges. It is not merely a collection of policies but a comprehensive worldview, a _Weltanschauung_, that seeks to fundamentally remake society.10

- Palingenetic Ultranationalism: This concept, most famously articulated by Roger Griffin, is widely considered the "fascist minimum" or the core of the ideology. It refers to a revolutionary form of nationalism centered on the myth of a national "rebirth" or _palingenesis_.10 Fascism diagnoses a profound crisis, a state of national decline, humiliation, and victimhood that can only be overcome by destroying the old order and creating a new, purified national community.10 This makes fascism inherently revolutionary, not conservative; it does not seek to preserve the status quo but to overthrow existing systems in favor of an "alternative modernity".3
- The Cult of the Leader and the Masses: Fascism is characterized by a powerful, populist nationalism centered on the cult of a redemptive, "infallible" leader who embodies the national will.11 This leader derives political power from questioning reality, promoting myth and rage, and cultivating a charismatic connection with the masses.11 Unlike traditional authoritarianism, which often seeks to demobilize the public and encourage passivity, fascism is a mass political movement that aims to energize and mobilize the population through spectacular rallies, propaganda, and participation in party-organized channels.12 The goal is to create a unified, indissoluble society where the individual is subsumed into the collective.10
- Ideological Foundations: The fascist worldview is defined by what it opposes as much as by what it supports.
  - Anti-Individualism and Totalitarianism: Fascism explicitly rejects the principles of classical liberalism, which it sees as having atomized society and weakened the state.14 As Mussolini wrote in
    _The Doctrine of Fascism_, "Anti-individualistic, the Fascist conception of life stresses the importance of the State and accepts the individual only in so far as his interests coincide with those of the State".14 The state is conceived as an all-embracing, "totalitarian" entity—a "synthesis and a unit inclusive of all values" outside of which "no human or spiritual values can exist".14
  - Anti-Enlightenment and Anti-Rationalism: Fascism thrives on the irrational, appealing to myth, faith, and will over reason and critical thinking.11 It exhibits a profound disdain for intellectuals and art that do not align with its narrative, seeing them as decadent and corrupting.11 This contempt for truth reflects the despair of followers who feel socially and spiritually adrift, for whom the leader's coherent, mythic narrative provides a sense of belonging and purpose.11
  - Anti-Egalitarianism: Fascism is fundamentally hierarchical. It opposes democracy that "equates a nation to the majority, lowering it to the level of the largest number" and instead champions a "purest form of democracy" based on quality and a unified will.14 It promotes extreme nationalism, often based on race, ethnicity, or religion, believing in the supremacy of certain groups over others.13 This leads to the identification of internal and external "enemies" or scapegoats—minorities, liberals, communists, socialists—whose persecution or elimination becomes a unifying cause.11
- Methods and Practices: The fascist ideology manifests in a consistent set of political practices.
  - Glorification of Violence and Militarism: Violence is not merely a tool but is celebrated as a redemptive and purifying force that forges national unity and strength.11 The state is marked by the supremacy of the military, a disproportionate amount of government funding for defense, and the embrace of paramilitarism.11
  - Suppression of Dissent and Human Rights: In the pursuit of national purity, fascism shows a disdain for human rights, which are seen as an obstacle to the needs of the state.11 Opposition leaders and minority groups are imprisoned or murdered, and a one-party state is created through fraudulent elections or the outright banning of other parties.8
  - Control of Information and Culture: The mass media is controlled either directly by the government or indirectly through regulation and sympathetic executives, with censorship being common.11
  - Corporatism and Social Hierarchy: Fascist regimes protect corporate power and suppress labor unions, which are seen as the only real threat to their power.11 They also enforce rampant sexism and rigid, traditional gender roles, often opposing abortion and promoting homophobia.11
  - Expansionism: The state is seen as a living entity whose will to power necessitates "organization and expansion, potential if not actual".14 This often translates into a foreign policy aimed at expanding territory through armed conflict.11

##### 2.3 Distinguishing Fascism from Modern Illiberalism and Authoritarianism

Applying this historical framework to the 21st century requires making careful distinctions between classical fascism and other forms of non-democratic rule that may share some of its characteristics.

- Para-fascism: This term, used by scholars to describe regimes in interwar Europe like those in Spain or Poland, refers to states that adopted or mimicked the external trappings of fascism—such as the leader cult, nationalism, and anti-socialism—but lacked the core revolutionary, mass-mobilizing, and palingenetic vision of genuine fascism.9 These were often traditional conservative or military dictatorships that used fascist aesthetics to bolster their authority. This concept is particularly useful for analyzing contemporary regimes that may appear fascist on the surface but whose underlying motivations are different.
- Authoritarianism: While all fascist regimes are authoritarian, not all authoritarian regimes are fascist. The key distinction, as noted by Robert Paxton, lies in mobilization. Traditional authoritarian rulers (monarchs, military dictators) often prefer a demobilized, passive, and obedient populace. They seek to maintain the status quo and suppress political activity.12 Fascists, by contrast, seek to "excite the public with rhetoric and political spectacle," mobilizing the masses in a revolutionary project to create a "new way" forward.12
- Illiberalism: This is a more recent concept, defined by Marlene Laruelle as an ideology that "pushes back against liberalism after having experienced it".5 Illiberalism rejects core tenets of liberal democracy, such as minority rights, separation of powers, and a free press, but it does not necessarily call for the "total destruction of modernity by creating an alternative world based on ancient values reconstructed with violent means," which Laruelle identifies as the essence of fascism.5 An illiberal democracy may hold elections (albeit flawed ones) and maintain some democratic institutions while hollowing them out from within.
- Kleptocracy: This refers to a system where rulers use their political power to plunder the state's wealth.5 While fascist regimes were notoriously corrupt, with cronyism and loyalty to the leader being paramount 11, kleptocracy can be an end in itself. In a purely kleptocratic system, the ideology serves the plunder, whereas in a fascist system, the plunder is often a byproduct of the ideological project.

Understanding these distinctions is crucial. It prevents the dilution of the term "fascism" and allows for a more precise analysis of contemporary political systems. The rise of a regime may exhibit illiberal, authoritarian, and kleptocratic features without necessarily constituting a fascist state in the historically grounded, scholarly sense of the term.

### Part II: Case Studies in Contemporary Power

#### Section 3: The United States \- Democratic Erosion or Fascist Turn?

The question of whether the United States is, or is becoming, a fascist country has moved from the fringes of political discourse to the center of academic and public debate.2 This debate is not merely an academic exercise; it reflects deep anxieties about the health of American democracy and the nature of the political forces shaping it. A rigorous analysis requires setting aside polemics and measuring the American political system against the scholarly framework of fascism, paying close attention to both its foundational principles and its contemporary strains.

##### 3.1 The American Political System: Foundation and Strains

The United States is a federal constitutional republic, founded on principles of liberal democracy.18 Its political system is defined by a strong rule-of-law tradition, a separation of powers between the legislative, executive, and judicial branches, and a Bill of Rights that enshrines robust freedoms of expression, religion, and assembly.19 This system is designed to protect individual liberties and prevent the concentration of autocratic power.

However, in recent years, these democratic institutions have suffered significant erosion.20 The system is under immense pressure from what political scientist Josep M. Colomer terms "constitutional polarization".21 The separation of powers, intended to create checks and balances, has, in an era of intense partisanship, often produced legislative gridlock, paralysis, and a rise in "negative political minorities able to block decisions".21 This institutional dysfunction is coupled with rising political extremism, partisan pressure on the electoral process, and growing disparities in wealth and political influence, leading to a decline in public trust and a weakening of democratic norms.20

##### 3.2 The Scholarly Debate on "American Fascism"

The rise of Donald Trump and the political movement he leads has ignited a fierce scholarly debate over the applicability of the "fascist" label to the United States. This is not a simple dispute over facts but a profound disagreement about historical analogy and the nature of the American political tradition.

- Arguments for the Fascist Analogy: Proponents of the analogy argue that the similarities between Trumpism and historical fascism are too significant to ignore. Historians and philosophers like Timothy Snyder, Jason Stanley, and Ruth Ben-Ghiat point to a range of overlapping features: the cultivation of a cult of personality around a strongman leader; relentless attacks on the press and the judiciary; the dehumanizing language used to describe immigrants and political opponents, such as "vermin" 23; the stoking of political violence; nostalgia for a mythic past ("Make America Great Again"); and explicit attempts to overturn democratic elections.2
  For these scholars, the use of the fascist label serves a critical purpose. Stanley argues that recognizing the "possibility that we are witnessing a fascist regime in the making" is essential to mobilize supporters of democracy.2 It functions as a "fire alarm," a warning that the country is on a dangerous path that has led to catastrophe elsewhere.8 This perspective often views the threat as an importation of a European ideology. However, other proponents, such as Alberto Toscano, connect it to indigenous American traditions, arguing that Black thinkers have long used the term "fascist" to accurately describe the country's racist carceral system and the terror of the Jim Crow era.2 In this view, American fascism is not an import but a homegrown phenomenon with deep roots.
- Arguments Against the Fascist Analogy: A significant number of scholars, however, caution against or outright reject the label. Historian Victoria de Grazia points to a fundamental divergence: historical fascism was a revolutionary movement born in the trenches of World War I, defined by imperial mobilization and expansionism. In contrast, the American right under Trump has often been more isolationist, led by a figure who avoided military service.2 Other critics, like Helmut Walser Smith, argue that while the Trump administration engaged in cruel rhetoric and policies, it did not succeed in systematically dismantling the country's political and legal system in the way the Nazis did.2 The institutions, though severely stressed, remained standing.
  Furthermore, opponents of the analogy argue that it is politically counterproductive and analytically misleading. Political scientist Samuel Moyn contends that "abnormalizing Trump" by labeling him a fascist "disguises that he is quintessentially American, the expression of enduring and indigenous syndromes".2 This view suggests that the focus on fascism distracts from the need to confront America's own long histories of racism, inequality, and violence, which created the conditions for Trump's rise. The danger, in this line of thinking, is that defeating a single "fascist" figure might lead to a complacent belief that the underlying problems have been solved, rather than spurring a deeper reckoning with the nation's chronic democratic deficits. The debate, therefore, is not just about whether Trump is a fascist, but about which historical parallel—1930s Europe or America's own troubled past—provides the most useful lens for understanding and combating the present threat.

##### 3.3 Analysis of Dissent and Civil Liberties

A crucial metric for assessing a country's political character is its treatment of dissent. A hallmark of a fascist state is the systematic and violent suppression of all opposition.11 The United States, by contrast, operates on a different plane. The First Amendment to the Constitution provides a strong legal foundation for freedom of speech and assembly, and these rights are central to the nation's political identity.26

This is not to say these rights are absolute or unchallenged. Throughout American history, access to free speech has been uneven, particularly for marginalized groups. The state has a long record of suppressing dissent from Black Americans, labor activists, socialists, and communists, using tools like the Criminal Syndicalism Acts of the early 20th century or the national security apparatus during the Cold War.26 In recent years, there has been a concerning wave of anti-protest bills introduced in state legislatures, aimed at chilling dissent and increasing penalties for protesters.26

Despite these real and significant pressures, the landscape of civil liberties in the United States remains fundamentally different from that of a fascist regime. According to Freedom House, the US people benefit from a "vibrant political system" and "robust freedoms of expression".20 There is a sprawling and active civil society, including organizations like the ACLU that legally challenge government overreach.26 The media, while facing political attacks, remains largely free and serves as a powerful check on government. Political opposition is not criminalized, and dissenters are not systematically imprisoned or executed for their views. The very existence of a loud, public, and high-level debate among prominent academics and journalists about whether the country is fascist—a debate conducted in the nation's leading newspapers and universities—is itself a powerful piece of evidence. In a consolidated fascist state, such a debate would be impossible; its participants would be silenced.11 This reveals a critical distinction between a democracy experiencing severe

_authoritarian tendencies_ and a society living under a _fascist regime_.

##### 3.4 Assessment

While the political movement known as Trumpism exhibits numerous characteristics that overlap with the style and substance of historical fascism—including ultranationalism, a cult of personality, the identification of scapegoats, a disdain for democratic norms, and the encouragement of political violence—the United States does not meet the scholarly definition of a fascist state.

The key missing element is the revolutionary, palingenetic core. Trumpism, for all its disruptive force, has not articulated a coherent ideology for the total revolutionary overthrow of the existing constitutional order to create an "alternative modernity".3 It is more reactionary than revolutionary, seeking to restore a mythologized past rather than build a new future. The American state has not been remade into a one-party system, the military has not been subordinated to a party, and the core institutions of civil society, while under attack, have not been destroyed.

The political system of the United States is more accurately characterized as a liberal democracy suffering from severe democratic backsliding and constitutional polarization.20 The political forces challenging its democratic norms are best understood as a form of right-wing or authoritarian populism, which operates within and against democratic institutions rather than seeking their complete abolition in a revolutionary project. The threat to American democracy is profound and rooted in its own history and contemporary dysfunctions, but to label the current system as fascist is to misdiagnose the illness and risk prescribing the wrong cure.

#### Section 4: The Russian Federation \- A New Fascism or a Familiar Autocracy?

The political system that has consolidated in the Russian Federation under the rule of Vladimir Putin presents a potent case study in 21st-century authoritarianism. Following the full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022, the term "fascist" has been increasingly applied to the Russian state, both by its international opponents and by some scholars.30 However, as with the United States, a careful analysis reveals a complex reality that defies simple categorization, highlighting a unique blend of historical autocracy, modern kleptocracy, and fascist-like aesthetics.

##### 4.1 The Nature of the Russian State: Putinism and Ruscism

The contemporary Russian Federation is a highly centralized, personalist autocracy.17 Power is concentrated in the hands of President Vladimir Putin and a close circle of allies drawn primarily from the security services (the

_siloviki_).33 The political system features a weak, rubber-stamp legislature and a subservient judiciary, with the ruling United Russia party dominating a political landscape devoid of genuine competition.32

The state's guiding ideology, often referred to as "Putinism," is an eclectic and malleable synthesis of various intellectual sources. It borrows heavily from czarist imperial nostalgia and Soviet-era statism, blending them with themes from 20th-century right-wing radical thinkers.35 Key pillars of this ideology include:

- Statism and Russian Exceptionalism: An elevation of the strong, stable state as the ultimate value, combined with a messianic belief in Russia's unique civilizational mission and greatness.36
- Anti-Westernism: A deep-seated hostility towards the liberal West, which is portrayed as decadent, aggressive, and intent on destroying Russia.36
- Conservative Traditionalism: The promotion of conservative Orthodox Christian values, traditional family and gender roles, and a rejection of liberal principles like LGBTQ+ rights.35

This ideology is not articulated in a single coherent text but is absorbed through symbols, state-controlled media, and popular culture, making it flexible and easily digestible.36 In the wake of the 2022 invasion, the term "Ruscism" (or "Rashism") has gained currency to describe this ideology, emphasizing its aggressive militarism, hypernationalism, neo-imperialism, and cult of personality.30 The Ukrainian parliament officially recognized Ruscism as the state ideology of Russia in May 2023\.30

##### 4.2 The Scholarly Debate on "Russian Fascism"

As with the American case, there is a vibrant academic debate over whether Putin's Russia qualifies as a fascist state.

- Arguments for the Fascist Label: Scholars like Timothy Snyder argue forcefully that the Russian regime is fascist. He points to the direct influence of Russian nationalist and fascist philosopher Ivan Ilyin on Putin and the regime's ideology.30 Snyder identifies numerous parallels with historical fascism: the use of the "Z" symbol as a swastika-like emblem, the staging of mass rallies to support the war, the promotion of a cult of personality around Putin, and the framing of the war in Ukraine as a "cleansing act of violence" to purify the nation.30 Political scientist Vladislav Inozemtsev describes Russia as a "moderate fascist state," arguing that while it may lack the overt racialism of Nazism, it closely resembles Mussolini's Italy in its core features. He highlights Russia's state corporatism, where the state dominates the economy while preserving nominal private property; the rise of powerful paramilitary and enforcement agencies personally loyal to the leader (like the National Guard); and an irredentist foreign policy aimed at reclaiming a "historical Russia".31
- Arguments Against the Fascist Label: The most prominent scholarly voice arguing against the direct application of the fascist label is Marlene Laruelle. She contends that while the regime is deeply authoritarian and illiberal, it lacks the essential revolutionary core of classical fascism.5 In her view, Putin's regime is fundamentally
  _reactionary_ and _anti-revolutionary_. It seeks to maintain the status quo, preserve the power of the ruling elite, and _demobilize_ the population through apathy and fear, rather than mobilizing it for a grand project of societal transformation.38 She argues that the regime lacks a coherent, forward-looking ideology aimed at creating a "new man" or an "alternative modernity," which was central to historical fascism.5
  Instead, Laruelle and others propose alternative characterizations. They suggest Putin's Russia is better understood as a "kleptocracy," where the primary goal of the elite is the plunder of state resources, with ideology serving as a justification for this plunder.5 Other labels include a "personalist authoritarian regime" or, drawing on the historical distinction, a form of "para-fascism".5 This perspective sees the regime as adopting the aesthetics and rhetoric of fascism—the nationalism, militarism, and leader cult—as useful tools for regime maintenance, without embracing its revolutionary substance. The ideology's function is not to transform society, but to secure the power and wealth of the ruling clique.

##### 4.3 Analysis of Dissent and Civil Liberties

On the metric of civil liberties and the treatment of dissent, Putin's Russia aligns very closely with the practices of historical fascist and totalitarian regimes. The state's suppression of opposition is systematic, pervasive, and violent.34

Freedom House rates Russia as "Not Free," with a dismal score for civil liberties, noting that the Kremlin "manipulates elections and suppresses genuine opposition".34 The state exercises near-total control over the media environment, with all national television networks controlled by the government or its allies.34 A raft of repressive legislation—targeting "fake news," "discrediting the armed forces," "extremism," and "foreign agents"—has been used to effectively criminalize any form of dissent, particularly opposition to the war in Ukraine.34

The political opposition is functionally non-existent. A "systemic opposition" in the parliament is largely loyal to the Kremlin, while the "non-systemic" opposition of genuine critics has been dismantled through imprisonment, assassination, and exile.40 Prominent figures like Alexei Navalny have been imprisoned under fabricated charges and died in custody. Freedom of assembly is non-existent; anti-war protests are met with mass arrests and police brutality, with nearly 20,000 detentions for anti-war activities since February 2022\.34 This comprehensive and ruthless eradication of political space and civil liberties is a hallmark of the most repressive authoritarian systems.

##### 4.4 Assessment

The Russian Federation under Vladimir Putin presents a complex hybrid of authoritarianism that incorporates many, but not all, of the core elements of classical fascism. It clearly exhibits ultranationalism, a hypermasculine cult of the leader, extreme militarism, state control over the economy, a close intertwining of church and state, and the systematic, violent suppression of dissent.

However, the scholarly consensus leans away from a direct one-to-one equation with historical fascism, primarily due to its perceived lack of a revolutionary, mass-mobilizing ideological core. Its ideology appears more reactionary and instrumental—a tool to justify personalist power and kleptocracy—than a genuine, transformative project aimed at creating a "new" Russian society and civilization. Therefore, labels such as "para-fascist," "moderate fascist state," or "personalist autocracy with fascist features" appear to be more precise analytical descriptions. It is a regime that has adopted the methods and aesthetics of fascism to serve the goals of a more traditional, if exceptionally brutal, form of autocracy. Russia's historical self-identity as the ultimate anti-fascist power, forged in the crucible of World War II, creates a unique paradox. This national narrative serves as a powerful ideological shield, making it difficult for the "fascist" label to gain traction domestically while simultaneously providing the rhetorical justification for the regime's aggressive and expansionist foreign policy, which it frames as a continuation of that historic anti-fascist struggle.7

### Part III: The Global Opposition to Authoritarianism

Having established that neither the United States nor Russia neatly fits the mold of classical fascism—with the former being a strained democracy and the latter a unique para-fascist autocracy—the original query must be reframed. The central question is no longer who opposes a singular "faciest establishment," but rather, what are the forces arrayed against the broader, global trend of rising authoritarianism? The answer reveals a world far more complex than the bipolar ideological struggle of the Cold War. The opposition is not a single entity but a fragmented, multi-layered, and sometimes contradictory collection of actors with different motivations and goals.

#### Section 5: The Shifting Global Order \- From Cold War Bipolarity to Asymmetric Rivalry

To understand the present, it is essential to first correctly characterize the past. The Cold War paradigm, which implicitly frames the user's query, was a unique historical moment whose dynamics do not map cleanly onto the 21st century.

##### 5.1 The Cold War Re-examined

The Cold War (c. 1947–1991) was a sustained state of geopolitical, economic, and ideological rivalry between two superpowers and their respective blocs: the United States and the Western allies on one side, and the Soviet Union and the Eastern Bloc on the other.43 This was not merely a competition between two powerful nations; it was a global conflict between two mutually exclusive systems of social and political organization. The United States and its allies championed liberal-democratic capitalism, characterized by free markets, competitive elections, and individual freedoms.44 The Soviet Union promoted an authoritarian, one-party state model based on the ideology of communism.45

The opposition was therefore fundamentally ideological. The US and the West opposed the _ideology_ of communism and its global expansion, viewing it as a threat to free trade, democracy, and human rights.44 This opposition was expressed through a policy of containment, military alliances like NATO, economic aid to non-communist nations via the Marshall Plan, and support for anti-communist forces worldwide.43 The eventual "loss" of the Soviet Union was not a simple military defeat but the collapse of its political and economic system, brought on by a combination of internal decay, economic stagnation, and sustained external pressure from the Western bloc.43 This historical context is vital because it establishes a baseline of a coherent, ideologically unified bloc opposing another.

##### 5.2 The Rise of Assertive Authoritarianism

The post-Cold War "unipolar moment," during which the United States stood as the world's sole superpower, has given way to a new era of great-power competition. A key feature of this new era is the global expansion and increasing assertiveness of authoritarian rule.47 According to Freedom House, the world has been in a "democratic recession" for nearly two decades, with the number of countries experiencing a decline in political rights and civil liberties consistently outnumbering those experiencing gains.29

This new wave of authoritarianism is not a monolithic ideological movement like 20th-century communism or fascism. It is not driven by a universalist, revolutionary creed. Instead, it is better understood as a loose, pragmatic, and opportunistic network of autocratic states that collaborate to ensure their own survival and push back against democratic pressures. This "antidemocratic alliance" or "authoritarian international" operates through several mechanisms 48:

- Mutual Economic and Diplomatic Support: Authoritarian powers like China and Russia provide economic aid, investment, and diplomatic cover to smaller autocratic regimes (e.g., in Venezuela or Myanmar), helping them withstand sanctions and international condemnation from democracies.48
- Sharing Repressive Technologies and Techniques: China, in particular, has become a major exporter of the technological tools of authoritarianism, such as surveillance systems and internet censorship models, which are adopted by other regimes to control their populations.50
- Transnational Repression: Autocratic states increasingly cooperate to silence and persecute exiled dissidents living in other countries, using tactics like rendition, digital surveillance, and abuse of international institutions like Interpol.48
- Norm-Spoiling in International Institutions: These powers work within international bodies like the United Nations to weaken norms related to human rights and democracy, promoting alternative concepts like absolute state sovereignty and non-interference.50

This assertive authoritarianism, led primarily by China with Russia as a disruptive junior partner, presents a systemic challenge to the liberal international order, but it does so on pragmatic and interest-based grounds rather than as part of a unified fascist crusade.

#### Section 6: Identifying the Counterweights \- Who Opposes the Authoritarian Advance?

The opposition to this authoritarian trend is as complex and varied as the trend itself. It cannot be reduced to a single country or a simple binary. It is a multi-layered coalition of different actors with distinct, and sometimes conflicting, motivations.

##### 6.1 The Liberal Democratic Bloc

The most direct and ideologically coherent opposition to authoritarianism comes from the world's established liberal democracies, which form a loose bloc successor to the Western alliance of the Cold War.

- The United States: Despite its significant internal democratic challenges, the United States remains the principal leader of this bloc. Its foreign policy establishment continues to officially prioritize the promotion of democracy and the countering of authoritarian influence as core national interests.20 The US underpins a global network of alliances (such as NATO and its partnerships in the Indo-Pacific) and wields immense economic and military power to support democratic partners and push back against autocratic rivals.
- The European Union: The EU is a unique, normatively-driven power. Its founding treaties commit it to the promotion of human rights, democracy, and the rule of law as a core part of its foreign policy.53 It implements this policy through various instruments: including human rights clauses in all trade and cooperation agreements with non-EU countries; providing funding for civil society and democracy-building projects worldwide through mechanisms like the European Instrument for Democracy and Human Rights (EIDHR); and engaging in political dialogue to encourage reforms.53 The EU acts as a powerful economic and regulatory counterweight to authoritarian models.
- Other Key Democracies (e.g., Japan): Other major democracies, such as Japan, Canada, and Australia, are crucial components of this bloc. Japan's foreign policy is explicitly based on a close partnership with the United States and other democracies.57 As a member of the G7, Japan actively contributes to efforts to maintain a free and open international order, promote global peace and prosperity, and provide development assistance to countries in its region and beyond.58

However, the effectiveness of this democratic bloc is hampered by a critical vulnerability: the erosion of democracy within its own leading members. An America perceived as hypocritical due to its own democratic backsliding, or a Europe divided internally, is less credible and effective as a global champion for democracy.52 The struggle against authoritarianism abroad is therefore inextricably linked to the struggle for democratic renewal at home.

##### 6.2 The Systemic Rival: The Role of China

China represents a different kind of challenge. As the world's most powerful authoritarian state, it is the primary driver of the new assertive authoritarianism.50 It actively promotes its governance model as an alternative to liberal democracy, offering investment and political support to autocrats and working to reshape international norms to be more favorable to state control.47

However, China is not part of a "fascist establishment" with Russia. Its political system is Leninist, not fascist, and its foreign policy is driven by a pragmatic, long-term strategy focused on securing its economic interests, ensuring the survival of the Chinese Communist Party (CCP), and achieving what it sees as its rightful place as a global leader.61 While it collaborates with Russia to counter US influence, their alliance is one of convenience, not deep ideological kinship. China is a

_systemic rival_ to the liberal democratic order, but it is not a fascist state and does not promote fascism. It opposes the _liberal_ and _democratic_ aspects of the current international system, not necessarily authoritarianism in other forms. In this sense, China is not an opponent of a "fascist establishment" but rather the leader of a competing authoritarian model.

##### 6.3 The Balancing "Middle Powers"

A defining feature of the new multipolar world is the growing influence of large, strategically autonomous countries that refuse to align neatly with either the democratic or authoritarian blocs. These "middle powers" or "swing states" act as a different kind of check on global power.

- India: As the world's largest democracy, India shares many values with the liberal bloc. However, its foreign policy has evolved from its Cold War-era non-alignment to a pragmatic strategy of "multi-alignment".62 India simultaneously deepens its strategic partnership with the United States, Japan, and Australia through the Quad while also participating in forums like the Shanghai Cooperation Organisation (SCO) and BRICS alongside Russia and China.64 India's primary foreign policy driver is the pursuit of its own national interests and its ambition to rise as an independent pole in a multipolar world.62 It does not seek to join a US-led crusade against authoritarianism but rather to balance relationships and maximize its own strategic autonomy.
- Brazil: Brazil's foreign policy follows a similar logic of independence and non-alignment.66 Under President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva, Brazil has actively sought to maintain strong relationships with both the United States and China, rejecting a binary choice between the two.68 Like India, Brazil uses platforms like BRICS and the G20 to advocate for a more multipolar world order where the voices of developing countries and middle powers are given more weight.66 Brazil condemns violations of international law, such as Russia's invasion of Ukraine, but also rejects the use of unilateral sanctions and seeks to position itself as a neutral peacemaker.68

These middle powers oppose not a specific ideology like fascism, but rather the concentration of global power in any single bloc. Their goal is a multipolar system where they have the freedom to maneuver and pursue their own interests. In doing so, they act as a constraint on both American and Chinese/Russian ambitions.

##### 6.4 The Internal and Transnational Opposition

Finally, the opposition to authoritarianism is not limited to the actions of nation-states. A diverse and resilient network of non-state actors forms a crucial frontline in this struggle.

- Civil Society and Dissent: Within even the most repressive authoritarian states, domestic opposition movements persist. Though often brutally suppressed, as seen in Russia, Iran, or China, these movements of activists, journalists, and ordinary citizens represent a powerful internal challenge to autocratic rule.22 They are supported by transnational networks of non-governmental organizations (NGOs), human rights defenders, and media outlets that work to expose abuses, provide support to activists, and keep the flame of dissent alive.22
- Oppositional Ideologies: The most enduring opposition to authoritarianism lies in the power of alternative ideas. The core principles of liberal democracy—individual rights, the rule of law, government by consent of the governed—remain a potent and attractive alternative for people living under repression.22 Beyond liberalism, other anti-authoritarian ideologies, such as social democracy, which advocates for democracy alongside social and economic equality, and anarchism, which offers a radical critique of all forms of state power and hierarchy, contribute to a rich and diverse intellectual opposition to the concentration of power.70 These ideas transcend borders and provide the moral and philosophical foundation for resistance.

The opposition to the 21st-century authoritarian advance is therefore not a singular "who" but a complex "what": a combination of a formal bloc of democratic states, a set of non-aligned balancing powers, and a global network of civil society actors and anti-authoritarian ideas.

#### Section 7: Conclusion and Future Outlook

The initial query—"if russia lost the cold war because of an opposing USA, now that USA and Russia are both Facist who will oppose the rise of the faciest establishment?"—is built upon premises that a rigorous, evidence-based analysis cannot sustain. The Cold War was an ideological contest between liberal capitalism and authoritarian communism, a dynamic fundamentally different from today's geopolitical landscape. More importantly, a scholarly application of the definition of fascism reveals that while the United States is experiencing severe democratic erosion and Russia is a deeply repressive autocracy with fascist characteristics, neither fully embodies classical fascism. The United States remains a flawed liberal democracy, and Russia is more accurately described as a unique para-fascist or kleptocratic hybrid.

Therefore, there is no singular "fascist establishment" composed of a US-Russia axis. The actual global conflict is a more complex and asymmetric struggle. On one side is a loose network of assertive authoritarian states, led systemically by China and disrupted aggressively by Russia. These states are not bound by a common fascist ideology but by a pragmatic desire to make the world safe for their autocratic rule, push back against democratic norms, and increase their own power and influence.

The opposition to this authoritarian advance is consequently not a single nation-state, as the United States was positioned during the Cold War. It is a multifaceted and fragmented coalition of forces operating on different levels with different motivations:

1. A Bloc of Liberal Democracies: Led by the United States and including key actors like the European Union and Japan, this group remains the most direct and ideologically committed opponent of authoritarianism. It wields significant economic, diplomatic, and military power to defend the liberal international order.
2. Balancing Middle Powers: Nations like India and Brazil are not joining a new Cold War. They practice strategic autonomy, seeking to build a multipolar world where they are not subordinate to any single hegemon. They act as a check on both American and Sino-Russian power, opposing the concentration of power itself rather than a specific ideology.
3. Transnational Civil Society: A global network of NGOs, human rights defenders, journalists, and activists forms a crucial opposition from below, working across borders to expose repression and support democratic movements.
4. The Power of Ideas: The principles of liberal democracy, human rights, and the rule of law remain a powerful ideological alternative to authoritarianism, inspiring dissent and resistance worldwide.

The frontline in this 21st-century struggle is thus both geopolitical and domestic. The future effectiveness of the opposition to authoritarianism will depend on several key factors. First is the ability of the liberal democratic bloc to address its own internal vulnerabilities. A United States weakened by polarization and a European Union beset by internal division will lack the credibility and cohesion to lead a global pro-democracy coalition effectively. Democratic renewal at home is a prerequisite for a strong posture abroad.

Second is the ability of this bloc to forge broader, more flexible, and less dogmatic partnerships. Engaging with non-aligned powers like India and Brazil on areas of common interest—rather than demanding complete alignment—will be essential to building a wider front against the most aggressive forms of autocracy. Finally, the long-term success of the opposition will hinge on its ability to effectively support the non-state actors and internal dissenters who represent the most authentic and potent challenge to authoritarian regimes from within. The new opposition is decentralized, complex, and faces a formidable challenge, but it is far from non-existent. The struggle for a free and open global order continues, fought on many fronts by a diverse array of actors.
