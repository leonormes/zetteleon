## Before Latin: A Journey into the Linguistic Ancestry of Rome

The common referral to Latin as a foundational language of Western civilisation often begs the question: what came before it? The answer lies in a rich tapestry of languages spoken across ancient Italy and, ultimately, in a reconstructed prehistoric language that is the ancestor of a vast swathe of modern tongues.
The immediate predecessor of Latin was a language known as Archaic Latin, spoken in the region of Latium in central Italy. Our earliest evidence of this language dates back to the 7th century BCE. However, Archaic Latin was just one of several related languages and dialects spoken on the Italian Peninsula.

### The Italic Family: Latin's Closest Relatives

Latin belonged to the Italic branch of the much larger Indo-European language family. The Italic peoples are believed to have migrated into Italy from the north around the 2nd millennium BCE, bringing their language with them. This language, through a process of dialectal divergence, gave rise to a number of related tongues.
Among the most prominent Italic languages that co-existed with and predated the rise of Latin were:

- Oscan: Spoken in the southern Apennines, Oscan was a major language of pre-Roman Italy and is attested in several hundred inscriptions.
- Umbrian: Spoken in the north-central Apennines, Umbrian is known primarily from the Iguvine Tablets, a set of bronze plaques detailing religious ceremonies.
- Faliscan: Spoken in a small area just north of Rome, Faliscan was the most closely related Italic language to Latin.

As Rome's power and influence grew, Latin gradually supplanted these other Italic languages, which eventually became extinct.

### The Etruscan Enigma: A Non-Indo-European Influence

A significant linguistic presence in pre-Roman Italy was the Etruscan language. Spoken in Etruria (modern-day Tuscany), Etruscan was not an Indo-European language and its origins remain a subject of scholarly debate. Despite being unrelated, the close contact between the Etruscan civilisation and the early Latins led to a degree of linguistic borrowing. The Latin alphabet itself was largely derived from the Greek alphabet via the Etruscans. While the extent of its lexical influence is still discussed, some Latin words are thought to have Etruscan roots.

### The Ultimate Ancestor: Proto-Indo-European

So, where did the Italic languages, and by extension Latin, come from? Historical linguists have traced them back to a hypothesised ancestral language known as Proto-Indo-European (PIE). There are no written records of PIE; it is a "reconstructed" language.
This reconstruction has been achieved through the comparative method, a meticulous process where linguists compare the vocabulary, grammar, and sound systems of known ancient and modern Indo-European languages (such as Sanskrit, Ancient Greek, and Gothic). By identifying systematic correspondences between these languages, scholars can work backwards to deduce the likely form of their common ancestor.
It is believed that PIE was spoken by a people who lived sometime between 4500 and 2500 BCE. The most widely accepted theory for their homeland is the Pontic-Caspian Steppe, a vast area of grassland stretching from modern-day Ukraine to southern Russia. From this homeland, successive waves of migration over thousands of years spread Indo-European languages across Europe, the Iranian plateau, and northern India.
In essence, the story of what came before Latin is a journey back in time: from the bustling streets of ancient Rome, through a peninsula alive with a variety of Italic and other languages, to a hypothesised prehistoric tongue spoken on the Eurasian steppes. Latin's legacy is immense, but it is itself a descendant of a much older and broader linguistic family tree.
