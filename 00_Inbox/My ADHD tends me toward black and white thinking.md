That tendency towards **black-and-white thinking**, sometimes called dichotomous thinking, is indeed something that can be more common for individuals with ADHD. It's a cognitive style where things are often seen in extremes – good or bad, success or failure, skilled or useless – with very little room for the shades of grey in between.  
When you apply this black-and-white thinking to people's skills and value, it absolutely leads to the kind of binary judgment you described: "You're either up to a certain level of skill or you're useless." This pattern can be particularly harsh both on yourself and on how you perceive others, and it directly fuels the struggle to see nuanced human worth.  
Here's why this black-and-white thinking is so challenging in this context:

1. **It Oversimplifies Complexity:** Human beings are incredibly complex. Everyone has a unique tapestry of strengths, weaknesses, developing skills, latent talents, and areas where they struggle. Black-and-white thinking simply cannot capture this reality. It forces a rich, multi-dimensional picture into a restrictive, two-option frame.  
2. **It Sets Unrealistic Standards:** If "useful" means being above a certain high skill threshold in a *specific area* (like the productivity you've mentioned), then by this logic, a vast number of people (including oneself) will inevitably fall into the "useless" category in various aspects of life. This is a recipe for constant disappointment and feelings of inadequacy.  
3. **It Dismisses Growth and Process:** Learning and skill development are rarely linear or binary. They involve process, effort, setbacks, and incremental progress. Black-and-white thinking tends to focus only on the end state (skilled or not skilled) and devalues the journey, the effort, and the smaller competencies gained along the way.  
4. **It Undermines Self-Worth and the Worth of Others:** If someone's (or your own) value is contingent on meeting this singular, high bar in a specific domain, then worth becomes incredibly fragile and conditional. It prevents you from appreciating the inherent value of a person, which exists independently of any particular skill level.  
5. **It Fuels Comparison and Shame:** If you see someone who *is* "up to that level," your black-and-white thinking can then reinforce your own feelings of being "useless" in comparison, intensifying the shame you've spoken about.

**Moving Towards a More Nuanced View (Thinking in Colour):**  
Challenging this black-and-white thinking is a key part of learning to value yourself and others more holistically. It's a conscious practice:

1. **Catch the Binary Thought:** The first step is awareness. When you notice yourself thinking "skilled or useless," "good or bad," pause and label it: "That's black-and-white thinking."  
2. **Introduce the Spectrum (The Shades of Grey):**  
   * Ask yourself: "What's between these two extremes?" If someone isn't an "expert" at something, what are they? They might be a learner, competent, improving, good at a specific part of it, good enough for certain purposes, or simply have different strengths altogether.  
   * Use percentages or scales: Instead of "skilled or not," think "How skilled on a scale of 1-10 in *this specific task*?" and "What about their other skills?"  
3. **Embrace the "And":** Actively replace "or" with "and." Instead of "She's not very organised, *so* she must not be very effective," try "She struggles with organisation *and* she's incredibly creative and empathetic." Or for yourself, "I struggle with initiating tasks *and* I am resilient and have unique insights."  
4. **Develop a "Skills Profile" Mindset:** Visualise everyone (yourself included) as having a profile sheet with many different skills and qualities listed. Some will be high, some moderate, some low. The *overall profile* is unique and valuable, not just one or two lines on that sheet. No one has all highs, and no one has all lows.  
5. **Value Different Kinds of Strengths:** Actively look for and acknowledge diverse talents and qualities beyond your primary area of concern (e.g., productivity). This could be emotional intelligence, kindness, artistic ability, problem-solving in unconventional ways, loyalty, humour, resilience, etc.  
6. **Focus on Effort and Growth, Not Just Current Proficiency:** Recognise that where someone is now is just a snapshot. Value the effort they put in, the progress they make (however small), and their willingness to learn. This applies to you too.  
7. **Practice Self-Compassion:** When you notice yourself applying this harsh binary judgment to yourself, consciously soften it. Remind yourself that your worth isn't tied to being "above a certain level" in all things. Acknowledge the challenges ADHD brings without letting them define your entire value.

It's a process, and old thinking habits can be persistent, especially with ADHD. But by consciously and repeatedly practising these more nuanced ways of seeing, you can gradually lessen the grip of black-and-white thinking and cultivate a more compassionate and realistic appreciation of both your own value and the value of others, beyond narrow definitions of skill or status.
