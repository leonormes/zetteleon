The idea behind allowing everyone to vote, even those who may not fully understand the complexities of running a country, is rooted in several principles fundamental to modern democracy:



1\. Equality and Representation:



Democracy is based on the belief that every individual has an equal right to participate in decisions that affect them. While not everyone may fully grasp the intricacies of governance, they still live under the laws, policies, and leadership, and therefore should have a say in shaping those.



2\. Diverse Perspectives:



Society is made up of different groups with different needs, experiences, and priorities. Limiting who can vote would exclude certain voices, leading to a government that only serves the interests of a few. Including everyone helps ensure a more balanced representation of societal interests.



3\. Trust in Collective Wisdom:



The wisdom of the crowd is a core principle of democracy. The collective judgment of millions of people, each with their own experiences and knowledge, is considered more reliable than that of a small group of elites. While individual voters may not have all the information, the assumption is that a wide electorate can balance out individual biases or misunderstandings.



4\. Preventing Elitism and Power Concentration:



If only a select group were allowed to choose leaders—perhaps based on education or expertise—it could lead to elitism and the consolidation of power in the hands of a few. History shows that concentrated power often leads to corruption and abuse. Broad participation helps diffuse power and keep leaders accountable.



5\. Political Education:



Although many people might not be deeply familiar with the mechanics of governance, participation in elections can serve as an opportunity for political education. Over time, through debates, campaigns, and media, voters learn more about key issues, which in turn can raise the general political awareness of the population.



6\. Evolving Public Opinion:



Public opinion and social priorities change over time. The electorate reflects these shifts in values, and by allowing everyone to participate, democracy adapts to changing societal needs, whether on economic, social, or environmental issues.



7\. Accountability and Legitimacy:



Governments that are chosen by a broad electorate tend to have greater legitimacy. If people feel their voices are heard, they're more likely to respect the system and comply with the laws. Excluding large sections of society from the decision-making process could lead to unrest, instability, and questions about the legitimacy of the government.



In summary, while the process of voting might not guarantee perfect decisions, it ensures that governance reflects the collective will and interests of society. Democracy isn’t perfect, but it’s designed to be adaptable, inclusive, and resilient to the challenges of governance.
