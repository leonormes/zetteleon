I have been using the resistance band to pracitce proper form without the full draw weight of the bow. I find that it is causing aching in my lower back. Is this just weak back muscles that I can train to strengthen or a sign I am doing it incorrectly

Lower back aching during resistance band practice is a clear sign of a postural error (incorrect technique) rather than simple muscle fatigue.

While it is natural to assume that lower back discomfort means those muscles are weak and need strengthening, archery coaching manuals and biomechanics sources emphasize that lower back pain almost always stems from the "Curve Trap" (or "Hollow Back")—an arched lumbar spine caused by improper core engagement during the setup and draw.

---

## Why the Lower Back Aches During Practice

1. The "Curve Trap" / "Hollow Back": When raising your arms or drawing tension into a band, your body naturally wants to puff the chest outward and raise the shoulders. This causes the lower back to arch backward (lumbar hyperextension) and the abdomen to relax. Instead of the core supporting your posture, the compressive forces of the draw are dumped directly into your lower back vertebrae and lumbar muscles.
2. Leaning Backward Away from the Target: As tension builds in the draw, archers frequently sway or lean their upper body back toward their rear foot. Bending back at the waist to compensate for tension disrupts spinal alignment and causes lumbar discomfort.
3. Lack of Band Frame Stability: A stretch band has zero rigid mass or vertical structure. Without a physical bow to anchor against, it is very easy to unconsciously arch your spine or flare your ribs during the draw without noticing.

---

## The "Flat Back" Posture Blueprint

To eliminate lower back aching and protect your spine, you must establish a flat lower back before you initiate any draw movement:

- Tuck the Hips (Pelvic Tilt): Stand in your stance and squeeze your glutes slightly (as if holding a coin between your butt cheeks). This rotates your pelvis down and forward underneath your spine, flattening your lower back into a solid, vertical column.
- Keep the Ribs Down ("Chest Flat"): Exhale fully to let your ribcage drop. Lock your core down by maintaining slight abdominal tension—feeling as if you are about to be punched in the stomach or holding a subtle crunch. This connects your chest to your pelvis and prevents your spine from arching.
- Maintain the Vertical "T" Posture: Keep your spine completely vertical over your hips. Keep about 60% of your weight balanced on the balls of your feet to prevent yourself from leaning back onto your heels as you draw.

---

## How to Check and Fix This at Home

Try the Rib-Separation Mirror Test during your next band session:

1. Stand at a 90-degree angle to a mirror without any band.
2. Tuck your pelvis, flatten your lower back, and engage your stomach.
3. Slowly raise your empty hands into the pre-draw position while watching your side profile in the mirror.
4. The Checkpoint: If you feel your ribs flare apart, your chest lift up, or your lower back arch backward as your arms go up, your core has disengaged.
5. Pause, reset your flat back, and practice raising the stretch band only as far as you can while keeping your lower back completely flat.

---

In archery, "locking the core" does not mean puffing out your chest or tensing every abdominal muscle as hard as possible. Instead, it means flattening the lower back (avoiding the "hollow back" or "curve trap") and anchoring your ribcage down into a stable, vertical "T" column that resists the compressive forces of the bow.

Here are four specific exercises and diagnostic drills from your sources designed to train and verify this core lock:

---

### 1. The "Belly on a Stick" (Low Center-of-Gravity Exercise)

This classic World Archery drill teaches you to keep your lower core "heavy" and lower your center of gravity so your chest does not lift or inflate when drawing.

- How to perform it:
    1. Take a wooden rod or stick (about 1 metre long) and place one end against the floor roughly 1.5 metres in front of you.
    2. Rest the upper end against your lower belly, just below your navel (alternatively, tuck a small squash or tennis ball behind your belt at the navel).
    3. Stand in your shooting stance and apply steady downward pressure through your lower stomach against the stick or ball.
    4. Maintain this continuous "heavy belly" pressure throughout your setup, draw, execution, and follow-through.
- Why it works: If you arch your lower back or flare your chest upward during the draw, the stick drops or pressure is lost. The drill forces you to keep your abdominal wall engaged and your ribcage down.

---

### 2. The "Tearing the Paper Towel" & Pelvic Lock Drill

Developed to lock the hips and lower body, this isometric exercise prevents your pelvis from shifting or rotating as draw weight increases.

- How to perform it:
    1. Set your feet in your normal stance (square or opened 10° to 15° toward the target line).
    2. Squeeze your glutes slightly (as if holding a coin between your butt cheeks) to tuck your pelvis underneath your spine.
    3. Imagine standing on a single sheet of paper towel.
    4. Without moving your feet, apply continuous isometric outward pressure into the ground as if trying to tear the paper towel in half between your feet.
- Why it works: This outward leg tension fires the glutes and locks the hip girdle, creating a rock-solid pedestal for your lower back that prevents pelvic swaying or recoil upon release.

---

### 3. The 100% Exhale & "Abdominal Punch" Reset

This breathing and posture sequence prevents ribcage flaring, which is the single most common cause of back arching.

- How to perform it:
    1. Stand upright in your stance before lifting the bow.
    2. Exhale 100% of the air out of your lungs to allow your ribcage to drop down into a low, flat position.
    3. Engage your abdominals into a subtle "crunch" position, feeling as though you are about to be punched in the stomach.
    4. Lock your core in this low position, and from that point forward, breathe strictly using your diaphragm (expanding the belly outward rather than raising the chest up and down).
- Why it works: Dropping the chest shortens the abdominal wall for maximum stability, flattens the lumbar spine, and provides generous clearance so the bowstring doesn't strike your chest or clothing at full draw.

---

### 4. The Upper Chest Balance Test (Partner Push Check)

A simple, objective diagnostic test to verify that your weight distribution and core brace can withstand external force.

- How to perform it:
    1. Assume your archery posture with your weight split 60% on the balls of your feet and 40% on your heels.
    2. Tuck your pelvis, brace your abs into your subtle crunch position, and hold your setup posture.
    3. Have a training partner apply light, steady hand pressure directly against your upper chest.
    4. The Checkpoint: If you tip backward, sway onto your heels, or collapse in the lower back, your core has disengaged. Adjust your pelvic tuck and forward weight distribution until you remain completely rock-solid under push pressure.

Verdict: more likely form than weakness. Your lower back is probably holding an arch or a lean-back that your skeleton should be holding. Weakness may contribute, but strengthening on its own would just let you hold the fault for longer.

## Why I Think that

- The ache is in the wrong place for a strength problem. In a correct draw the work is in your upper back, between and behind the shoulder blades. If those muscles were weak, that's where you'd ache.
- The lower back is a stabiliser in archery, not a prime mover. When it aches, it's usually propping up a posture: a hollow lower back, or your whole body drifting backwards during the draw.
- Your coaching manuals name this fault:
    - The Level 1 manual lists gradually leaning backwards during draw expansion as a common novice error.
    - Its set-up cue is to tilt the pelvis backwards to flatten the lower back.
    - The Level 2 manual notes that bending back at the waist curves the spine and can cause discomfort over a day's shooting.
- The band makes it more likely. There's no arrow and no shot to end the rep, so it's easy to keep expanding, often by arching to find more back tension. Band sessions also tend to pack in more reps and longer holds than a real end.

|Signal|Points to|

## The Test: You Make the Diagnosis

1. Phone where the target would be, at chest height.
    - Watch the curve of your lower back.
    - Check whether your belt line tips forward between set-up and full draw.
    - Note whether your weight sits on your heels or your forefoot.
2. Phone facing your chest.
    - Check whether your shoulders stay stacked over your hips or drift towards your string foot as you draw.
3. Compare your set-up frame with your full-draw frame. You're looking for change _during_ the draw, not your resting posture.

Before you watch it back, write down which pattern you expect to see. That's your hypothesis to falsify.

## If It's Form (Most Likely)

- Set-up cues from the manual:
    - Tuck the pelvis.
    - Keep a flat chest and low shoulders.
    - Put slightly more weight on the forefoot than the heels.
- Drill from the Level 1 manual, which you can do at home:
    - Rest a broom handle between the floor and your belly.
    - Keep a "heavy belly" pressing into it through the whole draw.
    - If the pressure fades, you've arched.
- Stop the set when you feel your lower back take over. Reps after that point practise the fault, which is the opposite of what you're using the band for.
- Ask a club coach to watch one end during the indoor season. A second pair of eyes catches what a phone misses.

## Strength Still Matters, but Second

- What it's for: strength gives you the capacity to _hold_ a correct posture. It doesn't replace learning the posture.
- Manual's core block:
    - Prone, side and supine planks.
    - Hold for 15–60 seconds.
    - Start with one set and build to three.
    - Do one or two exercises per session, not the whole menu.

## When to Stop Self-diagnosing

See a GP or physio rather than training through it if any of these happen:

- Pain spreads into your buttock or leg.
- You get numbness or tingling.
- The pain is sharp rather than achy.
- It wakes you at night.
- It hasn't settled within a couple of weeks of fixing your posture and cutting volume.
