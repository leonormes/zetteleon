The Core Hypothesis:

You're suggesting that extroverts, by potentially "thinking in public" more readily, might have their thinking "bounded" by their immediate social environment. This could lead to earlier social validation of their ideas, and consequently, a reduced _perceived need_ for the kind of deep, private elaboration that someone more introverted (or someone like yourself, for reasons we've discussed) might engage in. This isn't to say they are less intelligent, but that their process might lead to ideas that are less internally elaborated before being expressed, which could _sometimes be perceived_ as less thoughtful by those who value deep, solitary reflection.

Investigating the Validity:

1. Extroverts and "Thinking in Public":
   - The definition of extroversion often includes drawing energy from social interaction and a tendency towards outward processing. It's plausible that many extroverts do process thoughts by talking them through, effectively "thinking aloud" or "thinking in public." This allows for immediate feedback and interactive idea development.
   - This contrasts with introverts, who often prefer to process information internally before sharing, developing their thoughts more fully in private.
2. "Bounded by Those Around Them":
   - If thinking occurs predominantly in a social context, it will inevitably be shaped by the input, norms, and understanding of that context. This can be positive–leading to well-calibrated, communicable, and collaboratively refined ideas.
   - However, it could also be "bounded" in the sense that ideas might not venture too far from the group's existing consensus or understanding if the environment isn't actively encouraging divergent thought. There's a risk of premature convergence or ideas being "levelled down" to what is easily accepted.
3. "Sooner Validation" and "Less Need to Elaborate":
   - If an idea receives quick positive social feedback and achieves its immediate communicative or social goal, the external impetus for further deep, solitary elaboration might indeed be reduced. The "problem" (of developing the thought) might appear "solved" at an earlier stage of internal complexity.
   - This doesn't mean the thinking stops, but it might evolve more through subsequent interactions rather than deep private reflection. The elaboration becomes a social, iterative process.
4. Perception of Intelligence/Thoughtfulness:
   - This is where it gets particularly subjective. Qualities often associated with "deep thought"–nuance, complexity, originality born from solitary reflection–might be less immediately apparent if an idea is presented in a more direct, socially adapted form.
   - Someone who values those qualities might perceive this style as less "thoughtful," not because it lacks intelligence, but because it hasn't undergone the same _kind_ of internal journey they associate with profound understanding.
   - It's crucial to distinguish between the _process_ of thinking and the _output_, and also between actual intellectual capacity and the _style_ of its expression.

How it Fits with Our Conversation:

- Social Validation: This idea offers a contrasting model of social validation. In our previous discussion, your need for validation was tied to the fragility of internally-built schemas and the pain of RSD. For the extrovert in your hypothesis, social validation might be a more continuous, less fraught, and integral part of the _initial_ idea formation, rather than a high-stakes checkpoint after extensive private work.
- "Thinking in Public": We discussed this as something you find disruptive to your deep thinking. Your hypothesis posits it as a primary mode for others, highlighting how different cognitive styles interact with this approach. For them, it might not "slam the brakes" but rather be the accelerator.
- "Elaborate Schemas" vs. Iterative Development: You build elaborate schemas internally. The extroverted style might result in less elaborate _initial_ schemas, but perhaps more socially robust and flexible ones that are built upon iteratively through interaction.
- "Illusion of Profundity": An extrovert who processes externally more often might encounter the "starkness of articulated language" much sooner and more frequently. This could mean they grapple with the "Illusion of Profundity" differently–perhaps experiencing less of an internal build-up of perceived profundity before an idea is tested externally. Their ideas might become "grounded" more quickly.
- Contrast with ADHD/RSD Experience: Your need for a safe internal world (due to RSD) and the unique way your ADHD mind works create a very different scenario. The extrovert in your hypothesis (especially one without strong RSD) wouldn't have the same barriers to early and frequent externalisation. Their "garage door" might be naturally more open because the perceived risks are lower and the social rewards more immediate.

Potential Truth in Your Idea:

Yes, there could be truth to your idea as an explanation for _differing cognitive and communicative styles_ stemming from personality traits, and how these styles might be perceived.

- It's plausible that a tendency to process thoughts externally within a social group leads to ideas that are more rapidly iterated and validated within that group's context.
- This could mean less _solitary_ elaboration on any single iteration, because the "elaboration" becomes a distributed, ongoing social process.
- This style might sometimes be _misinterpreted_ by those who equate deep thoughtfulness with extensive solitary reflection and highly nuanced individual articulation.

Important Caveats:

- Generalisation: As you said, this is a generalisation. Extroverts are not a monolith; many are capable of and engage in deep private thought. Similarly, introverts can be excellent communicators.
- Intelligence: This model should not be taken to imply differences in underlying intelligence. It's about preferred processing styles and how ideas are developed and shaped.
- Context and Group Dynamics: The quality of the "social bounding" is critical. A highly intellectual, critically engaging group might push an extrovert's thinking to great depths, while a more superficial group might not.

In conclusion, your idea is a thoughtful extension of our conversation. It highlights how personality traits like extroversion could lead to a different rhythm and mode of idea development and validation, which, in turn, might contribute to certain social perceptions. It provides a valuable counterpoint to your own experience, showing how the journey from initial thought to validated idea can vary significantly based on individual differences.
