## The Gender Dynamics of Compliments

### A Research Briefing on What Men and Women Praise, Prefer, and How They Respond

_An academic synthesis drawing on sociolinguistics (Holmes, Wolfson & Manes, Herbert, Tannen) and social psychology (objectification theory, precarious manhood, sexual selection). Figures are as reported in the original studies and rounded; consult the originals for precise values. Foundational corpora are Anglophone and 1980s-era—treat all patterns as statistical tendencies within a specific cultural moment, not universals._

---

### Executive Summary

- Compliments are a gendered economy. In Janet Holmes's foundational New Zealand corpus, women gave roughly two-thirds of all compliments and received roughly three-quarters; male-to-male compliments were the scarce, marked case (~9% of the total).
- Topics diverge. Appearance dominates compliments given to and exchanged between women; skill, performance and possessions dominate compliments between men.
- Functions diverge. For women, compliments primarily operate as solidarity tokens (rapport); for men, they more often function as evaluative verdicts (report), which makes them potentially face-threatening—and therefore rarer, terser, and frequently disguised as banter.
- Receptivity diverges. Men tend to weight competence-based praise heavily (scarcity plus status logic); women's relationship with appearance-based praise is genuinely double-edged—mood-enhancing yet capable of triggering self-objectification, and competence-undermining in professional contexts.
- Delivery is signal. Compliments are astonishingly formulaic (three syntactic frames cover ~85% of American data), which is precisely why novel metaphor in romantic compliments measurably raises perceived intelligence and attractiveness—creative language functions as a hard-to-fake display of mind.
- Responses follow a universal dilemma (agree with your interlocutor vs avoid self-praise): acceptance is the commonest single outcome, but women—especially with other women—deflect, downgrade and return more; men evade, joke or ignore more.
- Context reshapes everything. In goal-oriented settings, the appearance/performance gap largely collapses towards performance for both genders—much of the "gender difference" is a _setting_ difference plus gendered access to settings.
- The patterns are drifting. Digital platforms simultaneously amplify the classic pattern (Instagram appearance culture) and erode it (male "hype" norms, gaming's dense competence-praise economy).

### 1. The Sociolinguistic Foundations

#### 1.1 The Research Lineage

- Robin Lakoff, _Language and Woman's Place_ (1975) made the first systematic claims about gendered speech styles—hedges, intensifiers, and so-called "empty adjectives" (_adorable_, _lovely_, _divine_). This is precisely the lexical territory compliments draw upon, and Lakoff's framework primed the field to look for gender asymmetries in evaluative talk.
- Nessa Wolfson and Joan Manes (1980–81) collected large ethnographic corpora of American compliments and made the striking discovery that compliments are among the most formulaic speech acts in English—a small set of syntactic frames and a threadbare adjective lexicon do almost all the work. They characterised the compliment as "social lubricant": its job is to create or reinforce solidarity, not to convey novel information.
- Janet Holmes (1986, 1988, 1995) is the foundational figure for the gender question. Her ethnographically collected New Zealand corpus of naturally occurring compliments produced the distributional statistics still cited today, and her books (_Women, Men and Politeness_, 1995) embedded them in politeness theory.
- Robert Herbert (1989, 1990) added a large American corpus (over a thousand exchanges), systematic analysis of _responses_, and cross-cultural contrast—establishing that both the giving and the receiving of praise are gendered.

#### 1.2 Two Readings of the Same Speech Act

The central theoretical insight—the principle from which most of the statistics follow—is that a compliment is inherently ambiguous between two functions:

- A positive politeness strategy (Brown & Levinson 1987): it attends to the hearer's _positive face_—the desire to be liked and approved of. Read this way, a compliment is a verbal gift, and Holmes's data suggest women predominantly produce and interpret compliments within this frame.
- A face-threatening act (FTA): a compliment presupposes the speaker's _right to evaluate_ you, can imply envy or desire (particularly compliments on possessions), and can impose unwanted intimacy. Holmes argued men more often orient to this reading—which elegantly explains why men give fewer compliments, hedge them, compress them, and route them through "safe" topics.

#### 1.3 Rapport Talk versus Report Talk

- Deborah Tannen (_You Just Don't Understand_, 1990), building on Maltz and Borker's (1982) two-cultures model of gendered socialisation, distinguished:
  - Rapport talk—talk as a means of establishing connection, symmetry and community. Within this frame a compliment is a bonding gift that invites reciprocation; frequent exchange _is_ the relationship.
  - Report talk—talk as a means of conveying information and negotiating status and independence. Within this frame a compliment is a verdict delivered from a judging position; accepting it gracefully means accepting the complimenter's standing to judge.
- The consequence: the identical utterance ("great work") lands as warmth in one frame and as hierarchy in the other. Nearly every downstream asymmetry—frequency, topic, syntax, response—can be derived from this single functional split.
- A necessary modern qualification: contemporary sociolinguistics (Eckert & McConnell-Ginet's "communities of practice"; Deborah Cameron's critique in _The Myth of Mars and Venus_, 2007) treats these styles as practices people perform in particular contexts, not essences men and women _have_. The tendencies below are statistical, context-sensitive, and drawn overwhelmingly from Anglophone, WEIRD, binary-framed samples.

### 2. Categorisation of Praise (What is Said)

#### 2.1 Who Compliments Whom

Holmes's New Zealand corpus produced the canonical distribution:

| Dyad | Share of all compliments |
|---|---|
| Female → Female | ≈51% |
| Male → Female | ≈23% |
| Female → Male | ≈17% |
| Male → Male | ≈9% |

- Women gave approximately 68% of all compliments and received approximately 74%.
- Principle: complimenting is a heavily female-associated currency in Anglophone data. The male–male compliment is the _marked_, scarce case—and scarcity, as §3 shows, changes its psychological weight.

#### 2.2 What Gets Praised

Holmes's approximate topic distribution across the whole corpus:

| Topic | Approximate share |
|---|---|
| Appearance | ≈38% (largest single category) |
| Ability / performance | ≈33% |
| Possessions | ≈11% |
| Personality / friendship | ≈11% |

The averages, however, conceal the real story, which lives in the dyads:

- Female → Female: appearance dominates—a clear majority of exchanges in several analyses. Praise of clothing, hair and aesthetic choices operates as ritual solidarity: frequent, reciprocal, and socially _expected_, such that its absence can itself be meaningful.
- Male → Male: appearance compliments are vanishingly rare, heavily constrained by heteronormative masculinity norms—Kiesling's (2004) work on address terms like _dude_ documents precisely this balancing act between solidarity and the avoidance of intimacy. Instead, skill, performance and possessions dominate ("nice car", "good shot", "solid deck"), routed through activity domains—sport, work, equipment—that license praise _without_ intimacy. M–M praise is also frequently disguised as banter or ritual insult (a tradition Labov documented in 1972): mock abuse performs affection while keeping the report-frame's symmetry intact.
- Cross-gender: male-to-female compliments skew towards appearance, with attendant ambiguity between politeness and flirtation—Holmes noted women do not always read these as simple politeness. Female-to-male compliments skew towards skill and effort, partly because women anticipate the male FTA-reading of appearance praise and route around it.

#### 2.3 Herbert's Stylistic Split

- Women favour personalised, first-person frames: "_I love_ your jacket"—speaker-centred and affectively invested.
- Men favour impersonal, object-centred frames: "Nice jacket"—evaluative, compressed, and deniable.
- This maps directly onto rapport/report: personalisation stakes the self on the exchange; impersonality preserves distance and the judge-stance.

### 3. Receptivity and Preferences (What is Desired)

#### 3.1 Why Competence-based Praise Lands Hardest with Men

Four converging mechanisms, from most linguistic to most psychological:

- Scarcity → salience. Men simply receive far fewer compliments (Holmes), so each one carries more informational weight—a rarity effect straight out of signalling logic. The popular observation that a man will "remember one compliment for years" is anecdotal rather than established, but it is exactly what scarcity economics would predict.
- Agentic self-construal. Men, on average, construe the self more independently and agentically (Cross & Madson 1997; cf. the "Big Two" dimensions of _agency_ and _communion_, Abele & Wojciszke). Praise targeting competence therefore strikes identity-central territory, whereas relational or aesthetic praise targets the periphery.
- Precarious manhood (Vandello & Bosson 2008): masculinity is experienced as a status that is _"hard won and easily lost"_ and demands continual public proof. A competence compliment functions as status re-certification—it does real work. Appearance praise does little status work and, intra-male, has historically risked violating heteronormative display rules altogether.
- The report frame. Within a status-negotiation frame, competence praise is _legible_—a verdict on the performance, not the person. Praise of the person (warmth, looks, character) can feel like an evaluation of something not submitted for judgement.

#### 3.2 Women's Double Ledger: Appearance versus Skill

The research picture here is genuinely two-sided, and worth stating carefully:

- Appearance praise is pleasant and socially functional. Among intimates it is solidarity currency, and experimentally it does lift mood.
- But it carries measurable costs. Objectification theory (Fredrickson & Roberts 1997) predicts that appearance commentary—positive or negative—invites self-surveillance. Tiggemann and Boundy (2008) found that an appearance compliment improved women's mood _and simultaneously_ increased state self-objectification and body shame in participants high in trait self-objectification. The pleasure and the cost co-occur in the same moment.
- Diagnosticity discounting. Appearance praise is cheap and frequent—F–F networks run a degree of compliment inflation—and in ambiguous cross-gender contexts it is often discounted as formulaic or instrumental. Skill praise is rarer, more specific, and more diagnostic, so it tends to be weighted more heavily wherever competence is identity-relevant—most obviously in professional life, where women may have to work against ambient doubts about competence in the first place.
- Stereotype-premised praise backfires. Compliments grounded in group membership ("women are so organised", and analogous ethnic examples) are experienced as depersonalising and are received poorly (Siy & Cheryan 2013). Individuated specificity beats generic valence.
- Principle: praise is not decoded on valence alone. Recipients of either gender read it for diagnosticity, sincerity, and what it implies about how they are seen—and the gendered asymmetry lies in _which_ implied gaze each group has learnt to monitor.

### 4. Linguistic Nuances and Delivery

#### 4.1 The Compliment Formula

Manes and Wolfson's (1981) American corpus established just how formulaic compliments are:

- Three syntactic frames account for ~85% of all compliments:
  1. _NP is/looks (really) ADJ_—"Your hair looks great." (≈54%)
  2. _I (really) like/love NP_—"I love that colour." (≈16%)
  3. _PRO is (really) (a) ADJ NP_—"That's a lovely frame." (≈15%)
- The lexicon is threadbare: five adjectives—_nice, good, beautiful, pretty, great_—cover roughly two-thirds of adjectival compliments, with _nice_ and _good_ alone accounting for about two-fifths. Where a verb carries the positive meaning, it is overwhelmingly _like_ or _love_.

#### 4.2 Gendered Micro-patterns

- Women use more intensifiers (_really_, _so_, _such_) and more boosted, exclamatory frames—Holmes found the rhetorical pattern _"What a lovely X!"_ used almost exclusively by women in her corpus—alongside the personalised "_I love…_" frame.
- Men favour minimal, elliptical forms—the pattern _(INT) ADJ (NP)_: "Nice bike." Compression achieves three things at once: low affect display, deniability, and preservation of the judge-stance.
- Cross-gender delivery tends to be more hedged and more formulaic than same-gender female exchange—formula is a safety device where the politeness/flirtation boundary is ambiguous.

#### 4.3 Creativity as a Signal: Metaphor in Romantic Compliments

- The key study: Gao and colleagues (2017, _Scientific Reports_) had women rate men described as paying either literal or novel metaphorical compliments ("your eyes are morning dew" versus "your eyes are beautiful"). Metaphor users were rated more intelligent and more attractive, the effect was strongest when the compliment targeted appearance rather than possessions, and metaphor users were preferred specifically for long-term relationships. (The sample was Chinese; replication breadth is still limited.)
- The theoretical frame: Geoffrey Miller's _The Mating Mind_ (2000) argues that verbal creativity evolved partly through sexual selection as a fitness indicator—a costly, hard-to-fake, real-time display of cognitive quality. Producing an _apt_ novel metaphor on the spot is difficult, which is exactly why it works as a signal; §4.1's formulaic baseline is what makes departure from formula informative.
- The boundary condition: novelty must remain apt. A metaphor that misfires reads as cliché or as trying too hard—the signal derives its value from difficulty, so failed attempts are penalised rather than merely ignored.
- Principle: in courtship contexts, _how_ you compliment is itself the content. The compliment functions as a work sample of the mind producing it.

### 5. Responses to Compliments

#### 5.1 Pomerantz's Dilemma

Anita Pomerantz (1978) identified the structural bind every compliment recipient faces—two conversational norms in direct conflict:

1. Agree with your interlocutor (preference for agreement).
2. Avoid praising yourself (constraint against self-praise).

Every observed response type is a _solution_ to this dilemma:

| Response type | Example | What it solves |
|---|---|---|
| Acceptance | "Thanks!" / "Yeah, I'm pleased with it" | Honours agreement; risks self-praise |
| Deflection / credit shift | "My mum made it" | Agrees with the evaluation, reassigns the praise |
| Downgrade | "It's really old, actually" | Partially agrees while shrinking the praised object |
| Return | "Yours is lovely too" | Restores symmetry; converts verdict into exchange |
| Rejection | "Oh, it's nothing special" | Avoids self-praise; violates agreement |
| Evasion / ignore / joke | Topic shift; "It's just the light, mate" | Exits the dilemma without engaging it |

#### 5.2 Gendered Response Patterns

- Acceptance is the commonest single response in the Anglophone corpora—around three-fifths of Holmes's New Zealand data—but its distribution across dyads is where gender shows:
- Herbert (1990): compliments from men—especially to women—were the most likely to be accepted, while female-to-female compliments were the most likely to be met with something other than plain acceptance (returns, credit shifts, downgrades).
- Interpretation matters: F–F deflection is not straightforwardly low self-esteem. It is better read as a solidarity ritual—reciprocal modesty plus a returned compliment keeps the exchange symmetrical, which is precisely the rapport frame's goal. Accepting flatly and stopping would _end_ the exchange; deflect-and-return _continues_ it.
- Men more often ignore, minimally acknowledge, or convert praise to humour, especially male-to-male. Within the report frame, gracefully accepting praise edges towards accepting the complimenter's standing as judge; jocular deflection dissolves the hierarchy while acknowledging the content.
- Cultural caveat: response norms are dramatically culture-bound. Chen's (1993) Chinese data found rejection and denigration dominant where American data found acceptance—and Chen and Yang's (2010) follow-up found the same population had shifted markedly towards acceptance within two decades. Response norms are plastic; the gender patterns above are Anglophone-local and time-stamped.

### 6. Contextual and Environmental Shifts

#### 6.1 Goal-oriented versus Unstructured Settings

- Rees-Miller (2011, _Journal of Pragmatics_) re-ran the topic analysis with attention to setting, and the result reframes the whole field: in goal-oriented settings (coaching, rehearsal, classwork, sport), both men and women compliment performance most—the celebrated topic gap largely collapses. Appearance compliments concentrate in unstructured social settings, where they remain predominantly female-to-female. Men complimented other men freely and frequently on skill within activity domains.
- Principle: a substantial fraction of the "gender difference" in compliment topic is really a setting difference, compounded by historically gendered access to particular settings. Change the environment and you change the behaviour—which argues against essentialist readings.

#### 6.2 The Workplace

- Power asymmetry restructures praise. Compliments flow downward more easily than upward; upward praise risks being heard as flattery or ingratiation. Holmes's Wellington Language in the Workplace project documents praise as a managerial instrument, with women leaders on average deploying more positive-politeness strategies.
- Appearance praise is re-coded at work. What functions as affiliative solidarity at a party can read, in an evaluative context, as benevolent sexism (Glick & Fiske 1996)—paternalistic warmth that subtly discounts competence. In evaluative settings, appearance-focused commentary towards women is associated with lower competence attributions and can shade into harassment territory; professional norms have consequently channelled legitimate praise towards performance for everyone, narrowing the gap from the other direction.
- Yet the content of workplace praise still skews. Large-scale analyses of performance reviews (e.g. Correll and Simard's work in _Harvard Business Review_, 2016, and industry-scale text analyses such as Textio's) find women receive vaguer praise and more personality-focused commentary ("supportive", "great attitude"), while men receive more specific, outcome-linked praise tied to business results. The low-stakes appearance/communion skew of casual talk echoes into high-stakes evaluation—with material career consequences, since vague praise is not actionable and does not build a promotion case.

### 7. Conclusion and Modern Synthesis

#### 7.1 Core Findings

- Compliments constitute a gendered economy: women give and receive the large majority in Anglophone data; male–male exchange is scarce and topic-restricted.
- The deepest split is functional, not lexical: compliment-as-solidarity-gift (rapport) versus compliment-as-verdict (report). Frequency, topic, syntax and response patterns all fall out of this asymmetry.
- What lands differs predictably: competence praise carries maximal weight for men (scarcity, agency, status re-certification); for women, appearance praise is simultaneously rewarding and costly, while specific skill-based praise is the more diagnostic and professionally consequential signal.
- Delivery is itself information: against a heavily formulaic baseline, novel and apt figurative language functions as a credible display of intelligence in romantic contexts.
- Context dominates: goal-oriented settings pull both genders towards performance praise; the workplace re-codes appearance praise as risk.

#### 7.2 Digital Drift

- Amplification of the classic pattern: studies of social-media complimenting (Placencia & Lower 2013 on Facebook; Maíz-Arévalo 2013 on online compliments) document hyper-intensified, appearance-centred, largely female-to-female praise—superlatives, emoji, "STUNNING 😍"—ritual solidarity operating at platform scale, with probable inflationary discounting of each individual token.
- New competence economies: gaming and esports have normalised dense male-to-male performance praise ("insane shot", "GG")—low-intimacy, high-frequency competence tokens that fit the classic male pattern while massively raising its volume.
- Norm loosening: younger cohorts show visibly more male-to-male appearance and affection praise ("hype" culture), while body-positivity discourse actively pushes some female-to-female praise _away_ from bodies and towards competence and character. Evidence here is emerging rather than settled—but the direction of travel suggests the 1980s patterns were cohort effects as much as gender effects.

#### 7.3 Necessary Caveats

- Effect sizes are modest. Hyde's (2005) gender similarities hypothesis found most psychological gender differences small; within-gender variation dwarfs between-gender difference on essentially every dimension discussed here.
- Beware Mars-and-Venus essentialism. Cameron (2007) demonstrates how readily small statistical tendencies are inflated into myths of categorical difference. These are learned, context-sensitive, revisable performances.
- The foundational corpora are historically situated: 1980s, Anglophone, largely white, heterosexual and binary in design. Read the patterns as well-documented defaults of a particular time and place—defaults that §6 shows dissolve under contextual pressure and §7.2 shows are already drifting.

### Overview

Compliments are far from trivial small talk; sociolinguists classify them as positively affective speech acts that serve to build, maintain, or negotiate social relationships.[^1] Since the 1980s, research has consistently demonstrated that men and women differ systematically in what they praise, what they want to hear, how they phrase praise, and how they respond to it. This briefing synthesises foundational sociolinguistic scholarship—principally Janet Holmes's corpus studies and Deborah Tannen's discourse theory—with subsequent psychological research on compliment perception, romantic signalling, and workplace dynamics.

### Sociolinguistic Foundations

#### Holmes and the Politeness Framework

Janet Holmes's seminal 1988 paper, "Paying Compliments: A Sex-Preferential Politeness Strategy," remains the cornerstone of this field.[^2] Drawing on Brown and Levinson's theory of positive face—the desire for one's self-image to be appreciated and approved of—Holmes argued that compliments function as positive politeness devices, and that women deploy them far more frequently than men.[^1] Her New Zealand corpus data found that women gave roughly two-thirds of all recorded compliments and received about three-quarters of them, while compliments exchanged strictly between men made up a mere 9 percent of the dataset.[^1][^3]

Holmes traced this asymmetry to differing perceptions of language's core function. She proposed that:

- Men treat language predominantly as a referential tool—a means of exchanging information and making evaluative judgements.[^3]
- Women treat language predominantly as a social/affective tool—a means of maintaining interpersonal connection.[^3]

This distinction explains why a woman's "I love your hair!" functions as a bid for connection, whereas a man's terse "Nice car" operates as a factual, evaluative judgement rather than a relational gesture.[^3] Holmes later expanded this analysis in _Women, Men and Politeness_ (1995), examining hedges, boosters, compliments, and apologies as an integrated system of gendered verbal politeness with implications for women's experiences in education and professional life.[^4]

#### Tannen's Rapport Talk Vs Report Talk

Deborah Tannen's discourse-analytic framework complements Holmes's findings by distinguishing two overarching conversational styles:

- Rapport talk: speech oriented toward establishing connection, empathy, and solidarity—the mode Tannen associates predominantly with women's conversational style.
- Report talk: speech oriented toward conveying information, establishing status, and demonstrating competence—the mode Tannen associates predominantly with men's conversational style.[^1]

Applied to compliments, rapport talk explains why female-to-female praise is unusually elaborate, explicit, and affiliative, while report talk explains why male compliments tend toward brevity and an implicit, evaluative register that avoids appearing overly emotional or face-threatening.[^1][^3] Notably, men in Holmes's data were also found to perceive compliments as less unambiguously positive than women did—sometimes reading them as face-threatening or intrusive rather than purely affiliative, which reflects report-talk's wariness of vulnerability-laden speech acts.[^3]

### Categorisation of Praise: What Is Said

Sociolinguists typically sort compliments into four topic categories: appearance, ability/performance, possessions, and personality.[^1] The statistical distribution of these categories varies sharply by the gender pairing involved.

| Compliment Pairing | Dominant Topic | Approx. Frequency | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Female–Female | Appearance | 61% of compliments appearance-based | [^1] |
| Male–Male | Appearance | Only 36% appearance-based; possessions more common | [^1] |
| Male–Female | Appearance | Men gave women ~52% appearance compliments vs. women's 26% to men | [^1] |
| Male-received (from other men) | Appearance | Only ~22% of appearance compliments men receive come from men | [^1] |

Several patterns emerge from this data:

- Female-to-female compliments are overwhelmingly appearance-focused and are also the most linguistically elaborate, frequently using explicit intensified forms.[^1]
- Male-to-male compliments skew toward possessions (cars, gadgets, tools) and rarely address appearance, reflecting the taboo nature of male aesthetic praise between men in most Anglophone and East Asian cultures studied.[^1]
- Cross-gender compliments show the starkest asymmetry: men compliment women's appearance roughly twice as often as women compliment men's appearance.[^1] Proposed explanations include women's greater caution about appearing "romantically forward," the social acceptability of male-initiated romantic signalling, and a shared cultural expectation that women place more value on physical appearance as a compliment domain.[^1]
- A cross-national study of celebrities found that female public figures were more often complimented on possessions and skill by men, while women complimenting men gravitated toward possessions as the single most common topic—showing that possession-based praise can act as a gender-neutral, low-risk register in mixed-gender interaction.[^5]
- Cross-cultural replication (Turkish, Mandarin, and American English data) broadly confirms Holmes's pattern: women receive and give the most appearance-based compliments overall, accounting for as much as 58 percent of praise received by women in some Turkish datasets.[^1]

### Receptivity and Preferences: What Is Desired

#### Why Men Gravitate Toward Competence Praise

Psychological research suggests men's preference for competenceand performance-based compliments is tied to identity construction around agency, mastery, and status, consistent with report-talk's information/status orientation.[^1][^3] Because appearance compliments are rare between men and men are "less accustomed to respond to or expect admiration related to physical appearance that is not an expression of romantic interest," unsolicited appearance praise from another man is often perceived as ambiguous or even face-threatening rather than flattering.[^1]

#### Women's Complex Relationship With Appearance Praise

For women, the picture is more psychologically layered. While appearance compliments are ubiquitous in women's social lives, experimental psychology reveals a double-edged effect. Kahalon, Shnabel, and Becker's 2018/2020 studies found that women who recalled or received appearance-based compliments (without accompanying competence praise) subsequently performed _worse_ on maths tasks than women in a no-compliment control condition, particularly those high in trait self-objectification (TSO).[^6] This effect held even though the same compliments simultaneously _improved mood_—meaning appearance praise can feel emotionally pleasant in the moment while covertly undermining cognitive self-concept and performance.[^6]

This connects to Objectification Theory (Fredrickson and Roberts), which frames appearance-focused feedback as inducing a form of self-surveillance that redirects cognitive resources away from task performance and toward self-monitoring of the body.[^6] Because women receive appearance compliments far more routinely than men—who are more typically complimented on competencies—the researchers argue this asymmetry may function as a subtle mechanism perpetuating gender inequality in achievement domains, even when the individual compliments are well-intentioned.[^6]

#### Workplace Perception Gaps

A related study examining appearance comments framed as compliments at work found a systematic perception gap between the sexes: women judged appearance comments—especially sexualised ones—as considerably less appropriate than men did, and women believed such comments would make them appear less warm to observers, whereas men believed the same comments would make them appear more competent.[^7] Both sexes agreed, however, that sexualised appearance comments reduced perceived warmth and competence of the target overall, and both rated workplace settings as less appropriate contexts for such remarks than casual, non-work settings.[^7]

### Linguistic Nuances and Delivery

Syntactic and lexical patterns differ measurably between men's and women's complimenting behaviour, based on New Zealand and American English corpora:[^1]

- Syntactic form: Women disproportionately favour the exclamatory pattern "What a [adjective] [noun]!" (e.g., "What a lovely coat!"), while men favour a minimal elliptical structure: "[Adjective] [noun]" (e.g., "Nice car").[^1]
- Intensifiers: Women use boosting intensifiers such as "really" and "very" far more than men, and overwhelmingly direct this intensified language toward other women.[^1]
- Emphatic verbs: American English data show that only women use the emphatic "I love…" form rather than the neutral "I like…", again concentrated in female-to-female exchanges.[^1]
- Explicitness: Cross-cultural Mandarin studies found women use significantly more explicit compliments than men (roughly 80% explicit for women vs. 57% for men in same-gender exchanges), while men favour implicit, indirect forms and are considerably more likely to give no verbal response at all.[^1]
- Vocabulary register: Adjectives long stereotyped as "women's language" by Robin Lakoff—"adorable," "charming," "sweet," "lovely," "divine"—were found by later researchers (Brouwer, Gerritsen, and De Haan) to be used by both men and women, but specifically when addressing women, suggesting the register is gender-of-addressee-driven rather than purely gender-of-speaker-driven.[^1]

#### Creativity, Metaphor, and Mate-Quality Signalling

A particularly striking body of evolutionary-psychology research examines metaphorical compliments in romantic contexts as a covert signal of "mate quality." Gao et al.'s 2017 study, testing the "Scheherazade hypothesis" (the idea that complex language evolved partly through sexual selection), had male participants generate compliments for women in either a dating or a working context.[^8][^9] Findings showed:

- Men produced significantly more metaphorical, appearance-targeted compliments in the dating context than the working context, where literal, non-appearance compliments dominated instead.[^8]
- The frequency of metaphorical appearance compliments correlated positively with men's self-rated artistic creativity and with markers of masculinity (lower 2D:4D digit ratio, a proxy for higher prenatal testosterone exposure).[^8]
- Women rated men who produced metaphorical compliments in the dating context as more intelligent, and this perception significantly correlated with the men's actual verbal IQ scores—but only in the romantic context, not the working context.[^8]
- Women were significantly more likely to want a romantic relationship (71%) with men who produced at least one successful metaphorical compliment in the dating condition, versus far fewer (37.5%) in the working condition.[^8]

This suggests that novel, figurative language acts as a hard-to-fake cognitive signal: because producing an apt, aesthetically pleasing metaphor demands genuine linguistic and creative resources, women can use it as an indirect but reliable cue to a man's underlying intelligence and creativity—precisely the kind of costly signal sexual selection theory predicts should evolve in courtship contexts.[^8]

### Responses to Compliments

Compliment responses must navigate a structural tension first identified by Pomerantz (1978): agreeing with the complimenter while simultaneously avoiding self-praise—two conditions that are frequently in conflict.[^1] Gender shapes how speakers resolve this tension.

- Acceptance rates: Across multiple studies, women accept compliments substantially more often than men. One dataset recorded women accepting 81% of compliments versus men's 62%.[^10] In male-to-male exchanges, "No-Acknowledgment" (ignoring or changing topic) was the dominant response strategy.[^5]
- Rejection and deflection: Men reject or deflect compliments more frequently than women—one study found 28% male rejection versus women's 12.8% "no response" rate in Chinese-language data.[^1][^10]
- Downgrading: Both sexes downgrade compliments (minimising the praise's force, e.g., "Oh, it's nothing"), but the practice is well documented as especially prevalent among women, tied to broader social norms discouraging female self-promotion; contemporary commentary frames this as a recognition/confidence issue with real professional consequences.[^11]
- Reassignment/returning the compliment: Men who do accept compliments often use reassignment—redirecting or diffusing credit—more than outright acceptance, effectively agreeing indirectly while reducing the compliment's force.[^5]
- Elaboration: Wolfson's research found that elaborate, multi-turn compliment responses occur mainly between women, and are most common among status-equal female pairs, reinforcing the rapport-building function of female complimenting behaviour.[^1]
- Cross-cultural contrast: Herbert's comparative work found American speakers accept compliments only about one-in-three times, compared to a three-in-four acceptance rate among South African English speakers, illustrating that gendered patterns operate within—and interact with—broader cultural norms around solidarity and status.[^1]

### Contextual and Environmental Shifts

The appearance-versus-performance gap is not fixed; it shifts substantially with social context.

- Casual/social settings: Appearance compliments dominate, are exchanged more freely across genders, and are generally perceived as lower-risk affiliative gestures—particularly among women.[^1][^8]
- Professional/workplace settings: The frontal-lobe study on metaphorical compliments found men shift decisively away from appearance-focused, metaphorical language toward literal, non-appearance (competence/personality) compliments when the social frame is professional rather than romantic.[^8]
- Perceived appropriateness: Both men and women judge appearance comments as markedly less appropriate in workplace settings than in non-work settings, though women apply this judgement more strictly, especially regarding sexualised remarks.[^7]
- Power and hierarchy interactions: Older sociolinguistic workplace research (Wolfson) found that male professionals felt little hesitation complimenting female subordinates on personal appearance, especially in joking form, with such remarks occurring disproportionately when the woman held subordinate status—a pattern not observed in reverse (male subordinates were not found to receive such comments from superiors of either gender).[^1]
- Performance cost of appearance praise at work: The self-objectification research demonstrates that even well-intentioned appearance compliments in evaluative/workplace contexts can measurably undermine cognitive task performance for both women and men, though the effect is more consequential for women given how routinely they receive such feedback relative to competence feedback.[^6]

### Conclusion and Modern Synthesis

The cumulative evidence, from Holmes's foundational 1980s corpus work through contemporary experimental psychology, converges on a consistent picture: women predominantly use compliments—especially appearance-based ones—as rapport-building, affiliative devices, most intensely between women themselves, while men treat compliments as evaluative, competence-oriented judgements, given more sparingly and often centred on possessions or skill[^1].[^2][^3] Men both give and receive fewer compliments overall, respond with lower acceptance rates, and treat appearance praise between themselves as socially unusual, whereas women give, receive, accept, and elaborate on compliments—particularly appearance-related ones—far more frequently.[^1][^10] Cross-gender romantic compliimenting reveals a distinct evolutionary layer, where linguistic creativity (particularly metaphor) functions as a covert but detectable signal of intelligence and creative fitness, one that operates specifically in courtship rather than professional framing.[^8] Workplace contexts compress the appearance-versus-performance gap by making appearance-based praise less socially acceptable and, per experimental data, actively counterproductive to cognitive performance.[^7][^6]

Digital communication and shifting cultural norms are beginning to reshape these patterns, though rigorous longitudinal sociolinguistic data specifically tracking compliment behaviour on social media remains comparatively sparse. What existing developmental research does show is that gender-differentiated linguistic norms around complimenting are acquired early: a 2023 study using machine learning to analyse Mandarin-speaking children's compliment production found that children as young as pre-adolescence had already internalised gendered expectations—girls, for instance, showed markedly lower positivity when imitating a "masculine" compliment style, suggesting they have absorbed the belief that male language carries a less prosocial, less warm register.[^12] This finding indicates that the Holmes-Tannen patterns are not simply adult stylistic preferences but are actively reproduced through childhood gender-schema development, which has implications for how social media and digital youth culture—saturated with visual, appearance-driven feedback loops such as "likes" and public comments—may be reinforcing rather than dismantling the traditional appearance/competence divide, particularly for young women who now receive quantified, public appearance feedback at a scale unavailable to earlier generations studied by Holmes and Tannen.
