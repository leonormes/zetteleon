It's insightful that you've recognised this shift in your perspective. This kind of self-awareness is a significant step in personal development. Let's break down your reflection:

1. **Core Assumption:** "Moral Absolutism in Response to Harm"  
   * Your original stance was underpinned by the belief that harmful actions demand an unequivocal and severe response, specifically one of complete rejection or exclusion. The idea was that any engagement or lesser response would somehow condone or be tainted by the harmful behaviour. Justice, in this view, was equated with a form of puritanical separation from the perceived wrongdoer and their actions. There was likely a conviction that this was the *only* ethically sound position.  
2. **Emotional Drivers:**  
   * **Root Emotions:**  
     * **Anger/Outrage:** The harmful behaviour (e.g., Trump pressuring Ukraine) likely triggered a strong sense of injustice and anger, leading to a desire for a powerful, definitive counter-action.  
     * **Fear:** Perhaps a fear of moral contamination or compromise. Engaging with those who have acted harmfully might have felt like a slippery slope towards normalising or accepting their behaviour. There could also be a fear that inaction or a softer stance would lead to further harm.  
     * **Desire for Moral Purity/Integrity:** A need to maintain a clear moral high ground, untainted by association with wrongdoing. Rejection offered a way to signal one's own values clearly and without ambiguity.  
     * **Desire for Control/Order:** Harmful actions can create a sense of chaos or a feeling that moral lines are being blurred. A rigid response like total rejection can feel like an attempt to restore order and control, to definitively categorise behaviour as unacceptable.  
   * **Psychological Needs:**  
     * **Safety:** A belief that complete rejection of harmful actors creates a safer environment, either physically, morally, or societally.  
     * **Certainty/Clarity:** An absolutist stance provides a clear, unambiguous framework for understanding and responding to complex situations. It removes the discomfort of nuance or uncertainty.  
     * **Validation:** Taking a strong, morally unambiguous stance can be validating, reinforcing one's own moral compass and potentially aligning with a perceived community of right-minded individuals.  
     * **Agency:** Demanding total exclusion can feel like a powerful act of agency, a way to actively fight against perceived wrongs.  
   * **Societal Influences:**  
     * **Binary Moral Frameworks:** Many societies operate on, or at least publicly espouse, good vs. evil narratives. This can encourage an "us vs. them" mentality where complex situations are simplified into right and wrong, with little room for shades of grey.  
     * **"Cancel Culture" Debates:** While complex, aspects of "cancel culture" can reflect a societal tendency towards swift and total rejection as a response to perceived transgressions, sometimes without a pathway for redemption or engagement.  
     * **Media Portrayals:** Often, media narratives (in news or fiction) simplify conflicts into heroes and villains, reinforcing the idea that villains must be unequivocally defeated and cast out.  
3. **Shift in Perspective:** "Pragmatic Engagement for Better Outcomes"  
   * Your perspective evolved because you recognised a crucial distinction: the *ideal* moral response versus the *most effective* practical response. The new understanding is that while the impulse for rejection is understandable (and perhaps even morally justified on one level), it may not always lead to the best or most constructive outcomes.  
   * **Nuance over Absolutism:** You've moved from an absolutist ("only moral response") to a more nuanced view that considers context, potential consequences, and the possibility of change or mitigation through engagement.  
   * **Focus on Efficacy:** The realisation that "engagement can yield better outcomes" signifies a shift towards pragmatism. The goal is not just to condemn but to actively work towards a more positive result, even if it requires difficult or uncomfortable interactions. For example, in diplomacy, continued dialogue with an adversarial nation, while challenging, might be the only way to prevent conflict or find common ground on critical issues. Similarly, in a personal relationship, cutting someone off for a mistake might be less productive than discussing the harm and seeking understanding or change.  
   * **Potential for Influence:** Engagement allows for the possibility of influencing the harmful actor or situation, an opportunity that is lost with total rejection.  
4. **Broader Meaning & Implications:**  
   * **Personal Growth:**  
     * **Increased Emotional Maturity:** This shift demonstrates an ability to manage strong emotional reactions (like anger or disgust) and choose responses based on effectiveness rather than solely on moral indignation. It’s about moving beyond reactive purity to proactive problem-solving.  
     * **Greater Tolerance for Ambiguity:** Recognising that engagement might be necessary even with those whose actions you condemn requires a higher tolerance for complexity and moral ambiguity.  
     * **Flexibility as a Strength:** The ability to adapt your approach based on the situation and desired outcome is a significant strength. Rigidity can be brittle; flexibility allows for resilience and more creative solutions. Think of setting personal boundaries: sometimes a firm "no" (rejection) is essential, but other times, a conversation explaining your needs and limits (engagement) can lead to better understanding and a stronger relationship.  
   * **Relationships:**  
     * **Conflict Resolution:** In personal conflicts, immediate rejection can escalate issues. A willingness to engage, listen (even when difficult), and seek solutions can often repair or even strengthen relationships. This doesn't mean tolerating abuse, but rather being open to dialogue when appropriate.  
     * **Forgiveness and Redemption:** While not explicitly stated, the shift opens the door to concepts of restorative justice rather than purely punitive measures, allowing for the possibility that individuals or entities can learn and change.  
   * **Societal Dynamics:**  
     * **Diplomacy and International Relations:** Your Ukraine example is a perfect illustration. While condemnation is vital, complete isolation of a nation can entrench harmful regimes, prevent humanitarian aid, or block pathways to de-escalation. Strategic engagement is often crucial.  
     * **Criminal Justice Reform:** Debates around punitive versus rehabilitative justice systems touch on this. Is the goal solely to punish and exclude (reject), or is it also to rehabilitate and reintegrate (engage) where possible, to reduce future harm?  
     * **Social Change Movements:** Successful movements often require a combination of clear condemnation of injustice and strategic engagement with existing power structures or opposing groups to effect change. Boycotts (a form of rejection) might be one tool, but lobbying and dialogue (forms of engagement) are often others.

In essence, your journey reflects a move from a position where moral clarity demanded a specific, rigid action (rejection) to one where moral goals can be pursued through more flexible, pragmatic, and potentially more impactful means (strategic engagement). This is a common and valuable evolution in how we navigate a complex world.
