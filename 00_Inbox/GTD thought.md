because I need to have the full project in mind and understand the why when doing the work. But a single next action
doesn't have that context. So I avoid them. It is my brain. This is why I should do the thinking clearly then trust
those next actions across multiple projects.

Your revelation perfectly isolates the exact mechanism that makes the Getting Things Done methodology function: the strict separation of executive decision-making from physical execution. When you encounter an amorphous task without having fully extracted its meaning, your brain instinctively hits a wall of subconscious resistance because the item feels like a "blob of undoability". By trying to load the full architectural context and the underlying "why" of a project every time you look at a single list item, you are forcing your brain to repeat the heavy lifting of the planning phase. Looking at a Todoist entry like `Terraform: create rg-fitapp-demo-ukw-01 and rebuild ff-demo-mysql` naturally triggers resistance if you feel compelled to mentally reconstruct the entire resource group strategy or the database backup architecture just to start typing. When you are deeply focused on one area, pivoting to a completely different project is jarring if it requires a full mental reboot. In GTD, the goal is to front-load that cognitive labour. You act as the architect when you are sketching out the system and defining the project in Obsidian, making the complex decisions about public access and shared environments. When it is time to actually execute, you shift into the role of the worker who simply follows the instructions. There is remarkable value in prethinking specific actions so that your system can guide you when you have the energy to *do*, but not necessarily the energy to *think*. This is exactly what is meant by widget spinning. A true next action must be the absolute next physical, visible activity required to move the current reality forward. It should require zero high-level planning to execute. Things rarely get stuck because of a lack of time; they get stuck because what "doing" actually looks like hasn't been definitively decided. If a task requires you to recall the entire project history to understand how to begin, it isn't a next action yet—it is still a project masquerading as an action, creating a psychological gap that inevitably leads to procrastination. **Trusting the system** means accepting that your past self already did the hard analytical work. Your only job in the moment is to execute the small, discrete step in front of you. If you complete that step and feel engaged, you can keep pulling the thread; if not, you have still moved the project forward and can catch up on the broader context during your next weekly review. When you look at that Terraform task now, what is the literal, physical next step you need to take at your keyboard to start it?
