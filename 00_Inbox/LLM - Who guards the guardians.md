Aren't Power, Domination, and Coercion just failures of law and politics with the traits they are need to control and mitigate leaking into the very control plane? Humans created the laws and politics so they are bound to incorrectly adopt the failings they are supposed to fix

Your insight cuts directly to the core paradox of political philosophy and institutional engineering: Who guards the guardians? (_Quis custodiet ipsos custodes?_)

By framing power, domination, and coercion as "failures that leak into the control plane," you identify a fundamental vulnerability of human governance. Because human beings design and operate legal and political systems, our evolutionary drives—such as status competition, tribalism, self-deception, and the desire for dominance—inevitably infiltrate the very administrative machinery created to restrain them.

However, when we examine this through your sources, a dual picture emerges: power and coercion are simultaneously a constant leak hazard _and_ a structural necessity for human social scale.

---

## 1. How Human Flaws Leak into the "Control Plane"

Your sources strongly support the argument that the control plane is constantly compromised by human nature:

- The Scaling Paradox: In ancestral bands, humans lived in "reverse dominance hierarchies". Armed with weapons and language, the rank-and-file banded together through gossip and collective force to suppress or kill would-be alpha bullies. But as societies grew too large for informal gossip, humans created formal legal codes and centralized governments (such as the Code of Hammurabi). In doing so, we centralized force into a single state apparatus—which frequently allowed alpha male dominance to re-emerge on a massive, institutionalized scale. As Ian Dunt notes, formal law historically did not challenge brute force; it often legitimised it, converting land grabbed by fists into statutory property rights and physical enslavement into legal title.
- The Problem of the First Defense: As Edmund Fawcett writes in _Liberalism_, liberals recognized that the first defense against arbitrary power—government itself—is a power, creating an abiding problem. John Locke famously warned against creating an unchecked sovereign to protect citizens from petty individual criminals: doing so is like taking care to avoid mischief from "polecats or foxes" while being content "to be devoured by lions".
- Hidden Motives & Systemic Misdesign: Kevin Simler and Robin Hanson (_The Elephant in the Brain_) observe that when humans design institutions, we systematically mistake _professed altruistic goals_ for _real motives_. Because humans are driven by hidden desires for status, dominance, and coalition-building, those who run bureaucratic and legal systems inevitably warp institutional rules to serve personal or class interests.
- Law as a Smokescreen: Thinkers like Michel Foucault argue that formal law and liberal institutions often function as a "smokescreen" behind which state bureaucracy exerts a pervasive, disciplinary control over citizens.

---

## 2. Why Coercion is Still a Structural Necessity

If coercion and power were _only_ bugs or failures, we could theoretically build a functioning society without any coercive force at all. However, your sources argue that a baseline of centralized coercion is an unavoidable structural requirement for large-scale peaceful coexistence:

- The Hobbesian Trap: Steven Pinker (_Enlightenment Now_) highlights Thomas Hobbes's insight that in "zones of anarchy" (where no state holds a monopoly on force), violence erupts not because everyone is evil, but because everyone fears being attacked first. Without an overarching authority to enforce safety, individuals must adopt an aggressive, belligerent posture to deter predators, leading to endless cycles of feuds and vendettas.
- Covenants Without the Sword: As Hobbes famously stated, "covenants without the sword are but words". A disinterested third party with a monopoly on legitimate violence—a state with a police force and judiciary—disincentivizes aggressors and reassures citizens, relieving them of the need for private violence.
- Raw Power vs. Legitimate Authority: Jonathan Haidt (_The Righteous Mind_) and Andrew Heywood (_Politics_) distinguish between raw _domination_ (a bully taking what they want through force) and _legitimate authority_ (agreed-upon rules and rank that perform vital social functions, such as resolving disputes and preventing internal chaos). Power becomes pure domination when it loses legitimacy, accountability, and the rule of law.

---

## 3. The Institutional Engineering Response: "Rules for the Control Plane"

Because human architects knew that human flaws would leak into the control plane, the central project of political philosophy became constitutionalism and the Rule of Law—building rules specifically designed to govern the governors.

- Dispersing and Breaking Up Power: Rather than trying to eliminate state power, liberal constitutionalism attempts to fragment it. As Locke, Madison, and Guizot argued, because power corrupts, ultimate sovereignty must belong to no single person, class, or branch. Power is broken into legislative, executive, and judicial branches so that power checks power.
- The Rule of Law: As Albert Venn Dicey and Raymond Wacks emphasize, the core ideal of the Rule of Law is that _no citizen, king, judge, or prime minister is above the law_. It establishes clear, prospective, and general rules that bind the state itself, serving as an indispensable curb against arbitrary domination and the misuse of public authority.
