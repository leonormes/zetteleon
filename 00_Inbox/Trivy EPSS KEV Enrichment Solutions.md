# **Risk-Based Vulnerability Triage in Kubernetes: Architecting EPSS and CISA KEV Enrichment for Trivy Operator**

The paradigm of vulnerability management within cloud-native environments and Kubernetes orchestrators is undergoing a fundamental mathematical and operational transformation. Historically, enterprise security operations relied almost exclusively on the Common Vulnerability Scoring System (CVSS) to prioritize remediation efforts across container registries and active cluster workloads. However, the operational reality of modern, highly distributed infrastructure has rendered CVSS-centric triage insufficient. With the deployment of continuous scanning mechanisms like the Trivy Operator, security operations centers are routinely inundated with thousands of vulnerabilities designated as "High" and "Critical," the vast majority of which will never be targeted by threat actors in the wild.  
To resolve the deep asymmetry between theoretical vulnerability severity and practical exploitation risk, the cybersecurity industry is aggressively pivoting toward Risk-Based Vulnerability Management (RBVM). This methodology relies on the synthesis of two foundational pillars of modern threat intelligence: the Exploit Prediction Scoring System (EPSS), which offers a probabilistic forecast of future exploitation, and the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency's Known Exploited Vulnerabilities (CISA KEV) catalog, which provides deterministic, historical evidence of active weaponization.  
The Trivy Operator represents the standard for continuous, in-cluster Kubernetes security scanning. However, it is fundamentally designed around CVSS-based severity grading and lacks native, out-of-the-box composite scoring for EPSS and CISA KEV data. This report delivers an exhaustive architectural analysis of all viable solutions to enrich Trivy Operator findings with EPSS and KEV threat intelligence. Furthermore, it addresses the critical supply chain security implications surrounding the Trivy ecosystem and outlines robust data engineering practices for air-gapped and high-volume environments, enabling organizations to achieve high-efficacy, low-noise vulnerability triage.

## **The Theoretical and Mathematical Framework of Vulnerability Prioritization**

Before architecting an enrichment pipeline for the Kubernetes ecosystem, it is essential to deeply understand the mathematical distinctions, predictive limitations, and operational applications of CVSS, EPSS, and KEV. Modern vulnerability triage requires treating these scores not as interchangeable metrics, but as distinct variables in a multidimensional risk equation.

### **The Statistical Failure of CVSS in Cloud-Native Triage**

The Common Vulnerability Scoring System is an industry-standard framework maintained by the Forum of Incident Response and Security Teams (FIRST) designed to capture the principal technical characteristics of a vulnerability and produce a numerical severity score ranging from 0.0 to 10.0. CVSS calculates scores using three primary metric groups. Base metrics represent intrinsic vulnerability characteristics that remain constant over time, evaluating parameters such as the attack vector, attack complexity, required privileges, user interaction requirements, and the ultimate impact on confidentiality, integrity, and availability. Temporal metrics capture characteristics that change over time, such as exploit code maturity, while environmental metrics allow organizations to customize scores based on their specific deployment context.  
Despite its ubiquitous adoption, empirical research demonstrates that CVSS operates poorly as a prioritization mechanism. Academic studies reveal that CVSS classifiers achieve an Area Under the Precision-Recall Curve (AUPRC) of approximately 0.011 when correlated with real-world exploitation data—a predictive accuracy that barely exceeds random chance. The fundamental flaw lies in the fact that CVSS measures theoretical maximum impact and technical severity in a vacuum, entirely divorced from adversarial behavior, economic incentives for attackers, and real-world exploitability.  
Furthermore, the distribution of CVSS scores is heavily skewed toward higher values. A substantial percentage of published Common Vulnerabilities and Exposures (CVEs) receive base scores of 7.0 or higher. Some studies indicate that CVSS labels up to 61% of all vulnerabilities as critical or high severity. Consequently, prioritizing based on CVSS forces remediation teams into analysis paralysis, allocating finite engineering resources to patch technically severe vulnerabilities that adversaries completely ignore, while potentially overlooking lower-severity vulnerabilities that are actively weaponized in active attack chains.

### **The Exploit Prediction Scoring System (EPSS)**

To bridge the gap between theoretical severity and actual adversarial behavior, FIRST developed the Exploit Prediction Scoring System (EPSS). EPSS is a data-driven, machine-learning model—specifically utilizing gradient-boosted trees (XGBoost)—that estimates the probability that a published vulnerability will be exploited in the wild within the next 30 days.  
The model aggregates diverse, high-volume threat intelligence sources to detect subtle patterns in attacker behavior. These inputs include the MITRE CVE list, the National Vulnerability Database (NVD), and extensive data on published exploit code availability. For instance, the model ingests telemetry on tens of thousands of CVEs possessing published exploit code from repositories such as Exploit-DB, GitHub, and Metasploit. The system also analyzes social media velocity, tracking the volume of unique daily tweets mentioning specific CVEs to gauge hacker interest, alongside evidence of vulnerabilities being integrated into offensive security tools and penetration testing frameworks like Nuclei, Jaeles, Intrigue, and Sn1per.  
The EPSS architecture outputs two critical metrics for every published CVE:

> 1. **Probability Score:** A continuous floating-point value between 0 and 1 (or 0% to 100%) representing the forecasted likelihood of exploitation within the 30-day temporal window.  
> 2. **Percentile Rank:** A relative metric indicating how a specific CVE compares against the broader dataset of all scored vulnerabilities. Because the vast majority of vulnerabilities are never exploited in the wild, the probability distribution is heavily skewed toward zero. Therefore, a seemingly low absolute probability score of 0.10 (10%) typically places a vulnerability in the 95th percentile, identifying it as representing an extreme relative risk compared to the global baseline.

The EPSS model is continuously refined, with significant architectural updates shifting from EPSS v1 (launched in April 2021\) to sophisticated subsequent iterations culminating in EPSS v5, which began publishing in June 2026\. While EPSS shifts vulnerability management from a reactive, severity-based model to a proactive, likelihood-based model, security architects must understand its limitations. EPSS scores cluster near zero because exploitation has a low base rate, with exploitation activity observed in only roughly 2.5% to 3% of published CVEs in any given 30-day window. Furthermore, EPSS is a predictive forecast, not an absolute measure of risk or a confirmation of an attack. It inherently cannot account for an organization's internal compensating controls, network reachability, firewall rules, or specific asset criticality. A low EPSS score on an internet-facing system handling highly sensitive data may still warrant immediate remediation, demonstrating that EPSS must be contextualized.

### **The CISA Known Exploited Vulnerabilities (KEV) Catalog**

While EPSS provides a probabilistic forecast of future events, the CISA KEV catalog provides absolute, deterministic ground truth regarding historical and ongoing attacks. The United States Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA) maintains this authoritative catalog of vulnerabilities that possess verified, documented evidence of active exploitation in the wild1. Under Binding Operational Directive (BOD) 22-01, all US federal civilian executive branch agencies are legally mandated to remediate KEV-listed vulnerabilities on strictly defined timelines, making the catalog the single most actionable prioritization signal in the cybersecurity industry.  
The CISA KEV feed is distributed as a public JSON document containing highly structured metadata designed for automated programmatic parsing. Presence on this list operates as a binary indicator: a CVE has either been weaponized by threat actors to compromise systems, or CISA currently lacks the evidence to confirm such activity. The catalog goes beyond a simple list of CVEs by providing vital contextual metadata designed for remediation workflows.

| CISA KEV JSON Field | Operational Significance in Triage |
| :---- | :---- |
| cveID | The primary key used to join the KEV threat intelligence with the findings produced by internal vulnerability scanners. |
| dateAdded | Indicates the exact date the vulnerability was formally recognized by CISA as actively exploited, allowing analysts to measure exposure windows. |
| dueDate | The federally mandated remediation deadline, which serves as a powerful SLA (Service Level Agreement) enforcement mechanism for compliance-focused organizations. |
| knownRansomwareCampaignUse | A critical contextual flag providing advanced threat intelligence. A value of "Known" indicates the vulnerability is actively weaponized by ransomware syndicates. This specific flag demands the highest possible operational escalation, as it directly correlates the vulnerability with immediate financial and operational extortion risks. |
| requiredAction | Describes the specific mitigation or patching action CISA expects administrators to execute, providing clear guidance beyond simple identification. |

### **Advanced Scoring Frameworks and Composite Risk**

The cybersecurity community is actively developing frameworks that synthesize CVSS, EPSS, and KEV into unified decision models. The Key Risk Indicators (KRI) framework, for example, combines EPSS probability, CVSS severity, and Common Weakness Enumeration (CWE) prevalence to construct a multidimensional risk profile. Research demonstrates that KRI re-orders remediation priority by impact and exposure, capturing 92.3% of impact-weighted remediation value and vastly outperforming standalone CVSS metrics.  
Other models include the Stakeholder-Specific Vulnerability Categorization (SSVC), which utilizes decision trees to align vulnerabilities with specific organizational missions, and the Likelihood of Exploitation in the Wild (LEV), which brings a retrospective dimension to vulnerability prioritization. Furthermore, organizations are increasingly adopting runtime reachability analysis through tools like Oligo Security to prove that a vulnerable software library is actually loaded into memory and executing functions in production, definitively proving whether the vulnerability is accessible to an attacker.

## **The Threat Landscape: The 2026 Trivy Ecosystem Supply Chain Compromise**

Before architecting an enrichment pipeline leveraging the Trivy Operator, security teams must address a critical security event that fundamentally altered the trust model of the Aqua Security ecosystem. In March 2026, a highly sophisticated threat actor tracked as TeamPCP executed a multi-stage supply chain compromise targeting the Trivy vulnerability scanner, successfully weaponizing the tool across more than 10,000 CI/CD pipelines.  
The attack vector exploited an incomplete credential rotation following an earlier incident, allowing the attackers to leverage a service account that bridged two organizational units within Aqua Security. The attackers compromised the Aqua Security GPG signing key (ID E9D0A3616276FA6C) and force-pushed 76 out of 77 version tags within the aquasecurity/trivy-action repository, injecting a three-stage credential stealer into the codebase. Furthermore, the attackers published malicious releases of the core Trivy binaries, specifically versions v0.69.4, v0.69.5, and v0.69.6, as well as compromising the setup-trivy action. This devastating vulnerability was formally tracked as CVE-2026-33634 and was rapidly inducted into the CISA KEV catalog due to the widespread active exploitation.  
The technical anatomy of the TeamPCP payload was exceptionally dangerous. Upon execution within a CI/CD runner, the malicious payload dumped the Runner.Worker process memory to extract pipeline secrets. On self-hosted runners, it systematically swept over fifty filesystem paths searching for SSH keys, AWS/GCP/Azure credentials, Kubernetes configuration files, Docker daemon secrets, database passwords, and cryptographic wallets. The exfiltrated data was encrypted using an AES-256 and RSA-4096 hybrid cryptographic scheme before being silently transmitted to attacker-controlled command and control (C2) infrastructure. Insidiously, the legitimate Trivy scan executed normally after the credential theft completed, ensuring that the pipeline output appeared completely benign to developers and security analysts.  
For organizations deploying the Trivy Operator, this incident establishes strict operational prerequisites. The deployment manifests, Helm charts, and container image registries must be rigorously audited to ensure the infrastructure is immune to this compromise. Administrators must strictly pin the Trivy Operator and the underlying Trivy server configurations to known-safe, immutable releases. Specifically, organizations must ensure they are utilizing Trivy binary version v0.69.3, or versions deployed subsequent to the v0.70.0 release, while actively monitoring their perimeter networks for traffic destined for the known C2 infrastructure.

## **Trivy Operator Architecture and the Contextual Deficit**

Assuming a secured, verified deployment, the Trivy Operator represents an exceptionally powerful Kubernetes-native security toolkit. Unlike the standalone Trivy CLI, which is typically invoked during CI/CD pipelines to scan static artifacts and Dockerfiles before deployment, the Trivy Operator is deployed directly within the Kubernetes cluster to provide continuous, dynamic security assessments.  
The Operator functions by utilizing a Kubernetes reconciliation loop. It continuously watches the Kubernetes API server for state changes, such as the instantiation of a new Pod, DaemonSet, or ReplicaSet. When a change is detected, the Operator dynamically triggers corresponding security scan jobs, launching ephemeral scanner pods that inspect the target workloads. The outputs of these localized scans are subsequently parsed and stored directly within the Kubernetes etcd database as Custom Resource Definitions (CRDs).  
The Trivy Operator generates several highly structured CRDs to represent the cluster's security posture:

* VulnerabilityReport: Details the OS-level package and application-layer software vulnerabilities discovered within the container images comprising a specific Kubernetes workload.  
* ConfigAuditReport: Details infrastructure-as-code misconfigurations and compliance violations based on predefined rules or custom Open Policy Agent (OPA) policies applied to Kubernetes resources.  
* ExposedSecretReport: Details hardcoded cryptographic keys, API tokens, and passwords detected within the container filesystem.  
* RbacAssessmentReport: Analyzes the Kubernetes Role-Based Access Control (RBAC) configurations to identify excessive permissions or potential privilege escalation vectors.

### **The Architectural Divergence from Grype and Composite Risk**

While the Trivy engine is universally recognized as a "Swiss Army knife" due to its speed, broad language ecosystem support, and comprehensive vulnerability database (trivy-db), it possesses a critical architectural deficit regarding composite risk scoring.  
A direct comparison with competing open-source scanners, most notably Anchore's Grype, illuminates this limitation. Grype natively supports composite risk prioritization, directly integrating EPSS probability metrics and CISA KEV catalog presence into its standard vulnerability output, generating an actionable 0.0 to 10.0 composite risk score that empowers developers to immediately triage critical threats. Conversely, the open-source Trivy project has deliberately maintained a rigidly scoped focus on vulnerability identification and CVSS-based severity classification. The project maintainers have historically viewed the native integration of EPSS scoring as falling outside the core scope of the scanner's foundational responsibilities, formally categorizing exploit prediction under the broader umbrella of "exploitability analysis" which they argue is better suited for downstream analytics tools.  
Consequently, the VulnerabilityReport CRDs generated by the Trivy Operator contain fields for severity (e.g., LOW, MEDIUM, HIGH, CRITICAL) derived directly from the NVD or vendor-supplied CVSS scores, but they contain absolutely no native fields for EPSS probability or CISA KEV presence. Therefore, to fulfill the objective of true risk-based vulnerability triage, the Kubernetes ecosystem requires the implementation of external enrichment pathways that intercept, augment, and selectively route the raw Trivy Operator findings.

## **Architectural Pathway 1: In-Cluster Metrics Enrichment via Prometheus**

For organizations heavily invested in cloud-native observability stacks, the most direct and least intrusive method to integrate EPSS and KEV data into Trivy Operator findings is through specialized metrics exporters. Rather than attempting to alter the underlying CRD schemas or modify the Trivy Operator binary, this approach extracts the vulnerability data, enriches it in memory using threat intelligence feeds, and exposes it as structured Prometheus metrics for deep visualization in Grafana.

### **The trivy-epss-kev-exporter Implementation**

The open-source community has engineered specialized microservices specifically tailored for this use case, most notably the mstiri/trivy-epss-kev-exporter. This Go-based application functions as a highly efficient bridge between the Kubernetes API, the Trivy Operator's CRD outputs, and global threat intelligence endpoints.  
The operational workflow of the exporter follows a precise sequence. First, the exporter operates as an autonomous pod within the Kubernetes cluster, utilizing standard client-go libraries to authenticate securely with the Kubernetes API server via a service account. It continuously monitors the cluster and retrieves the VulnerabilityReport CRDs generated by the Trivy Operator.  
Concurrently, the exporter maintains internal mechanisms to interface with external intelligence sources. It synchronizes with the CISA KEV JSON feed and establishes connections to the FIRST EPSS API. As the exporter processes the arrays of CVEs documented in the VulnerabilityReport CRDs, it correlates the CVE IDs in real-time, fetching the respective EPSS probability scores and querying its internal cache for KEV presence.  
Finally, the enriched vulnerability data is translated into Prometheus metric formats. The exporter exposes a standard /metrics HTTP endpoint that can be scraped iteratively by the cluster's centralized Prometheus server. The resulting time-series data utilizes extensive labeling to attach the EPSS scores, the boolean KEV status, and the original CVSS severities directly to specific workloads, container names, and Kubernetes namespaces.

### **Operational Advantages and Limitations**

This architecture presents several distinct advantages. It is entirely non-invasive, requiring zero modification to the Trivy Operator deployment itself, thus ensuring that the core security scanning infrastructure remains highly stable. The resulting metrics seamlessly integrate into existing Grafana dashboards, allowing security teams to immediately visualize the highest-risk workloads. For example, an analyst can construct a dashboard panel filtered exclusively to display Kubernetes deployments containing KEV-listed CVEs or vulnerabilities possessing an EPSS score exceeding 0.50. Because the exporter reads directly from the active CRDs, the metric state precisely mirrors the current security posture of the cluster.  
However, the Prometheus-centric pathway suffers from inherent limitations regarding actionability. While Prometheus Alertmanager can be configured to trigger alerts based on EPSS thresholds, constructing complex, conditional routing rules purely through PromQL is notoriously cumbersome. Furthermore, Prometheus is fundamentally optimized for managing ephemeral time-series metrics, making it entirely unsuitable for acting as a system of record for long-term vulnerability management. While dashboards provide exceptional visibility, they do not natively drive automated patching mechanisms or generate stateful tickets for development teams.

## **Architectural Pathway 2: Event-Driven Enrichment Pipelines via Webhooks**

For organizations requiring active remediation, sophisticated contextual routing, and deep integration with Security Orchestration, Automation, and Response (SOAR) platforms, leveraging the Trivy Operator's native webhook broadcasting capability constitutes the optimal architectural pattern. This pathway intercepts the vulnerability data at the exact moment of generation, enriches the payload dynamically, and pushes the contextualized data to external execution systems.

### **Configuring the Trivy Operator Webhook Engine**

The Trivy Operator includes a highly configurable, built-in mechanism to broadcast its generated reports to an external HTTP endpoint. This functionality is controlled via the operator's environment variable configuration, specifically utilizing the OPERATOR\_WEBHOOK\_BROADCAST\_URL parameter.  
When deployed via Helm charts, administrators can configure this feature by modifying the deployment manifests directly, or preferably by passing the values through the trivy-operator-config ConfigMap or a secured Kubernetes Secret. A robust configuration typically requires defining the endpoint URL, establishing an appropriate timeout threshold, and injecting custom HTTP headers to ensure secure, authenticated communication with the receiving middleware.

| Configuration Variable | Operational Function |
| :---- | :---- |
| OPERATOR\_WEBHOOK\_BROADCAST\_URL | Defines the absolute HTTP/HTTPS endpoint where the Trivy Operator will POST the generated reports. |
| OPERATOR\_WEBHOOK\_BROADCAST\_TIMEOUT | Establishes the maximum duration the operator will wait for a response from the receiving webhook before terminating the connection, defaulting to 30 seconds. |
| OPERATOR\_WEBHOOK\_BROADCAST\_CUSTOM\_HEADERS | Allows the injection of static authentication tokens (e.g., X-API-Key) to securely authenticate the Trivy Operator to the receiving service. |

Once enabled, whenever the Trivy Operator successfully completes a scan and writes a new VulnerabilityReport CRD to the Kubernetes API, it simultaneously POSTs the full JSON representation of that CRD directly to the configured webhook URL.

### **Architecting the Custom Enrichment Middleware**

Because the Trivy Operator broadcasts the raw, unenriched VulnerabilityReport payload exactly as it exists in the Kubernetes API, a custom middleware service must be deployed to receive the webhook, execute the data enrichment logic, and forward the results. This middleware can be developed as a custom microservice using languages like Python or Go, or constructed using advanced no-code automation platforms like n8n, which excel at orchestrating complex data joining operations.  
The enrichment middleware must perform a complex, asynchronous sequence of operations to process the incoming telemetry. Upon receiving the JSON payload, the middleware first applies a filtering rule to ensure the payload kind is explicitly a VulnerabilityReport, deliberately dropping configuration audit or RBAC reports if they are not relevant to the current enrichment pipeline. The service then iterates through the array of detected vulnerabilities, isolating the unique CVE IDs.  
To determine if a CVE is actively exploited, the middleware must consult the CISA KEV catalog. The optimal approach is for the middleware to fetch the public JSON feed from CISA's servers on a scheduled, recurring basis (e.g., every 4 hours) and cache it locally in memory or a fast data store like Redis. The middleware iterates through the Trivy findings and cross-references each cveID against the cached KEV data to determine intersection.  
Simultaneously, the middleware must retrieve the EPSS probability score for each identified CVE. While the FIRST EPSS API (https://api.first.org/data/v1/epss) allows for querying specific CVEs dynamically, this approach is highly inefficient. The API returns a JSON response containing the epss and percentile values, but querying the API dynamically for every CVE present in a dense Kubernetes cluster will instantaneously exhaust the FIRST API's public rate limit, which is strictly capped at 1,000 requests per minute. Therefore, robust middleware designs employ a daily synchronization job that downloads the bulk EPSS CSV file, decompresses the archive locally, and loads the entire scoring matrix into a high-performance database (e.g., PostgreSQL or SQLite) to facilitate sub-millisecond lookup latency.  
Once the intelligence lookups are complete, the middleware constructs a vastly enriched JSON object. Each vulnerability record within the payload is appended with the boolean KEV status, the KEV ransomware flag, and the EPSS probability floating-point value. This deeply contextualized payload is then routed to its final execution destination.

### **Executing Remediation and Routing**

The true power of the webhook pipeline architecture lies in the ability to execute automated remediation and contextual routing based on the composite risk profile.  
One prominent implementation involves routing the enriched telemetry to **AWS Security Hub**. Utilizing open-source integration patterns, such as the csepulveda/trivy-webhook-aws-security-hub project, the enriched payload can be transformed into the strictly defined AWS Security Finding Format (ASFF). The middleware ingests the findings into the cloud provider via the BatchImportFindings API, utilizing IAM Roles for Service Accounts (IRSA) for secure authentication. The integration of EPSS and KEV data directly into the ASFF allows AWS Security Hub administrators to dynamically filter findings by real-world exploitability across a multi-account organization.  
Furthermore, the enriched payloads can drive **Automated Patching Systems** such as Copacetic (Copa). Copacetic can be triggered via a webhook to automatically parse the enriched report and directly patch vulnerable container images at the binary level, utilizing buildkit sidecars, without requiring developers to initiate a full rebuild from the source Dockerfile. In this architecture, the middleware acts as a highly intelligent gatekeeper, ensuring that only container images containing KEV-listed vulnerabilities or vulnerabilities possessing an EPSS score above a strict threshold trigger the automated, compute-intensive patching cycle.  
For organizations leveraging advanced commercial threat intelligence, the middleware can also query APIs such as VulnCheck. VulnCheck provides extremely granular signals beyond the standard CISA KEV catalog, such as reported\_exploited\_by\_vulncheck\_canaries (indicating that honeypots are observing live attack traffic targeting the CVE) and vulncheck\_xdb (providing direct links to published exploit code repositories). Injecting this level of intelligence into a ticketing platform enables the SOAR system to dynamically assign priority: a vulnerability with a critical CVSS score but a 0.01 EPSS score generates a "Low" priority backlog ticket, whereas a medium CVSS score actively observed in VulnCheck honeypots instantly generates a "P1 Blocker" incident.

## **Architectural Pathway 3: Centralized Application Security Posture Management (ASPM)**

While custom webhook pipelines provide immense flexibility and control, engineering the middleware, managing caching logic, parsing complex JSON schemas, and maintaining API synchronization requires sustained, dedicated engineering effort. For organizations seeking a comprehensive, turnkey solution for vulnerability lifecycle management, delegating the enrichment process to a centralized Application Security Posture Management (ASPM) platform is highly effective.  
In this architectural paradigm, the Trivy Operator acts strictly as a raw data generator. It utilizes its webhook feature or a dedicated synchronization script to forward the unenriched VulnerabilityReport CRDs to the centralized platform. The ASPM platform itself possesses the native logic to ingest, deduplicate, contextualize, and manage the findings over time.

### **DefectDojo Integration Mechanics**

DefectDojo is an industry-standard, open-source vulnerability management platform licensed under the BSD 3-Clause license, and it excels in this exact architectural pattern2. DefectDojo natively supports parsing Trivy JSON reports and specifically understands the Trivy Operator CRD formats.  
The platform architecture is built upon Django and PostgreSQL, utilizing a robust asynchronous task queue powered by Celery workers backed by Redis message brokers. When a Trivy report is ingested via the DefectDojo REST API, the platform utilizes its Celery workers to manage background processing. DefectDojo possesses native, built-in functionality to automatically pull EPSS scores and CISA KEV data from the upstream sources. As the background workers process the incoming Trivy findings, they automatically deduplicate the records based on the CWE classification, file path, and component hashes, while simultaneously enriching the database records with the exploitability metrics.  
This architecture provides a unified pane of glass where security analysts can view the cluster's comprehensive vulnerability posture, sort the aggregated findings by EPSS score or KEV exposure, analyze scan-over-scan deltas, and manage formal risk acceptance workflows with complete auditability2. By executing the enrichment processes asynchronously in the background via Celery, DefectDojo completely decouples the heavy lifting of API polling and data joining from the Kubernetes cluster environment, ensuring the production infrastructure is never impacted by security processing overhead. Furthermore, advanced features like SCIM 2.0 provisioning and the ability to export the enriched data as CycloneDX VEX (Vulnerability Exploitability eXchange) documents make it a formidable enterprise solution2.

### **Commercial and Open-Source Alternatives**

Several other robust platforms offer parallel functionality tailored for Kubernetes environments:

* **Dependency-Track:** Maintained by the OWASP foundation, Dependency-Track is a premier open-source component analysis platform. While primarily designed for SBOM (Software Bill of Materials) analysis, it can ingest CycloneDX SBOMs generated by the Trivy Operator (often facilitated via intermediary tools like sbomreport-to-dependencytrack). Dependency-Track continuously monitors the ingested components against vulnerability databases and threat intelligence feeds, providing a highly accurate, continuous component risk analysis layer.  
* **Kondukto:** A commercial ASPM that offers deep, native integration directly with the Trivy Operator. Kondukto features a sophisticated "Custom threat intelligence" module capable of automatically augmenting incoming scanner data with CISA KEV catalogs and EPSS signals. It excels at mapping these enriched findings to customized asset criticality profiles, utilizing the NVD API for rapid updates, and triggering targeted notifications via Slack or Jira based exclusively on the composite risk score rather than the raw CVSS severity.  
* **SecObserve:** An open-source vulnerability management system capable of parsing Trivy results and providing a unified overview that allows for both manual and rule-based assessments, effectively supporting external EPSS integrations and providing a lightweight alternative to larger platforms.

## **Architectural Pathway 4: Shift-Left CI/CD Enrichment via CLI Plugins**

The preceding pathways focus primarily on enriching vulnerabilities *after* a resource has been successfully deployed to the Kubernetes cluster and scanned by the Trivy Operator. However, a comprehensive RBVM strategy mandates a "shift-left" approach, requiring that high-risk vulnerabilities be intercepted and blocked before they ever enter the runtime environment or the container registry.  
While the standalone Trivy CLI does not natively output EPSS or KEV scores, the open-source community has developed highly effective plugins to augment its capabilities during the CI/CD build phase. The melmorabity/trivy-plugin-epss is a specialized Python-based plugin explicitly engineered to enhance Trivy scan results with EPSS data directly within the pipeline.

### **Plugin Mechanics and Pipeline Integration**

During a standard CI/CD pipeline execution (such as GitHub Actions or GitLab CI), the pipeline builds the container image and immediately executes a Trivy scan. By utilizing the plugin, the pipeline can enrich the findings instantaneously before rendering a deployment decision.  
The plugin requires Python 3.10 or newer and is installed via the standard trivy plugin install command. It is invoked by piping the standard Trivy JSON output directly into the plugin binary, or by leveraging the \--output plugin=epss flag in the Trivy execution command.  
When executed, the plugin automatically downloads and caches the latest EPSS dataset from FIRST, mitigating redundant network requests by utilizing a local cache directory (defaulting to \~/.cache/trivy/epss) that invalidates every 24 hours. It parses the Trivy JSON output, and for every vulnerability identified, it injects a highly structured EPSS object.  
An example of the enriched JSON structure illustrates the depth of the injected data:

JSON  
{  
  "VulnerabilityID": "CVE-2023-34152",  
  "EPSS": {  
    "score": 0.70203,  
    "percentile": 0.98573,  
    "model\_version": "v2025.03.14",  
    "score\_date": "2025-05-30T12:55:00Z"  
  }  
}

Once the JSON report is enriched, a subsequent validation script within the CI/CD pipeline can parse the output programmatically. If the script detects a vulnerability with a critical CVSS score *and* an EPSS probability score exceeding a defined risk tolerance threshold (e.g., \> 0.30), the script returns a non-zero exit code. This halts the CI/CD pipeline, failing the build and irrevocably preventing the compromised artifact from reaching the Kubernetes cluster.  
While this solution is highly effective for pre-deployment validation, security architects must recognize that it cannot substitute the continuous scanning provided by the Trivy Operator. A vulnerability may possess an EPSS score of 0.01 at the time of the CI build, allowing it to pass the pipeline gate. Six months later, while the container is actively running in the cluster, a proof-of-concept exploit may be published on GitHub, causing the EPSS score to spike dynamically to 0.95. Only continuous in-cluster monitoring, enriched dynamically, will detect this dangerous temporal shift in risk.

## **Architectural Pathway 5: Synergizing Trivy with Kubescape for Runtime Reachability**

While threat intelligence scores like EPSS and KEV provide a macro-level view of exploitation likelihood, they do not account for a cluster's micro-level execution context. Integrating the Trivy Operator with Kubescape—a CNCF incubating project specialized in Kubernetes posture management and runtime security—provides the ultimate enrichment layer: runtime reachability analysis3.  
Operating as a complementary stack, Trivy handles the rigorous identification of vulnerabilities across container images, while Kubescape leverages eBPF (Extended Berkeley Packet Filter) technology at the kernel level to monitor which software packages are actively loaded into memory and executed during runtime3. This reachability analysis differentiates theoretical vulnerabilities resting dormantly on a filesystem from vulnerabilities actively exposed to attack paths3.  
The synergy between these tools is formalized through the generation of Vulnerability Exploitability eXchange (VEX) documents. The Kubescape Operator (version 1.16.2 and higher) can automatically generate VEX documents and store them as Kubernetes API objects, specifically marking runtime-reachable vulnerabilities as "affected" and unloaded packages as "not affected"5. Trivy is capable of natively ingesting these VEX documents5. When Trivy processes a scan alongside the Kubescape VEX data, it filters the output, dramatically improving the signal-to-noise ratio.  
By combining Trivy's comprehensive scanning capabilities with Kubescape's runtime context, organizations can achieve a mathematically rigorous prioritization model. The broader ARMO platform, which underpins Kubescape, natively incorporates both EPSS and CISA KEV feeds directly into this reachability matrix6. Applying this combined intelligence matrix—where a vulnerability is only prioritized if it possesses a high EPSS/KEV profile *and* is confirmed reachable by Kubescape—has been shown to reduce actionable CVE counts by 90% or more, allowing security teams to focus exclusively on verifiable, exploitable risks3.

## **Data Engineering: Operating in Air-Gapped and High-Volume Environments**

Architecting vulnerability enrichment pipelines requires careful consideration of network topologies, data ingestion mechanics, and corporate security policies. Kubernetes clusters frequently operate in strictly controlled, network-isolated, or completely air-gapped environments where direct outbound HTTP requests to external domains like api.first.org or cisa.gov are explicitly prohibited at the firewall layer. Furthermore, high-volume environments processing thousands of distinct CVEs across hundreds of microservices will rapidly exceed external API rate limits, causing the enrichment pipeline to fracture.

### **Designing the Internal Threat Intelligence Mirror**

To ensure absolute reliability and compliance with zero-telemetry mandates, security architects must deploy internal threat intelligence mirrors. This process involves a secured, outbound-permitted proxy node periodically fetching the bulk datasets from the global authorities and distributing them to internal, air-gapped endpoints accessible by the Kubernetes cluster's enrichment webhooks or Prometheus exporters.  
**Optimizing EPSS Data Ingestion:** As previously noted, the FIRST EPSS API is explicitly designed for single-CVE lookups and strictly rate-limited to 1,000 requests per minute. Querying the API iteratively for the hundreds of CVEs typically found in a standard container image is mathematically inefficient and operationally fragile.  
Instead, the internal mirroring system must execute a scheduled daily download of the bulk CSV file provided by FIRST. This gzip-compressed CSV contains the complete scoring matrix for every published CVE. The internal mirror retrieves the archive, decompresses the file, and systematically loads the data into an internal relational database or a high-speed key-value store like Redis. The Trivy Operator webhook middleware then queries this internal datastore exclusively, achieving microsecond latency per CVE lookup without generating any outbound network traffic or telemetry that could compromise the integrity of the air-gapped environment.  
**Optimizing CISA KEV Data Ingestion:** The CISA KEV catalog presents a different engineering challenge. It is a relatively small, static JSON file containing roughly 1,200 to 1,500 entries (as of late 2025/early 2026), but it is updated unpredictably. To ensure the internal enrichment pipeline is utilizing the most current intelligence, the internal mirror can execute a conditional HTTP GET request (If-Modified-Since) to CISA's servers on a highly frequent basis (e.g., every 2 to 4 hours). If the feed has been updated by the agency, the mirror downloads the new known\_exploited\_vulnerabilities.json file and caches it internally for immediate consumption by the downstream enrichment middleware.  
By utilizing robust internal mirrors for both the EPSS bulk CSV and the KEV JSON catalog, the entire vulnerability triage pipeline is rendered self-sufficient. This architecture guarantees resilience against external API outages, prevents rate-limiting failures during massive cluster rescans, and ensures strict compliance with zero-telemetry and air-gapped operational mandates.

## **Real-World Efficacy and Operational Outcomes**

The theoretical benefits of synthesizing EPSS and KEV data with Trivy Operator findings translate into dramatic, quantifiable operational improvements for security teams. Case studies of specific vulnerabilities illustrate the stark contrast between CVSS-based severity and actual exploitability.  
For instance, consider CVE-2025-55752 (an Apache Tomcat vulnerability) and CVE-2025-59287 (a WSUS Spoofing vulnerability). A CVSS-only triage system flags the Tomcat vulnerability as a 7.5 (High) and the WSUS vulnerability as an 8.8 (High). Without further context, an automated system or junior analyst might prioritize them equally or prioritize the WSUS vulnerability slightly higher. However, examining the EPSS data reveals a profound disparity: the Tomcat vulnerability possesses an EPSS score of 0.003 (0.3% likelihood of exploitation), while the WSUS vulnerability possesses an EPSS score of 0.42 (42% likelihood of exploitation). The EPSS data unequivocally dictates that the WSUS vulnerability represents an immediate, high-probability threat demanding immediate remediation, while the Tomcat vulnerability poses virtually no real-world risk.  
Furthermore, vulnerabilities with the maximum possible CVSS score of 10.0 frequently possess near-zero EPSS scores. A critical CVSS score combined with a 0.008 EPSS score indicates that while the vulnerability is theoretically catastrophic, the probability of an attacker actually successfully exploiting it across the internet is practically zero.  
By adopting a synergistic decision matrix that cross-references Trivy's CVSS findings against EPSS and KEV data, organizations achieve transformative efficiency gains.
