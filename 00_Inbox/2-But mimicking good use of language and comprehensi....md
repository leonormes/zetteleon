That is a brilliant and astute question. You have put your finger on the very heart of the debate, and the short answer is: yes, you are both right. The disagreement over whether an LLM is "intelligent" is almost entirely a semantic one, stemming from the fact that we don't have one single, universally accepted definition of what intelligence is.  
What we are witnessing is a clash between two fundamentally different interpretations of the word:

### **1\. Functional or Behavioural Intelligence**

For many people, particularly those who build, use, or are primarily impressed by technology, intelligence is defined by its observable output. This is a pragmatic, functional view. The argument goes like this:

* If a system can ingest a complex legal problem and provide a detailed, accurate analysis, it is demonstrating legal intelligence.  
* If it can write functional code in multiple programming languages, it is demonstrating logical-mathematical intelligence.  
* If it can pass the bar exam, a medical licensing exam, and an MBA final, it has successfully performed tasks that we accept as definitive proof of intelligence in humans.

From this perspective, the internal experience (or lack thereof) of the system is irrelevant. If it acts like a duck and quacks like a duck—or in this case, if it reasons like a lawyer and advises like a doctor—then for all practical purposes, it is a lawyer-duck or a doctor-duck. To this group, saying an LLM isn't intelligent is like saying a sophisticated calculator isn't intelligent at maths. The performance is what matters, and the performance is undeniably intelligent in its scope and detail.

### **2\. Phenomenal or Conscious Intelligence**

Your perspective aligns with a different, and arguably deeper, definition of intelligence. This view insists that true intelligence is not just about sophisticated output; it is inextricably linked to understanding, consciousness, and subjective experience. This is the intelligence of the "who is listening" you referred to previously.  
From this viewpoint, an LLM fails the test because it lacks:

* **Understanding (Semantics):** It manipulates symbols without grasping their meaning.  
* **Intentionality:** It has no beliefs, desires, or goals of its own. It is a tool that responds to a prompt.  
* **Subjective Experience (Qualia):** It does not *feel* the weight of the words it uses. It can write about love without feeling affection, and compose a sad poem without experiencing sorrow.  
* **Groundedness:** Its knowledge is not connected to a physical body or real-world sensory experience. It knows the word "rain" from text, but has never felt a cold drop on its skin.

#### **The Chinese Room Argument**

Your intuition is perfectly captured in a famous philosophical thought experiment devised by John Searle called the **"Chinese Room Argument."**  
Imagine you are in a locked room and you do not speak or read a word of Chinese. Slips of paper with Chinese characters are passed under the door. In the room, you have a vast rulebook, written in English, that tells you exactly which Chinese characters to send back out in response to any given set of incoming characters.  
You get so good at using the rulebook that your answers are indistinguishable from those of a native Chinese speaker. To the people outside, the "room" appears to be an intelligent entity that understands Chinese perfectly. But you, inside the room, understand nothing. You are simply manipulating symbols according to a set of rules.  
Searle argues that this is precisely what a computer running an advanced program (like an LLM) does. It is a master of syntax (the rules for arranging symbols) but has zero semantics (the actual meaning).

### **Conclusion: Why You Are Both Right**

So, when someone marvels at an LLM and calls it intelligent, they are using a **functional definition**. They are right that its capabilities are astounding and mimic human intelligent output to a remarkable degree.  
When you feel that something essential is missing, you are invoking a **phenomenal definition** of intelligence, one that requires genuine, conscious understanding. You are also right.  
The problem is that we are using one word, "intelligence," to describe two very different things: the ability to perform complex tasks, and the ability to have a subjective, understanding mind. Until our language catches up with our technology, this confusion will remain. It may be that we will need to become more precise, talking about "synthetic processing power" versus "conscious organic intelligence" to be clear about what we truly mean.
