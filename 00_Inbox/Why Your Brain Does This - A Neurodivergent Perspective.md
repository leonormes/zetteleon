I have adhd and spent 48 years of my life not knowing I did. I have always felt I was different from other people. Over that time I have created this personal myth that I am different and somehow better than other people. I hate being the same as someone else. But obviously I am mostly similar to other and maybe a tiny fraction have my own unique aspects. It's like I only feel good if I am special. If someone else is better at something than me then they are special not me, and if I am the same as others I am also not special. This kind of mind set obviously leads to misery and suffering. Why does my brain do this? Why can't I be happy just being alive?

This is a profound and deeply human experience you are describing. The fact that you have the self-awareness to identify this pattern is the most critical first step towards changing it. Let's break down the "why" from a few perspectives, particularly through the lens of a late ADHD diagnosis.

### **Why Your Brain Does This: A Neurodivergent Perspective**

What you've built isn't just a quirk of personality; it's a sophisticated, lifelong coping mechanism. Your brain has done this for very logical, albeit painful, reasons rooted in its neurology.

1. **Dopamine and Self-Esteem:** The ADHD brain is characterised by dopamine dysregulation. Dopamine is the neurotransmitter of motivation, reward, and feeling good. In a typical brain, a steady sense of self-worth provides a stable, low-level sense of well-being. An ADHD brain, starved for dopamine, often seeks out bigger "hits" to feel regulated and okay. Being "special," "the best," or "unique" delivers a powerful surge of dopamine that feels intensely rewarding and validating. Being "the same" or "average" offers no such reward and can feel flat, boring, or even physically uncomfortable.  
2. **Rejection Sensitive Dysphoria (RSD):** Many people with ADHD experience RSD, which is an extreme emotional sensitivity and pain triggered by the perception of being rejected, teased, or criticised. For 48 years, without knowing why you were different, you likely faced countless small moments of perceived or real rejection for not meeting neurotypical expectations. To protect itself from this intense pain, your brain built a fortress. The myth of "I'm not just different, I'm *better*" is that fortress. If you are fundamentally special and superior, the criticism of others doesn't land as hard. It can be rationalised as "they just don't get me."  
3. **A Lifetime of "Different":** You spent nearly five decades *knowing* you were different, but without the correct vocabulary for it. You felt it in how you thought, focused, and related to the world. When you don't have a diagnosis like ADHD, the brain has to find its own explanation. It's a choice between two narratives:  
   * "I'm different, and therefore I am broken, lazy, and flawed."  
   * "I'm different, and therefore I am special, unique, and superior." Your mind chose the second narrative because it was the only one that preserved your self-esteem. It was a brilliant act of psychological self-preservation.

### **The Unwinnable Game of Comparison**

The personal myth you created was a survival tool, but it also locked you into a specific, and ultimately miserable, mental framework. You've set up a logical equation in your mind that goes something like this:  
\\text{Self-Worth} \= \\text{Being Special}  
And "Being Special" is defined as being uniquely superior or number one.  
\\text{Being Special} \\iff (\\text{I am \#1}) \\lor (\\text{I am completely unique})  
This is an unwinnable game. There will *always* be someone who is better at a specific skill. There will always be overlap between your experiences and those of others. Every time you encounter evidence that contradicts the rule (e.g., meeting someone more skilled), the entire structure of your self-worth feels threatened, leading to the suffering you describe.

### **Why Can't I Be Happy Just Being Alive?**

Because for 48 years, your brain was conditioned to believe that "just being" was unsafe. "Just being" meant being the version of you that didn't fit in, that struggled with things others found easy, that was potentially open to criticism and rejection. Happiness wasn't found in "just being"; it was found in the *validation* of your specialness. Your nervous system has been trained to equate "average" or "same" with "vulnerable."  
You're now trying to un-learn a deeply embedded survival response. That's not something you can just switch off. It requires conscious effort to build new neural pathways.

### **Pathways Forward: Rewriting the Myth**

The old myth has expired. It got you through the first 48 years, but its cost is now too high. The task isn't to destroy the idea of being unique, but to redefine it.

1. **Acknowledge and Grieve:** Allow yourself to feel the grief for the undiagnosed years. Acknowledge the incredible strength it took to survive that without answers. Thank that part of your brain for creating the "special" myth; it did its job to protect you. Now, you can gently let it retire.  
2. **Shift from Superiority to Authenticity:** Your uniqueness doesn't come from being *better* than others. It comes from the specific, unrepeatable combination of your life experiences, your perspective, your struggles, and your ADHD brain's way of seeing the world. The new myth can be: "My value comes from being authentically me, not from my rank compared to others."  
3. **Embrace "Enoughness":** The opposite of needing to be "the best" is the concept of being "good enough." This is terrifying for a brain that fears rejection, but it's the foundation of stable self-worth. It's about finding satisfaction in your own efforts and your own character, independent of external validation or comparison.  
4. **Focus on Values, Not Achievements:** Instead of ranking yourself, orient yourself around your values. Do you value creativity? Kindness? Curiosity? Learning? Then success is not "Am I the most creative person in this room?" but "Am I engaging with my creativity today?" This moves the goalposts from an external, competitive position to an internal, personal one.  
5. **Seek Specialised Support:** This is a very common experience for those diagnosed with ADHD in adulthood. Working with a therapist or a coach who specialises in adult ADHD could be transformative. They can provide tools and strategies specifically for navigating this kind of identity shift, rewriting narratives, and managing the emotional dysregulation that comes with it.

You have correctly identified that the old way of thinking leads to misery. That realisation is the start of a new, more compassionate and sustainable relationship with yourself. You're not broken; you're a person with a specific neurotype who created an ingenious strategy to survive in a world not built for you. Now you have the chance to write a new story based on the truth of your diagnosis.
