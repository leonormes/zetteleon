The team meeting is a perfect microcosm of this principle in action. Let's break down why the person who talks more is often perceived as more intelligent and competent.

### **The Performance of Intelligence**

In the absence of a direct wire into someone's brain, we are forced to judge their internal state—their knowledge, thought processes, and competence—by their external output. In a professional or collaborative setting, the primary form of output is speech.

1. **The Visibility Heuristic:** Thought is invisible, but speech is not. The person who talks more provides more data points for others to evaluate. Their thinking process is made external and observable. A silent participant, no matter how brilliant their internal thoughts, offers no evidence of that brilliance. Out of sight is literally out of mind, and their perceived contribution is diminished.  
2. **Fluency as a Proxy for Mastery:** This is the core link. We have a deep-seated cognitive shortcut that equates verbal fluency with intellectual mastery. Someone who can articulate their thoughts clearly, structure an argument on the fly, and command a rich vocabulary creates a powerful impression of competence. Their command of *language* is taken as proof of their command of the *subject*. The brain's quick-fire heuristic is: "If they can explain it this well, they must really understand it."  
3. **Confidence and Dominance:** Speaking up, and speaking at length, is often interpreted as confidence. We have another powerful bias that conflates confidence with competence. The person who takes up more "conversational space" appears more self-assured, and this assurance is often misread as a reliable signal of their expertise. They are effectively demonstrating social dominance, which people often associate with leadership and, by extension, intelligence.  
4. **Framing the Narrative:** The person who speaks most tends to frame the problem, introduce the terminology, and set the agenda for the discussion. By defining the terrain of the conversation, they automatically position themselves as the intellectual guide, making others react to their points rather than formulating their own.

### **The Quiet Expert and The Eloquent Novice**

This dynamic also explains two common archetypes. There is the "quiet expert," who may possess the deepest knowledge in the room but struggles to articulate it or get a word in, and is therefore tragically undervalued by the group. Conversely, there is the "eloquent novice," who has only a superficial understanding but can talk confidently and articulately, and is thus perceived as far more competent than they truly are.

The LLM is designed to perfectly exploit the cognitive biases we use to judge each other in a team meeting. It presents an overwhelming performance of linguistic competence, confidence, and fluency. Our brains, conditioned for millennia to believe that eloquent output \= intelligent source, find it very difficult not to make that leap.
