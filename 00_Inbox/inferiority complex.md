It sounds like you're grappling with a profound sense of inferiority and low self-esteem, which are unfortunately common experiences for individuals with ADHD, especially when it has been undiagnosed for a period. The persistent feeling that your state is less than someone else's, particularly those you perceive as productive and reliable 'doers', is a core aspect of these feelings.

Here are some words and concepts that might help you describe this experience:

- Inferiority Complex: This is a term popularised by psychologist Alfred Adler. It describes a persistent and intense feeling of being less adequate than others. This can stem from various sources, including repeated experiences of perceived failure or not meeting societal or personal expectations–something many with unrecognised ADHD can relate to due to challenges with executive functions like organisation, time management, and task initiation.
- Low Self-Esteem: This refers to a generally negative overall opinion of oneself and a lack of self-worth. The shame you describe, stemming from past failures and underperformance, directly erodes self-esteem. When you automatically place others as more valuable, it's a strong indicator of low self-esteem.
- Feelings of Inadequacy: This is the sense that you are not good enough, that you lack the qualities or abilities to meet demands or a certain standard. Your struggle with productivity and the resulting shame likely fuel these feelings.
- Shame: As you've rightly identified, shame is a powerful and painful emotion. It's not just about regretting an action (guilt) but feeling that you, as a person, are fundamentally flawed or bad. The history of underperformance, especially without understanding the underlying ADHD, can create a deep well of shame.
- Self-Criticism/Internalised Criticism: Often, individuals who have faced repeated difficulties or negative feedback begin to internalise these messages, leading to a harsh inner critic that constantly reinforces feelings of being 'less than'.
- Comparison: The act of constantly measuring yourself against others, particularly in areas where you feel vulnerable (like productivity), can exacerbate feelings of inferiority. You see someone who is a 'doer' and the comparison highlights your own struggles, reinforcing the belief that they are more valuable.

ADHD and These Feelings:

It's crucial to understand how ADHD can contribute to these feelings:

- Executive Function Challenges: ADHD often impacts executive functions, which are the cognitive skills needed for goal-directed behaviour. Difficulties with planning, initiating tasks, sustaining focus, and managing time can lead to a genuine struggle with productivity and tangible outcomes. This isn't a personal failing but a neurological difference.
- History of Negative Feedback: Before diagnosis, many individuals with ADHD accumulate a history of being told they are lazy, not trying hard enough, or careless. This external criticism can become internalised, leading to shame and low self-esteem.
- "Masking" and Exhaustion: Many people with ADHD try to "mask" their symptoms to fit in or meet expectations. This can be incredibly tiring and lead to burnout, and when the mask slips or efforts don't yield the desired results, it can intensify feelings of failure.
- The "Doer" Ideal: Society often places a high value on visible productivity and a certain type of 'doing'. When your brain is wired differently, and achieving that ideal is a constant struggle, it's easy to feel devalued and to overvalue those who seem to achieve it effortlessly.

Recognising these feelings and their connection to your ADHD is a significant first step. It allows you to begin to separate your inherent worth from your productivity levels and to understand that the challenges you've faced are linked to a neurodevelopmental condition, not a lack of character or effort.

It might be helpful to explore these feelings further with a therapist or coach who has experience with ADHD. They can help you develop strategies for managing ADHD symptoms, challenge negative self-beliefs, and build a more compassionate and accurate sense of self.
