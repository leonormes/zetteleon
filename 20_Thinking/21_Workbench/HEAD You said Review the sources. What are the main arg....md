Based on the provided sources, the primary arguments for the existence of God and their corresponding counterarguments can be categorised into cosmological, design-based, moral, and historical/psychological frameworks.

### 1\. The Cosmological Argument (The Beginning of the Universe)

- Steel-manned Argument for God: The universe is not eternal but had an absolute beginning at the Big Bang. Philosophically, an infinite past is impossible because an "actual infinite" leads to logical contradictions (e.g., infinity minus infinity). Scientifically, the Big Bang represents the origin of all matter, energy, space, and time from nothing. Since out of nothing, nothing comes, there must be a cause that is timeless, spaceless, immaterial, and personal (as only an intelligent mind fits this description and can choose to create).
- Counterarguments: \* Scientific Uncertainty: Human inquiry into the origin of the universe is in its "infancy"; asserting a "finger of God" based on limited evidence is a form of hubris.
	- Infinite Regress: If everything requires a cause, then God requires a cause ("who designed the designer?"), leading to an unanswerable chain of creators.
		- Ockham's Razor: We should not multiply entities beyond necessity. Scientific theories of the cosmos can function perfectly well without the added "God hypothesis".

### 2\. The Teleological Argument (Fine-Tuning and Design)

- Steel-manned Argument for God: The initial conditions of the universe and the fundamental constants of physics (e.g., gravity, the atomic weak force) fall into an extraordinarily narrow range of "life-permitting" values. Biological complexity, such as the "supercomputer" level of the primordial cell, could not have evolved because evolution presupposes a functioning cell. These features are better explained by an intelligent designer than by vanishingly small odds of chance or a multiverse hypothesis for which there is no independent evidence.
- Counterarguments:
	- Bad Design/Hostility: The universe is overwhelmingly hostile to life, filled with collapsed suns, failed galaxies, and headed toward "nothingness".
		- Evolutionary Prowess: Natural selection is "smarter than you are" and can create organs of "extreme perfection," like the eye, through gradual inheritance without a designer.
		- Wastefulness: A designer who takes eons and requires the mass extinction of 99.9% of all species to produce one "imperfect race of primates" appears incompetent rather than intelligent.

### 3\. The Moral Argument

- Steel-manned Argument for God: Objective moral values (behaviours that are valid and binding regardless of belief) exist—for example, the Holocaust was "really wrong". Without a transcendent, non-human foundation like God, morality is merely a biological adaptation for survival or socio-cultural relativism. God provides the necessary "moral lawgiver" to account for the sense of duty and the cosmic struggle between good and evil.
- Counterarguments:
	- Biological Adaptation: Morality is an evolutionary byproduct of social mammals (seen also in primates and pigs) to ensure group survival and reproduction.
		- Secular Superiority: Secular concepts of justice and law are morally superior to religious law, which often focuses on "self-exculpation" and "guise of forgiveness".
		- Religious Immorality: Many religious texts and dictates (like original sin or sexual repression) confound and warp our natural moral sense rather than grounding it.

### 4\. Historical and Psychological Arguments

- Steel-manned Argument for God: \* Resurrection: The historical facts of the empty tomb and Jesus's post-mortem appearances are recognized by a majority of scholars and are best explained by a divine miracle.
	- Personal Experience: For many, God is a "properly basic" belief grounded in immediate personal experience, making it as rational as believing in the existence of other minds.
		- Predictive Power of Myth: Cultures that hold certain "mythic" images sacred (like the mother and infant) survive and flourish, suggesting a deep "story-based" truth to religious structures.
- Counterarguments:
	- Adaptive Illusion: Belief in a "watchful Other" is an evolved cognitive blemish (a "God instinct") that helped ancestors solve the problem of human gossip by curbing selfish behavior through fear of being watched.
		- Incompatibility: The world's religions are independently "ridiculous" and "collectively incompatible"; they cannot all be right, though they can all be false.
		- Wish-Thinking: Faith is an infantile attempt to meet the demand for comfort and to escape the "fear of death".

### Part A—Critique of the Source Document as a Philosophical Summary

#### A.1 Where it Conflates Distinct Arguments

The source treats "the cosmological argument" as a single thing. It is at least three:

| Family | Key premise | Defender (contemporary) | Crucial difference |
|---|---|---|---|
| Kalam | Universe began to exist | Craig, Sinclair, Loke | Premise is scientific/temporal; needs A-theory of time |
| Leibnizian (contingency) | PSR over the Big Conjunctive Contingent Fact | Pruss, Koons, Gale–Pruss (modal PSR) | Works equally if universe is past-eternal; load-bearing premise is PSR |
| Thomistic (essentially-ordered series) | Per-se causal series requires unmoved mover | Feser, Oderberg | Concerns sustenance, not origination; not refuted by an eternal universe |

These are _not_ variants of one argument—they require different defences, face different objections, and survive different counter-models. Conflating them is the single biggest structural defect of the source. The "actual infinite" sub-argument and "Hilbert's Hotel" belong only to Kalam; the "Ockham" framing only loosely bears on Leibnizian; neither bears on Thomistic.

Similarly, "the teleological argument" in the source mixes:

- Cosmic fine-tuning of constants/initial conditions (Robin Collins, Luke Barnes)—a serious contemporary argument;
- Biological complexity / "cells are like supercomputers"—this is Intelligent Design rhetoric (Behe, Meyer, Dembski), not mainstream philosophy of religion teleology. Flag this clearly: serious philosophers of religion since Sober (2003) have largely conceded biological design to Darwin and retreated to cosmological fine-tuning.
- "Bad design" rebuttals (Gould's panda's thumb, recurrent laryngeal nerve) target the ID version, not Collins's fine-tuning, and the source elides this.

The moral argument in the source likewise conflates an _epistemological_ claim ("we know objective moral facts") with an _ontological_ grounding claim ("such facts require God"). Wielenberg's non-natural realism is the live counter and the source omits it.

### Part B—Contemporary Literature on Each Argument Family

#### B.1 Cosmological

Kalam (Craig/Sinclair)—the source captures the public-facing version. The serious literature now turns on:

- _Premise 1 ("whatever begins to exist has a cause")_: Morriston ("Causes and Beginnings in the Kalam Argument", 2002; "Doubts about the Kalam Cosmological Argument") argues that intuitions about causation derive from things _within_ time and may not extend to the universe itself; quantum vacuum fluctuations and the conceivability of uncaused beginnings (Hume) put pressure on it.
- _Premise 2_: BGV theorem is _narrower than Craig presents_. Vilenkin's own statement (Alexander Vilenkin, "The Beginning of the Universe," _Inference: International Review of Science_, Vol. 1, No. 4, October 2015): "if the average expansion rate is positive along a given world line, or geodesic, then this geodesic must terminate after a finite amount of time"—this is _geodesic incompleteness_, not "the universe began to exist". Sean Carroll at the 2014 Greer-Heard Forum debate: "Where Dr. Craig says that the Borde-Guth-Vilenkin theorem implies the universe had a beginning, that is false. That is not what it says. What it says is that our ability to describe the universe classically, that is to say, not including the effects of quantum mechanics, gives out." Crucially, Alan Guth himself disagrees with Vilenkin: Carroll quoted Guth live at the same 2014 Greer-Heard debate as saying, "I don't know whether the universe had a beginning. I suspect the universe didn't have a beginning. It's very likely eternal but nobody knows."
- _Hilbert's Hotel / actual infinite_: Morriston's symmetry argument—if past actual infinites are impossible, why are future ones (predicted by physical eschatology and standard theology of heaven) permitted? Loke (2017) and Craig have responded; Malpass–Morriston (2020) extend the objection.
- _Personal cause_: Morriston ("Must the Beginning of the Universe Have a Personal Cause?", _Faith and Philosophy_ 2002)—even granting premises 1 and 2, the inference to personhood relies on the dubious idea of timeless agency producing temporally first effects.

Leibnizian (Pruss)—Pruss in the _Blackwell Companion to Natural Theology_ (2009) defends multiple variants: full PSR ("every contingent truth has an explanation"), restricted PSR over the BCCF, weak PSR ("possibly every contingent truth has an explanation"—Gale–Pruss). Standard objections: van Inwagen's modal collapse worry (if BCCF has a _necessary_ explanation, all contingent truths become necessary); Oppy's Hume-style rejection of cosmic-scale explanatory demands.

Thomistic—Feser, _Five Proofs of the Existence of God_ (2017). Oppy's _Religious Studies_ article (2021) is the canonical critique; Feser's reply in the same volume is the response. The Thomistic argument is _not_ about whether the universe had a temporal beginning at all—a critical disambiguation the source misses.

Oppy's systematic critique—_Arguing About Gods_ (CUP 2006) and _The Best Argument Against God_ (Palgrave 2013). Oppy's strategy: compare "minimal theism" against "minimal naturalism" as explanatory hypotheses on parsimony grounds; conclude that naturalism is at least as good on every datum and simpler.

Sean Carroll—Greer-Heard 2014 debate with Craig + "Does the Universe Need God?" (Blackwell Companion to Science and Christianity, 2012). Key moves: (i) "cause" is a high-level emergent concept, not fundamental; (ii) modern physics works in terms of unitary evolution under physical laws, with no need for an external cause; (iii) plausible cosmological models (Carroll–Chen, Aguirre–Gratton) avoid BGV's premise of average expansion.

Fallacy of composition—that the universe has a cause because every part does. The standard response (Pruss, Koons) is that fallacy-of-composition charges only apply when the property in question doesn't transfer reliably; "having a cause" arguably does for contingent entities. Not a knock-down objection in either direction.

#### B.2 Teleological

Robin Collins—_Blackwell Companion to Natural Theology_ ch. 4 (2009), "The Teleological Argument: An Exploration of the Fine-Tuning of the Universe", is the most rigorous version. Uses a "restricted likelihood principle": _P(LPU | T & k′) ≫ P(LPU | NSU & k′)_. The most sophisticated theist position; the source doesn't engage with it.

Multiverse + inverse gambler's fallacy—Ian Hacking, "The Inverse Gambler's Fallacy: The Argument from Design. The Anthropic Principle Applied to Wheeler Universes", _Mind_ 96 (1987): 331–340; Roger White, "Fine-Tuning and Multiple Universes", _Noûs_ 34 (2000): 260–276. The charge: from "this universe is life-permitting" one cannot infer "there are many other universes," because the existence of other universes does not raise the probability of _this_ one being life-permitting. Replies: Bradley 2009, Friederich 2019, Goff 2024 (_Synthese_)—the literature is alive and unsettled.

Normalisability problem—McGrew, McGrew & Vestrup, "Probabilities and the Fine-Tuning Argument: A Sceptical View" (_Mind_ 2001): if the constants range over an infinite space, no uniform probability measure is normalisable, so "improbability on naturalism" is undefined. Replies: Collins, Pruss (_Philosophia Christi_ 2005), Koperski, Luke Barnes ("A Reasonable Little Question", _Ergo_ 2018)—Barnes argues the infinity assumption is dispensable. This is the live frontier of fine-tuning debate; the source ignores it entirely.

Anthropic principle—Weak AP (we observe a life-permitting universe because we couldn't observe any other) vs. Strong AP. Collins's "firing squad" analogy is the standard theistic rejoinder to WAP-deflations.

Bad design—Gould's panda's thumb, Williams's senescence, recurrent laryngeal nerve. These target the _biological_ design argument; modern philosophical teleology has largely conceded biology and retreated to cosmology, so this material is mostly fighting yesterday's war (but it does bear on theistic theodicy more broadly).

Hume's _Dialogues_. The source doesn't reference Hume at all. Philo's objections (analogical weakness, the multiplicity of possible designer hypotheses, the problem of inferring infinite/perfect designer from finite/imperfect product, the Epicurean hypothesis) remain live. Sober's "The Design Argument" (in _The Blackwell Companion to the Philosophy of Religion_, 2003) carries Hume forward into the modern Bayesian frame.

#### B.3 Moral

Craig's structure: (1) If God does not exist, objective moral values do not exist. (2) Objective moral values exist. (3) Therefore God exists. Live targets: both premises.

Wielenberg's _Robust Ethics: The Metaphysics and Epistemology of Godless Normative Realism_ (OUP 2014)—defends non-theistic robust normative realism: objective, non-natural, irreducible, causally inert ethical properties that supervene on natural ones via a "making relation". Directly targets Craig's P1. Reviews in _Ethics_ (Suikkanen 2016) and _NDPR_ take it as a serious live alternative. Major omission from the source.

Sharon Street, "A Darwinian Dilemma for Realist Theories of Value" (_Philosophical Studies_ 2006)—the evolutionary debunking argument. Crucially, it cuts against both theistic and non-theistic moral realists equally if they hold that moral facts are causally inert: evolution selects for adaptive evaluative attitudes, not truth-tracking ones, so on realism we should expect our moral beliefs to be mostly false. Replies: Enoch ("Why There is No Darwinian Dilemma for Ethical Realism", in Bergmann–Kain 2014); Skarsaune; Talbott; Wielenberg himself (third-factor / "common cause" replies in his 2014 book).

Euthyphro + Adams. Robert Adams, "A Modified Divine Command Theory of Ethical Wrongness" (1979) and _Finite and Infinite Goods_ (OUP 1999)—split the dilemma into value (grounded in God's nature) and obligation (grounded in commands of a loving God). The "loving God" rider is supposed to dissolve the arbitrariness horn. Critics: the rider smuggles in an independent standard of love.

Mackie's argument from queerness (J. L. Mackie, _Ethics: Inventing Right and Wrong_, Penguin 1977, p. 38): "If there were objective values, then they would be entities or qualities or relations of a very strange sort, utterly different from anything else in the universe. Correspondingly, if we were aware of them, it would have to be by some special faculty of moral perception or intuition, utterly different from our ordinary ways of knowing everything else." Targets the _existence_ of objective values—i.e., Craig's P2. Notable: Mackie himself in _The Miracle of Theism_ (OUP 1982) conceded that _if_ such values existed, they'd raise the probability of theism—a sophisticated concession the new-atheist literature usually ignores.

#### B.4 Historical / Resurrection

The source presents the resurrection apologetic at roughly the level Craig presents it in popular debates. The actual scholarly situation is more contested.

Habermas's "minimal facts" methodology is a popular apologetic device, not a scholarly consensus. Craig has explicitly disowned the label on the Reasonable Faith podcast episode "An Objection to the Minimal Facts Argument": "You'll never find in anything I've written or said any endorsement of the minimal facts approach or the use of that expression. This is an approach popularized by Gary Habermas…" Craig still uses Habermas's facts but rejects the framing; he prefers a maximalist case that includes the empty tomb on independent grounds.

Dale Allison Jr., _The Resurrection of Jesus: Apologetics, Polemics, History_ (T&T Clark 2021) is the most important recent scholarly treatment. Allison accepts much of the empirical content of the "minimal facts" list (he affirms an empty tomb and post-mortem experiences) but rejects the apologetic methodology, arguing that Habermas dismisses extra-biblical apparition traditions (Sabbatai Sevi, Marian apparitions, Tibetan "rainbow body", parapsychological apparitions of the recently dead) using arguments structurally identical to skeptical dismissals of the resurrection—i.e., special pleading. Allison: "several naturalistic theories can explain the minimal facts"; "Although the Gospels contain mythical elements, they are not on the whole mythological constructs" (p. 21). His "Rainbow Body" chapter is structurally devastating to the "best explanation" inference.

Bart Ehrman has explicitly _repudiated_ the empty tomb. From his blog (22 October 2019): "There is no way to know if there was an empty tomb. There probably wasn't even a tomb to begin with because Jesus probably was either never buried at all or was thrown into a mass grave with other people who were executed by the Romans." See also _How Jesus Became God_ (HarperOne 2014) on Roman crucifixion-burial practice.

Mike Licona, _The Resurrection of Jesus: A New Historiographical Approach_ (IVP 2010) narrows the "bedrock" to three facts (crucifixion, post-mortem experiences, Paul's conversion), explicitly classifying the empty tomb as merely a "second-order fact" because consensus is "far from unanimous". Habermas himself reports only ~75% scholarly acceptance for the empty tomb (from his bibliographical survey of over 2,200 publications on the resurrection in English, French and German since 1975, cited in Craig's "Accounting for the Empty Tomb" in _America Magazine_)—_below_ the "near-universal" threshold his own criterion requires.

Statistical critique: Michael J. Alter & Darren M. Slade, "Dataset Analysis of English Texts Written on the Topic of Jesus' Resurrection" (_SHERM_ 4.1, 2022) shows Habermas's count of ~3,400 scholarly works counts confessional/apologetic authors as critical scholars, uses an idiosyncratic definition of "critical scholar", and samples only those who chose to write on the topic.

Bayesian treatments: Swinburne, _The Resurrection of God Incarnate_ (OUP 2003)—concludes _P(R | E & K) ≈ 0.97_, but this requires the prior _P(God exists) ≈ 0.5_ drawn from his cumulative case in _The Existence of God_. Lydia & Tim McGrew, "The Argument from Miracles" in _Blackwell Companion to Natural Theology_ (2009). Standard critique (Beilby's _NDPR_ review of Swinburne): the conclusion is hostage to the priors, and Swinburne's priors are not independently defensible. Cavin & Colombetti (2013) and Carrier offer Bayesian counter-cases.

#### B.5 Properly Basic Belief

Plantinga's _Warranted Christian Belief_ (OUP 2000) is the canonical statement—a substantial epistemological project the source treats as a slogan. Core claims:

- _De jure_ objections to theistic belief depend on _de facto_ objections—i.e., the rationality of theism cannot be assessed independently of its truth. If theism is true, theistic belief is likely warranted (via the _sensus divinitatis_ and the Internal Instigation of the Holy Spirit).
- _Great Pumpkin objection_: if theism can be properly basic, can't anything? Plantinga's reply (WCB ch. 10): the criteria of proper basicality come out of an inductive procedure on clear cases; the objector who insists on classical foundationalism is committed to self-defeating criteria.
- _Son of Great Pumpkin_ (Michael Martin) and the _parallelism_ / _religious pluralism_ objection (Zagzebski, Quinn, Hick): if the model warrants Christian belief, it equally warrants Islamic or Hindu basic belief, generating community-relative warrant. This is the _real_ live objection.
- Zagzebski's responses in _Religious Studies_ and her edited volume _Rational Faith_ (1993).

The source's treatment is roughly: "some people think belief in God is properly basic, but Great Pumpkin." This is to Reformed Epistemology what "the cell is like a supercomputer" is to design theory.

#### B.6 Major Omissions—Filled In

Problem of evil (against theism):

- _Logical_: Mackie, "Evil and Omnipotence" (_Mind_ 1955)—generally regarded as defeated by Plantinga's Free Will Defence in _The Nature of Necessity_ (1974) and _God, Freedom, and Evil_ (1974), via possible counterfactuals of creaturely freedom.
- _Evidential_: William Rowe, "The Problem of Evil and Some Varieties of Atheism" (_American Philosophical Quarterly_ 16, no. 4, October 1979): "There exist instances of intense suffering which an omnipotent, omniscient being could have prevented without thereby losing some greater good or permitting some evil equally bad or worse" (p. 336)—illustrated by the fawn dying in a forest fire: "In some distant forest lightning strikes a dead tree, resulting in a forest fire. In the fire a fawn is trapped, horribly burned, and lies in terrible agony for several days before death relieves its suffering" (p. 337).
- _Hypothesis of indifference_: Paul Draper, "Pain and Pleasure: An Evidential Problem for Theists" (_Noûs_ 23, 1989)—biological distribution of pain and pleasure is better explained by HI than by theism.
- _Skeptical theist response_: Stephen Wykstra's CORNEA (_IJPR_ 1984), Michael Bergmann's ST1–ST3 (_Noûs_ 35, 2001, p. 279: "We have no good reason for thinking that the possible goods we know of are representative of the possible goods there are"), William Alston, Peter van Inwagen. Counter-replies: Almeida & Oppy (2003) and Wielenberg ("Sceptical Theism and Divine Lies", _Religious Studies_ 46, 2010) argue skeptical theism collapses into global moral scepticism and undermines religious epistemology.

Divine hiddenness (Schellenberg): _Divine Hiddenness and Human Reason_ (Cornell 1993); _The Hiddenness Argument_ (OUP 2015). Roughly: (1) If a perfectly loving God exists, no one capable of personal relationship with God would non-resistantly fail to believe; (2) such non-resistant non-believers exist; (3) so no perfectly loving God exists. Currently one of the most active arguments in analytic philosophy of religion. Standard replies: religious-experience-availability (Howard-Snyder/Moser), greater-goods of allowing search.

EAAN: Plantinga, _Warrant and Proper Function_ (1993), ch. 12; _Where the Conflict Really Lies_ (OUP 2011). Combine N (naturalism) + E (evolution): P(R | N&E) is low or inscrutable; therefore N&E is self-defeating. Critics: Fitelson & Sober, Stephen Law (_Analysis_ 2011—semantic-epiphenomenalism objection), the _Naturalism Defeated?_ volume (Beilby ed. 2002). EAAN is not an argument for theism; it's an internal-coherence challenge to naturalism.

Modal ontological argument: Plantinga, _The Nature of Necessity_ (1974)—_if_ it is possible that a maximally great being exists, then it exists in every possible world (using S5). The argument is _valid_; the entire dispute is over whether the possibility premise can be defended without question-begging. Gödel's ontological proof (Sobel, Pruss). Oppy's parodies ("maximally great pizza"). Useful pedagogically because it transparently exhibits how much work the modal premise is doing.

Religious diversity / Hick: Hick, _An Interpretation of Religion_ (1989). Pluralistic hypothesis: the great traditions are culturally conditioned phenomenal responses to a single noumenal Real. Tension with Plantinga's exclusivism is well charted.

Pascal's Wager: Decision-theoretic; Hájek "Waging War on Pascal's Wager" (_Philosophical Review_ 2003); the "many gods" objection.

### Part C—The Genuine Cruxes

For each family, the _single load-bearing disagreement_ a serious epistemologist should focus on:

| Family | Surface debate (source level) | Actual crux |
|---|---|---|
| Cosmological | Did the universe begin? Big Bang etc. | (Kalam) Is the A-theory of time true _and_ do the causal principles familiar from intra-temporal cases extend to the universe-as-a-whole? (Leibnizian) Does the PSR hold for the BCCF, given the modal-collapse worry? |

## Part B—Critique of the Ontological Coverage

### B What's Right

Alex presents Anselm's _Proslogion_ II argument roughly faithfully:

- Define God as "that than which nothing greater can be conceived" (TTWNGCBC)
- Existence-in-reality > existence-in-mind-only
- Therefore the greatest conceivable being must exist in reality

His response to Steven's "greatest conceivable unicorn" objection—that the argument operates over _universal_ property-maximisation, not species-specific maximisation—is a defensible reading of Anselm. Unicorns are essentially limited (they're a kind of horse with a horn); the GCB has no essential limits. This is Anselm's own response to Gaunilo's Lost Island, though Alex doesn't cite it.

### B What's Missing

Three substantial gaps, in increasing order of importance.

1. Gaunilo's Lost Island (c. 1078)—the original parody. Not the unicorn. The "perfect island" objection. Anselm's reply _in Proslogion_ III actually shifts to necessary existence as the relevant great-making property—which Alex doesn't reach. Mentioning the unicorn without Gaunilo is like discussing Kalam without mentioning Hilbert's Hotel.

2. Kant's "existence is not a predicate" (_Critique of Pure Reason_, A592/B620, 1781). This is the load-bearing objection in the history of the argument. Roughly: when we say "X exists", we are not ascribing a property to X; we are positing the instantiation of the concept of X. So "exists in reality" is not on the list of great-making properties to be maximised at all. Frege and Russell later formalised this: existence is a _second-order_ predicate (a property of concepts, "is instantiated"), not a _first-order_ predicate (a property of objects).

If Kant is right, the entire Anselmian move collapses at step two. The fact that neither Alex nor Steven raises this is the most striking feature of the discussion.

3. Plantinga's modal ontological argument (_The Nature of Necessity_, 1974). The form the argument actually takes in contemporary philosophy of religion. Roughly:

- Define a "maximally great being" as one that is maximally excellent in every possible world
- It is _possible_ that a maximally great being exists
- Therefore (using S5 modal logic) a maximally great being exists in every possible world, including this one

The argument is valid. The entire dispute is over premise 2—whether the _possibility_ premise can be defended without question-begging. Oppy's "maximally great pizza" parodies are the standard rebuttal.

This is pedagogically valuable for your epistemology project: it transparently shows that the argument's force depends entirely on a single modal intuition. The "lesson" the ontological argument actually teaches is about the limits of modal reasoning, not about God.

### B The "scope of properties" Move, Examined

Alex's reply to the unicorn—that "sharper horn" is a unicorn-specific property whereas "exists in reality" is a universal one—assumes precisely what Kant denies: that existence is in the property-space at all. So the move is internally consistent within the Anselmian frame but cannot survive Kant. Worth a dedicated claim card with that exact crux.

## Part C—The Teleological Recap (Briefer)

Most of what's said here you can link to existing cards from the previous critique. Three points specific to this source:

### C Steven's Argument is Paley, not Collins

"Sheer complexity from Big Bang to consciousness implies underlying energy/intelligence" is a _combined_ biological-and-cosmological design intuition. The literature distinguishes these, and the distinction matters because:

- Biological design → conceded to evolution (Sober 2003, and Alex's own response)
- Cosmological fine-tuning → still live (Collins, Barnes vs. McGrew normalisability, Hacking/White inverse-gambler)

Steven runs them together. Alex's counter only addresses the biological half.

### C Alex's CPU Analogy is Rhetorically Clever but Philosophically Inert

The move: "CPUs look magical but are reducible to physics, therefore maybe consciousness is too."

The problem: this is an observation about emergent complexity, not a counter to the design argument. A fine-tuner could happily concede:

> "Yes, CPUs reduce to physics. They reduce to physics because the _laws of physics_ permit such reduction. The fine-tuning argument is asking why those laws are what they are."

The CPU example presupposes a substrate of physical laws that already permit silicon to conduct, transistors to switch, and information to be physically realised. Those laws are exactly the _explanandum_ of the cosmic teleological argument. Alex hasn't engaged the argument; he's restated the materialist hypothesis.

This is a useful card on its own: "Emergence-of-complexity observations don't rebut fine-tuning."

### C The Dawkins-style "watch evolution" Reply

"It would be like finding a watch with a multi-billion-year history of watches reproducing with variations"—this is the standard Dawkins/_Blind Watchmaker_ response. It works against Paley's biological argument and was decisive against it in the 19th–20th centuries. It does not touch:

- Cosmic fine-tuning (no Darwinian mechanism for the constants of physics; Lee Smolin's cosmological natural selection is the speculative attempt)
- The origin of life pre-replication (where natural selection has no traction by definition)
- The laws of physics themselves

So the response is correct but local. A serious decomposition should split it into "rebuts Paley-biological ✓" and "does not rebut Collins-cosmological ✗".
