### The Architecture of Procrastination

To build a strong mental model of task initiation, it is essential to reframe what procrastination actually is. It is not a symptom of laziness or a character flaw; rather, procrastination is a mechanism for coping with the anxiety associated with starting or completing a task. When the brain anticipates that a project will be overwhelming, boring, or difficult, it seeks temporary relief from that stress by avoiding the work entirely.

For the ADHD brain, this friction is magnified. The neurological intolerance to boredom acts like kryptonite. The mind recoils from under-stimulating tasks, disappearing into a fervent search for immediate stimulation. This creates a vicious cycle: perfectionistic demands or overwhelming expectations lead to a fear of failure, which triggers procrastination, subsequent self-criticism, and even greater anxiety.

### The Friction of Traditional "Next Actions"

The traditional _Getting Things Done_ (GTD) methodology combats workflow stalls by identifying the "next action"—defined as the next physical, visible activity required to move a situation toward completion. While identifying this physical step is intellectually sound, it can still present a formidable wall for chronic procrastinators.

The problem arises when a defined next action still carries too much cognitive weight. If an action feels like a heavy obligation, it activates an internal resistance. Traditional tasks often feel too large because the ADHD mind inherently struggles with time management and executive function, making it difficult to gauge how much energy a "next action" will actually require.

### The "Starter Task" Paradigm

To successfully bypass this neurological blockade, the mental model must shift from traditional "next actions" to "starter tasks." A starter task is a micro-action designed specifically to neutralise the fear of failure and the terror of being overwhelmed.

Rather than focusing on completing a unit of work, a starter task requires zero mental horsepower. It relies on the psychological principle of making "the slightest move". By reducing the amount of pain and threat associated with the work, the brain is tricked into beginning.

If a traditional next action is "Draft the budget proposal," the starter task is simply "Open the spreadsheet application." The goal is exclusively to cross the threshold of initiation. Once the inertia is broken and the starter task is executed, the natural human drive for fruitful activity often takes over. You do not need to feel ready or motivated to execute a starter task; the secret is to start before you feel ready.

### Visualising the Work

An invisible task is an unmanageable task. When we cannot see our work, our options are obscured. For a mind that struggles to hold multiple moving parts in working memory, unvisualised commitments create mental overload and stress.

To solidify this mental model, your starter tasks must be extracted from your head and placed into a trusted, visible system.

By coupling the radical simplicity of starter tasks with a highly visible tracking system, you remove the emotional weight of getting started. You no longer have to manage a sprawling project; you only have to execute one microscopic starter task at a time.

Ultimately, a primitive GTD model treats tools as disposable means to an end. The goal is never to build the perfect system; the goal is simply to clear the runway so you can take the next smallest step.

### 2. The Project: The Outcome Vector

A project is not an action; it is a destination. In a primitive, high-friction model, a project is strictly defined as any commitment requiring more than one step to complete.

- Nature: Conceptual and directional.
- Function: Alignment and boundary-setting.
- Example: "Car passes its annual MOT test."
- System Location: A project requires an environment built for holding disparate information and tracking outcomes, such as a dedicated, flat-text note in Obsidian. It serves as the container for the actions required to achieve it.

### 3. The Next Action: The Physical Requirement

Coined by the Getting Things Done (GTD) methodology, a next action translates the abstract "task" into the physical realm. It defines the absolute next physical activity required to move a project forward.

- Nature: Logical and mechanical.
- Function: Translation.
- Example: "Call the local garage on 01632 960123 to book an MOT."
- System Location: The active execution list.
- The Limitation: A next action describes what the _world_ requires to move the project forward. However, it does not account for what the _brain_ requires to initiate the movement. It often assumes a baseline level of motivation or dopamine that may not be present.

### 4. The Starter Task: The Psychological Catalyst

A starter task is a micro-action. It is a further refinement of the next action, explicitly engineered to bypass neurological friction and executive dysfunction.

- Nature: Behavioural and microscopic.
- Function: Initiation and momentum generation.
- Example: "Unlock phone and open the contacts app."
- System Location: The immediate focal point of your attention at the exact moment of execution.
- The Advantage: While the next action is a logical step, the starter task is a psychological trick. It requires zero mental horsepower. It strips away the weight of the "task," the pressure of the "project," and the effort of the "next action," leaving only the slightest physical movement.

### How They Fit Together: The Pipeline of Clarification

To conceptualise how these entities interact, view them as a pipeline that filters commitments from high ambiguity down to zero friction:

1. Capture: An ambiguous thought (Task) is captured into an inbox to prevent it from consuming working memory.
2. Clarification: You look at the task. You recognise it requires multiple steps, so you elevate it to a Project (e.g., documenting the desired outcome in an Obsidian note).
3. Translation: You determine the mechanical step required to move the project forward. This becomes your Next Action.
4. Execution: When you sit down to do the work, you do not look at the project, and you do not try to summon the energy for the next action. You define the Starter Task—the most primitive physical movement—and you execute only that.

By categorising your work through this ontological lens, the system does the heavy cognitive lifting. You are no longer managing overwhelming obligations; you are simply maintaining a pipeline that turns vague intentions into frictionless, physical starting points.
