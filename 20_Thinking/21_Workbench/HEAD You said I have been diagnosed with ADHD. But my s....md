---

## 1\. Social Communication and Interaction

Clinicians aren't just looking for "shyness." They are looking for how your brain processes the "unwritten rules" of socialising. This includes:

- Social-Emotional Reciprocity: This is the natural "give and take" of conversation. Clinicians look at whether you struggle to share interests, fail to initiate interactions, or find it difficult to keep a back-and-forth flow going.
- Non-verbal Communication: This involves how you use eye contact, facial expressions, and gestures. It's not always about _avoiding_ eye contact; it can also be about using it in a way that feels manual or forced rather than automatic.
- Relationship Management: Difficulty adjusting your behaviour to suit various social contexts (e.g., talking to a boss vs. a friend) or having trouble making or maintaining friendships.

## 2\. Restricted and Repetitive Patterns

This is where the "stereotypes" usually come from, but the clinical reality is often more subtle:

- Repetitive Movements or Speech: This is often called "stimming." It could be hand-flapping, but it could also be clicking a pen, pacing, or repeating certain phrases (echolalia).
- Insistence on Sameness: A strong preference for routines and a genuine sense of distress when things change unexpectedly. This might manifest as eating the same meal every day or needing to take the exact same route to work.
- Highly Fixed Interests: An intensity of focus on a specific topic that is unusual. While ADHD "hyperfocus" jumps from thing to thing, autistic "special interests" often remain constant for years.
- Sensory Processing: Being overor under-sensitive to things like the smell of a perfume, the texture of clothing tags, or the hum of a refrigerator.

## The "AuDHD" Overlap

Since you already have an ADHD diagnosis, it's worth noting that ADHD and Autism have a significant overlap. This is often nicknamed "AuDHD."

Sometimes, one condition can "mask" the other. For example, your ADHD might crave novelty and excitement, while the potential autistic side might crave routine. This internal tug-of-war can make you feel like you don't "fit" the standard description of either perfectly.

### Stereotypes vs. Clinical Reality

Your sister might be seeing "executive dysfunction" (struggling to start tasks) or "sensory overload" and assuming it's autism, when those are also very common in ADHD.

> Note: A clinical diagnosis also requires that these traits have been present since early childhood (even if they weren't noticed then) and that they significantly impact your daily life.

## The "Social Battery" vs. The "Social Manual"

There is a massive difference between finding socialising tiring and finding it confusing.

- Autism (The Social Manual): For many autistic people, socialising is hard because they are manually decoding body language, tone, and unwritten rules that others seem to "just get." It's like trying to speak a second language you haven't quite mastered.
- ADHD (The Social Battery): For those with ADHD, socialising is hard work because it requires an immense amount of executive function. You are likely working hard to stay focused on the topic, filtering out background noise, suppressing the urge to interrupt, and managing your own energy levels. You understand the "rules" perfectly; it just takes a lot of mental fuel to follow them.

## Small Talk and the "Dopamine Gap"

Disliking small talk because it is boring is a very common ADHD experience. ADHD brains are often "interest-based"—they crave stimulation and dopamine. Small talk about the weather or "how was your weekend" offers almost zero neurological reward.

While an autistic person might struggle with small talk because they don't see the _purpose_ of it or don't know how to navigate the "script," an ADHD person usually knows exactly how to do it—they just find it excruciatingly dull and would rather skip to a "deep dive" or a more stimulating topic.

## Novelty-Seeking vs. Sameness

This is perhaps the biggest "clash" between the two conditions. One of the core diagnostic criteria for autism is a "strong preference for sameness" and distress over changes in routine.

ADHD is almost the polar opposite. The ADHD brain often thrives on novelty and change.

- If you find yourself constantly rearranging your furniture, picking up new hobbies, or getting "itchy" when life feels too predictable, that points very strongly away from the typical autistic profile and right into the heart of ADHD.
- Your sister might be seeing your "social exhaustion" and mislabelling it as "social withdrawal," which is a common outside perspective error.

## The Role of Empathy and "Reading" People

Being highly sensitive to another person's reaction is sometimes called Emotional Hyper-vigilance. In ADHD, this can be quite common—you are so tuned in to the other person's vibes that you might even over-analyse them. This is quite different from the "social-emotional reciprocity" challenges looked for in an autism assessment, where a person might miss those subtle cues entirely.

> Perspective: If you can read the room but just find the room exhausting, it's much more likely to be the ADHD "tax" on your energy than a second diagnosis.
