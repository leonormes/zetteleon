Claude

You're circling several overlapping ideas, and yes—semiotics is the umbrella field. But there are more precise names for the specific things you're fumbling at:

The core distinctions

- Signifier vs. signified (Saussure): the word "cat" (sound/marks) vs. the _concept_ of a cat. His key insight: the link is arbitrary—nothing cat-like about the letters c-a-t. Different languages prove this daily.
- The map–territory relation (Korzybski, _general semantics_): "The word is not the thing; the map is not the territory." This is _exactly_ your "we conflate words with what they point to" observation. The error itself is sometimes called reification—treating the label as if it were the object.
- Sense vs. reference (Frege): "the morning star" and "the evening star" both refer to Venus, but carry different _senses_. Labels do more work than mere pointing.

Why communication works at all

Your amazement is well-founded. A word like "cat" is doing _compression_—it triggers a whole learned model in the listener's head (whiskers, behaviour, the neighbour's tabby). Communication succeeds not because words carry meaning, but because we share roughly similar internal models that the word _activates_. Wittgenstein went further: meaning isn't in the word at all, but in its use within a shared "language game".

Concrete next step: read the Wikipedia article on _"Map–territory relation"_—it's short, links to all the thinkers above, and is the closest single label for your specific observation. If it hooks you, Chandler's _Semiotics: The Basics_ is the standard gentle entry point.

Claude

Extremely common—and well-studied. You've moved from semiotics into cognitive psychology, and the phenomenon has a precise name:

The Illusion of Explanatory Depth (IoED)

Rozenblit & Keil (2002) showed that people confidently rate their understanding of everyday things—zips, flush toilets, bicycles—then collapse when asked to actually _explain_ the mechanism step by step. Ratings of self-assessed understanding drop sharply after the attempt. The label "zip" felt like knowledge; it was only familiarity.

Related findings, all pointing the same way:

- Fluency illusion: ease of _recognising_ material (re-reading notes, nodding along to a lecture) is mistaken for ability to _reproduce_ it. This is why passive revision fails and retrieval practice works.
- The Knowledge Illusion (Sloman & Fernbach): we systematically mistake knowledge stored _in the community_ for knowledge in our own heads. You "know" how a toilet works because _someone_ does, and the word gives you a handle on their knowledge.
- Feynman's framing: _"knowing the name of something"_ vs _"knowing something"_—he considered confusing the two the cardinal sin of education.

So it's not a fringe error—it appears to be the default state. Vocabulary is a compression scheme, and the brain doesn't flag that it's holding a pointer rather than the data. It usually only surfaces when you try to _generate_—explain, teach, build, predict.

The encouraging flip side: the fog-cutting feeling you describe is real and testable. The IoED studies suggest a reliable probe: pick something you believe you understand and write a causal, step-by-step explanation with no gaps. Where you stall is exactly where the label was masquerading as the model.

The trait has a name: Need for Cognition (NFC)

Cacioppo & Petty (1982) identified a stable personality dimension: how much a person _enjoys and seeks out_ effortful thinking. High-NFC people don't deliberate because they decide to—they find unresolved questions mildly aversive and resolution intrinsically rewarding. It correlates with what psychologists call epistemic curiosity, which comes in two flavours: _interest-type_ (joy of discovery) and _deprivation-type_—the itch of a knowledge gap that won't let go until closed. Your 2:30 am questioning sounds like deprivation-type: Loewenstein's "information gap" theory says curiosity behaves like hunger or an open loop, not like a choice. That matches your "I'm not deciding to do this" exactly.
