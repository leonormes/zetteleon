## The US Founders and the Constitution

Short answer: They were clever, but mostly in a practical way. The Constitution (1787) was not mainly a reaction to the British monarchy. It was a reaction to their own first system, the Articles of Confederation, which wasn't working. The break with Britain was the 1776 Declaration of Independence, which came eleven years earlier. A lot of the Constitution's design is actually _borrowed_ from British tradition. For its time it was genuinely radical in how it set up government, and deeply compromised on slavery and on who counted as a citizen.

---

### 1. Who Were They?

There are two overlapping groups:

- "Founding Fathers" is the broad term: the leaders of the Revolution and independence, including Jefferson, John Adams, Samuel Adams and John Hancock.
- "The Framers" are the narrower group: the 55 delegates who met in Philadelphia in 1787, of whom 39 signed. The key figures:
    - James Madison: chief designer, often called the "father of the Constitution".
    - Alexander Hamilton: pushed for a strong central government.
    - George Washington: chaired the convention. His presence gave it legitimacy more than ideas.
    - Benjamin Franklin: aged 81, the elder statesman and peacemaker.
    - Gouverneur Morris: wrote the final wording, including the preamble ("We the People…").
    - Roger Sherman: brokered the deal that gave every state two Senators.
    - George Mason and Elbridge Gerry: ultimately refused to sign, influencing the subsequent push for a Bill of Rights.

A detail people often miss: Jefferson and John Adams weren't there. Both were abroad as diplomats, in Paris and London.

### 2. What Drove Them?

The cause was a government failing, not tyranny.

- The Articles of Confederation emerged in 1777 in the midst of the Revolutionary War. They set up a deliberately weak centre, because the states had just fought a war against a powerful central authority.
- That overcorrection failed. The Articles lacked authority to tax, regulate trade, or enforce laws effectively, which led to economic instability and interstate conflicts.
- No provisions were made for an executive branch to enforce the laws nor for a national court system to interpret them.
- What triggered action was Shays' Rebellion (1786–87), an armed uprising by indebted farmers in Massachusetts that the national government was powerless to deal with. That frightened the elites.
- The convention also overstepped its brief. It was convened to revise the Articles of Confederation, but the delegates threw the Articles out and wrote a new constitution from scratch.

### 3. Where Did the Ideas come From?

|Source|What they took from it|
|---|---|
|John Locke|Natural rights, and the idea that government rests on the consent of the governed|
|Montesquieu|Separation of powers into legislative, executive and judicial branches|
|David Hume|The argument that a _large_ republic could work. Madison used this in Federalist No. 10.|
|Classical republics (Rome, Polybius, Cicero)|A "mixed constitution", plus warnings about how republics decay into tyranny|
|English constitutional tradition|Magna Carta, the 1689 Bill of Rights, habeas corpus, jury trial, a two-chamber legislature|
|Colonial charters and state constitutions|Practical experience: Massachusetts' 1780 constitution was a direct model|
|Failed confederations (Dutch Republic, Swiss cantons, ancient Greek leagues)|Examples of what _not_ to do|

The 18th-century statesmen who met in Philadelphia were adherents of Montesquieu's concept of the balance of power in politics. This principle was supported by colonial experience and strengthened by the writings of John Locke, with which most of the delegates were familiar.

There's an irony here. Montesquieu built his theory partly on an idealised reading of the _British_ constitution. So the Americans rejected the monarch while keeping much of the British system's design.

One claim to be cautious about: you'll sometimes read that the Iroquois Confederacy directly inspired the Constitution. The US Congress passed a resolution saying so in 1988, but most historians think the direct influence is weak and the claim is overstated.

### 4. Was it a Reaction to Britain and the Monarchy?

Partly, but less than people assume.

- Where Britain shows: Congress is limited to listed powers. There's a ban on titles of nobility. The President is elected for a fixed term and can be impeached. The delegates were fearful of standing armies. All of these reflect a fear of a king-like ruler.
- The bigger fear was the opposite problem. They worried about too much democracy: state legislatures cancelling debts and printing money. Madison's main concern was "factions", meaning majorities oppressing minorities, especially property owners.
- So it was designed against two dangers at once: tyranny from the top, and mob rule from below. That's why there are so many checks, including an indirectly elected Senate and the Electoral College.

### 5. Was it Good and Progressive for Its Time?

#### Genuinely Radical

- A written constitution with the people as the source of authority. It was ratified by special conventions elected for the purpose, not just by existing politicians.
- A built-in way to amend it (Article V). The system was designed to be changed lawfully rather than overthrown.
- No religious test for public office (Article VI). That was extraordinary in 1787, when Britain still barred Catholics and Dissenters from many offices.
- A republic covering a whole continent. Conventional wisdom held that republics only worked at the size of a city-state.
- Durability. It's still in force, and it became the model for written constitutions around the world.

#### Seriously Limited

- Slavery was built in. Slavery was so controversial and threatening that the word itself is conspicuously absent from the final draft of the Constitution. Yet one historian counts five explicit recognitions of slavery: the three-fifths clause, the slave trade clause, the capitation tax clause, the fugitive slave clause, and Article V, which prevented any amendment of the slave trade provision.
- A common myth about the three-fifths clause: it was based on status, not race, and didn't define blacks as three-fifths human. In practice it boosted the political power of slaveholding states.
- Voting was left to the states. In practice that meant mostly white men who owned property. Women and Native Americans were largely excluded.
- There was no Bill of Rights at first. It had to be added in 1791 as the price of getting the Constitution ratified.

#### Historians Still Disagree

Whether it was a pro-slavery document has been argued over since the 1790s. Curiously, the most vehement arguments construing the Constitution as a pro-slavery document came from abolitionists, notably Garrison. Lincoln, and later Frederick Douglass, argued the opposite: that it put slavery on the path to ultimate extinction. Modern scholars are still split. Either way, the question was only settled by a civil war and the amendments passed after it in the 1860s.

Where I'd push back on "very clever": much of the design was messy horse-trading rather than grand vision. Madison lost several arguments he cared about, for example on how the Senate should be made up. The biggest problem, slavery, wasn't solved. It was pushed onto the next generation, and that ended in the Civil War.

### 6. Other Forward-thinking Examples

- Magna Carta (1215): a limit on royal power, though it protected barons rather than ordinary people.
- The Putney Debates and the Levellers' _Agreement of the People_ (1647): remarkably early English arguments for something close to manhood suffrage and popular sovereignty.
- The Instrument of Government (1653): Cromwell's constitution, England's only written constitution.
- Corsica (1755): Pasquale Paoli's constitution, which the Americans admired.
- Poland–Lithuania, 3 May 1791: Europe's first modern codified constitution. Neighbouring powers crushed it within two years.
- France, 1789: the Declaration of the Rights of Man, broader in its ambitions than the American founding but far less stable.
- Haiti, 1805: abolished slavery outright, which went further than the Americans did.
- Norway, 1814 (Eidsvoll): still in force, and Europe's oldest working written constitution.

The test of being "forward-thinking" is partly whether it survived. Several of these were arguably bolder than the American one but didn't last.
