The concept of the **12 Favorite Problems** stems from a strategy Richard Feynman used to keep his mind constantly engaged, structured, and open to unexpected breakthroughs.

Rather than just passively consuming facts, Feynman kept a dynamic list of a dozen open questions running in his subconscious. Whenever he encountered a new concept, tool, or random piece of information, he would instantly stress-test it against his list to see if it unlocked a solution.

As Feynman famously put it: *"You have to keep a dozen of your favorite problems constantly present in your mind, although by and large they will lay in a dormant state. Every time you hear or read a new trick or a new result, test it against each of your twelve problems to see if it helps."*

Here is how the strategy works in detail, along with specific examples of how Feynman applied it and how you can apply it yourself.

## The Anatomy of Feynman's Problems

Feynman’s list wasn't just a rigid set of textbook physics equations. It was a diverse mix of grand scientific mysteries, operational challenges, and quirky personal hobbies.

A few real examples of questions Feynman kept on his mind include:

- **The Fundamental:** *What is the unifying principle underlying light, radio, magnetism, and electricity?* (This eventually led to his Nobel Prize-winning work in quantum electrodynamics).
- **The Technical:** *How can we design a large-scale computing system using only basic equipment?*
- **The Behavioral:** *How can I accurately keep track of time in my head?* (Feynman actually practiced counting to 48-60 seconds while reading or doing other tasks to train his internal clock).
- **The Playful:** *How can I sustain a two-handed polyrhythm on the African drums?*

By mixing massive, long-term mysteries with smaller, highly achievable hobby goals, he ensured he always had a constant stream of momentum. Solving a small problem gave him a burst of motivation to keep tackling the massive ones.

## How It Works: The Cross-Pollination Effect

Most people learn linearly: you pick up a book on finance to learn finance, or a book on coding to learn coding. Feynman learned **multidirectionally**. His list acted as a magnet for cross-pollination (applying insights from one field to an entirely unrelated one).

Imagine your mind is a bank vault containing twelve distinct locks. Every time you learn something new—a software design pattern, a psychological bias, an architectural concept—you are handed a unique key. Most people throw the key in a drawer. A polymath takes that key and tries it on all twelve locks.

### A Real-World Example of Feynman's Cross-Pollination

One day in the Cornell University cafeteria, Feynman saw a student spin a cafeteria plate in the air. As the plate spun, he noticed the university medallion on the plate was wobbling, and it seemed to wobble faster than the plate was rotating.

Because he had dormant problems regarding the physics of subatomic particle spin in his mind, he didn't just see a cafeteria prank. He went back to his desk, calculated the motion of the rotating plate, and linked the equations of the wobbling plate directly to the way electron orbits behave. This playful cross-pollination directly laid the groundwork for his Nobel Prize.

## How to Build Your Own "12 Problems" List

To apply this strategy, you don't need to be a quantum physicist. You just need a vector for your curiosity. Your problems should span your career, your personal life, your habits, and your creative outlets.

Here is a blueprint to help formulate your list, categorised by area:

### 1\. The Work / Craft Category

These are structural bottlenecks or big questions within your industry or daily operations.

- *Weak:* "How do I write better code?"
- *Strong:* "How can I completely eliminate the 'merge skew problem' in our CI/CD deployment pipeline without delaying deployment times?"
- *Weak:* "How do I grow my business?"
- *Strong:* "What is the single biggest point of friction keeping a user from completing their checkout on our platform?"

### 2\. The Self-Mastery / Behavioral Category

These focus on your personal habits, cognitive biases, or physical well-being.

- *Example:* "How can I structure my morning routine to consistently hit a state of deep focus within fifteen minutes of sitting at my desk?"
- *Example:* "How do I naturally de-escalate tension when someone challenges my core worldview in a conversation?"

### 3\. The Curious / "Just for Fun" Category

These keep your mind playful, creative, and comfortable with experimentation.

- *Example:* "What is the underlying mathematical pattern behind the chord progressions of my favourite grunge and nu-metal songs?"
- *Example:* "How can I integrate a seamless smart-lighting setup in my home that responds perfectly to analogue pull-string switches?"

## Implementing the Strategy

Once you have your list, don't overcomplicate it. Write it down in a basic digital note or a physical journal and review it once a week.

When you read a book, watch a documentary, listen to a podcast, or even notice a weird trend in your daily life, glance at your list. Ask yourself: **"Does this piece of information give me a new perspective on any of my twelve locks?"** Most of the time, the answer will be no. But when the answer is yes, you hit the jackpot.
