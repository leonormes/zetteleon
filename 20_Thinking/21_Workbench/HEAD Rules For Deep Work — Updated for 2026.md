Here is a summary of the video "Rules For Deep Work—Updated for 2026" by Cal Newport:

Ten years after publishing his bestselling book _Deep Work_, Cal Newport revisits its four core rules to discuss how his advice holds up in 2026 and what updates are necessary in today's increasingly digital landscape \[[01:03](http://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nPzFhkTe2Uw&t=63)\].

Here is how he would update the four rules for 2026:

Rule 1: Work Deeply

- The Original Advice: Create specific rituals, routines, and physical environments to protect your focus and integrate deep work into your professional life.
- The 2026 Update: Newport suggests adopting a "hybrid attention model" for teams. This means designating remote working days entirely for uninterrupted deep work—no meetings, emails, or chat. Office days should then be reserved for shallow work, meetings, and collaboration \[[05:45](http://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nPzFhkTe2Uw&t=345)\].
- The AI Threat: He introduces a vital new rule for 2026: Do not let AI write for you. Relying on tools like ChatGPT to draft emails, reports, or memos removes the cognitive strain required to deeply understand your business. Bypassing this mental effort leads to "work slop" and ultimately diminishes your cognitive fitness \[[08:33](http://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nPzFhkTe2Uw&t=513)\].

Rule 2: Embrace Boredom

- The Original Advice: Focus is a skill that must be trained. Instead of taking breaks from distraction, you should schedule occasional breaks from focus.
- The 2026 Update: Newport provides a modern toolkit to train your brain's reward circuits:
	- Leave your phone plugged in a separate room (like the kitchen) whilst at home to weaken the neurological urge to check it \[[16:33](http://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nPzFhkTe2Uw&t=993)\].
		- Read physical books or use dedicated e-readers rather than reading on devices associated with digital distractions \[[18:05](http://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nPzFhkTe2Uw&t=1085)\].
		- Engage in hobbies that strictly reward focus and penalise distraction, such as tennis or golf \[[19:17](http://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nPzFhkTe2Uw&t=1157)\].
		- Take regular self-reflection walks without bringing your phone \[[20:00](http://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nPzFhkTe2Uw&t=1200)\].

Rule 3: Quit Social Media

- The Original Advice: Adopt a "craftsman approach" to digital tools, using social media only if its positive impacts substantially outweigh its negatives.
- The 2026 Update: Social media has drastically mutated from a utilitarian tool for keeping up with friends into an algorithmic engine of pure consumption and addiction (e.g., TikTok) \[[26:45](http://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nPzFhkTe2Uw&t=1605)\]. Because users are no longer posting or connecting, treating social media like a pros-and-cons tool selection is obsolete. Instead, Newport argues it must now be approached more like a "sobriety" issue, focusing on heavily restricting access to dumb down your phone and starving short-term dopamine circuits \[[29:22](http://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nPzFhkTe2Uw&t=1762)\].

Rule 4: Drain the Shallows

- The Original Advice: Use techniques like time blocking, tracking your deep-to-shallow work ratio, and executing strict shutdown routines to contain administrative tasks.
- The 2026 Update: Personal habits alone are no longer enough to combat the modern inbox. Instead, teams must replace the "hyperactive hive mind" (the culture of ad-hoc, back-and-forth messaging) with structured collaboration systems that require far fewer inbox checks \[[34:08](http://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nPzFhkTe2Uw&t=2048)\]. Additionally, explicitly managing your workload limits is critical; taking on too many concurrent tasks creates a flood of uncompressible administrative overhead that destroys any chance of deep work \[[35:54](http://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nPzFhkTe2Uw&t=2154)\].
