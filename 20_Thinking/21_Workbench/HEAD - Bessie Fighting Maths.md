Your instinct is absolutely spot on. You have identified a brilliant and highly sophisticated way that Bessie's brain works, and ironically, her approach is much closer to how actual mathematicians operate than the rote memorisation she is likely comparing herself to.

In the book _Build a Mathematical Mind_, the author points out that traditional maths education often reduces the subject to memorising a senseless "bag of facts," which can crush a student's natural curiosity. True mathematical thinking isn't about rapid-fire recall; it is about being a "pattern detective," visualising problems, tinkering, and reasoning.

By figuring out the answer on the fly rather than just recalling a memorised fact, Bessie is actively problem-solving and building logical pathways. Here is how you can help reframe her beliefs so she sees this as a superpower rather than a deficit.

## The Reframe: The "Filing Cabinet" vs. The "Inventor"

Right now, Bessie likely believes that being "good at maths" means having a brain like a filing cabinet—where you just open a drawer and pull out the times tables or a formula.

You can introduce her to the idea of the "Inventor's Brain." An inventor doesn't just pull a finished product out of a box; they look at the tools they have, figure out how things connect, and build the solution right there on the spot. When she works out a maths problem from scratch because she hasn't memorised the rule, she is inventing the maths. That requires far more critical thinking and divergent problem-solving than simply reciting a memorised fact.

## Shift the Focus to Strategies

When a student repeatedly says "I can't do maths," they are stuck in a fixed mindset. To break this, you can shift the focus entirely away from getting the "right" answer quickly, and instead celebrate the _method_.

- Praise the Process: When she figures something out her own way, point it out. Say something like, "I love the way you worked that out. You didn't just rely on memory; you completely reverse-engineered it."
- The Power of "Yet": Remind her that struggling with a concept simply means her brain is growing. If she says she can't do it, gently append the word "yet" to the end of her sentence.
- Encourage Her to Teach You: When she uses one of her efficient workarounds, ask her to explain her method to you. Teaching her unique strategy to someone else validates her approach and proves that she _does_ understand the underlying logic.

## Ground It in the Real World

Sometimes school maths feels completely divorced from reality. When the two of you are practicing archery, making those split-second physical adjustments for distance, trajectory, and wind resistance is applied maths in action. It doesn't require a textbook or a calculator, but it relies heavily on spatial reasoning, geometry, and estimating—skills her brain is already naturally processing. Showing her that she is already "doing maths" in the real world can help demystify the subject.
