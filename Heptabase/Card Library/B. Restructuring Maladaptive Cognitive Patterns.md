## B. Restructuring Maladaptive Cognitive Patterns

Adults with ADHD frequently develop maladaptive cognitive patterns and irrational beliefs as a consequence of their ongoing struggles with attention, impulsivity, and executive functions. These cognitive distortions—systematic errors in thinking—can significantly impact emotional well-being and behavior, often fuelling procrastination, emotional dysregulation, self-doubt, and a fear of failure.

Fostering Cognitive Flexibility and Nuanced Self-Evaluation:

Beyond restructuring specific thoughts, a deeper goal is to foster cognitive flexibility—the ability to shift thinking patterns and adapt cognitive strategies in response to changing situational demands. Deficits in cognitive flexibility are associated with increased psychological distress. Exposure Therapy, traditionally used for anxiety disorders, has shown promise in enhancing cognitive flexibility in adults with ADHD. By systematically confronting situations or tasks that are typically avoided (often due to fear of failure or overwhelm linked to cognitive distortions), individuals are encouraged to develop and practice more adaptive cognitive and behavioural responses. This process promotes engagement with novel problem-solving approaches rather than relying on rigid, maladaptive thought patterns.

Developing nuanced self-evaluation involves moving away from the harsh, dichotomous self-judgments often fuelled by cognitive distortions (e.g., "I'm either a complete success or a total failure"). The aim is to cultivate a more balanced, compassionate, and realistic self-assessment that acknowledges both strengths and challenges, efforts as well as outcomes.

It is important to recognize that cognitive distortions in adults with ADHD are not merely abstract errors in logic; they are frequently and intimately connected with underlying emotional dysregulation and executive function deficits. For example, a cognitive distortion like catastrophizing ("If I miss this deadline, my career is over") is not just a thought; it is typically accompanied by intense anxiety and may be triggered by genuine difficulties with time management and task completion stemming from executive dysfunction.27 Therefore, effective cognitive restructuring for ADHD must often be multi-modal. Simply identifying and challenging the thought content may prove insufficient if the underlying emotional reactivity or practical organizational difficulties persist and continue to generate "evidence" that seems to support the distortion. This implies that interventions for cognitive distortions in ADHD should be integrated with strategies that simultaneously address emotional regulation (such as mindfulness practices or skills derived from DBT) and provide practical support for executive functioning (such as systematic task breakdown, use of planning tools, and environmental modifications).

Furthermore, the development of cognitive flexibility, as highlighted by the potential of exposure therapy 31, suggests a mechanism for change that transcends simple thought replacement. Cognitive flexibility implies the cultivation of a meta-cognitive capacity—the ability to step back from one's thoughts, evaluate different perspectives, and consciously choose more adaptive thinking strategies. This is a more profound and sustainable form of change than merely altering the content of specific, isolated negative thoughts. It equips the individual with a transferable skill set to navigate future novel situations and potential cognitive pitfalls, rather than just addressing the distortions that are active at the time of therapy. This capacity for flexible, adaptive thinking is a cornerstone of deep cognitive transformation.
