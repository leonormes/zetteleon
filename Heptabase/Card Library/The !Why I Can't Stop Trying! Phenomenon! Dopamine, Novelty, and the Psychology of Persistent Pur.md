# **The "Why I Can't Stop Trying" Phenomenon: Dopamine, Novelty, and the Psychology of Persistent Pursuit.**

The compulsion to repeatedly try new productivity tools and systems, despite a history of unsuccessful attempts, is a complex phenomenon rooted in the neurobiology and psychology of ADHD. Several factors contribute to this persistent pursuit:

## **Dopamine And Novelty Seeking**

The ADHD brain is often characterized by differences in dopamine regulation, leading to an "interest-based nervous system". It is highly motivated by novelty, challenge, urgency, or genuine interest, as these experiences can trigger dopamine release, which is associated with pleasure, motivation, and focus. Each new productivity tool, app, or system represents a novel approach, a fresh start, and the *anticipation* of finally achieving organization and control. This anticipation itself can be a potent dopamine trigger, making the act of trying something new inherently rewarding, even if experiences suggest a low probability of long-term success. This is often referred to as "bright shiny object syndrome," where the allure of the new overshadows the lessons from the old.

## **The Hope for a "Magic Bullet"**

After numerous attempts that fall short, the desire for a system that will *finally* click and solve persistent organizational challenges can become intense. This hope fuels the continued search, as the perceived benefits of a truly functional system—reduced stress, increased accomplishment, a sense of calm—are incredibly high.

**Sunk Cost Fallacy:** Having invested significant time, energy, and emotional resources ("many years trying") into understanding and attempting to implement these systems, the sunk cost fallacy can make it difficult to disengage. Abandoning the pursuit might feel like admitting defeat or acknowledging that past efforts were wasted, making it psychologically easier to continue investing in the hope of an eventual payoff.

## **Perfectionism And Idealism**

The drive to find or create the "perfect" all-encompassing system can also contribute. If one system reveals flaws or doesn't meet every idealized expectation, the tendency might be to discard it and seek another, rather than iteratively adapting the existing one to be "good enough". This ties back to the allure of the idealized organized self.

## **The "Productivity Trap"**

The very act of researching, selecting, and setting up new systems can itself feel productive, providing a temporary sense of accomplishment and control, even if it doesn't translate into sustained output or organization. This "busywork loop" can mask a lack of meaningful progress on actual goals.
These factors intertwine to create a powerful cycle of hope and frustration. The compulsion to keep trying is not simply a matter of poor judgment; it's driven by the brain's inherent reward-seeking mechanisms and a genuine desire for a better way of functioning. The initial engagement with a new system is often stimulating due to its novelty and the promise it holds. However, as the inherent executive function deficits inevitably clash with the system's demands, frustration, overwhelm, and a sense of failure often follow. This negative experience, rather than extinguishing the search, can paradoxically strengthen it. The failure reinforces the perceived *need* for an even better or different system, one that will finally overcome these persistent challenges. The allure of the next "bright shiny object" becomes even stronger as a potential escape from the disappointment of the current or previous system, thus perpetuating the loop: search for novelty → temporary reward/hope → EFD clash → failure/frustration → renewed search for a "better" novelty. Understanding this cycle is the first step toward breaking it and finding more sustainable solutions.
