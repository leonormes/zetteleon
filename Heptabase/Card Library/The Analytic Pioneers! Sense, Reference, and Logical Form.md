Here is the updated map of the corpus, newly integrating the analytic pioneers and the evolution of Wittgenstein.

The introduction of Frege and Russell fundamentally changes the landscape. We can now trace how the concept of the proposition began as a pure logical abstraction before being dismantled by the pragmatists, post-structuralists, and eventually the knowledge architects.

## 1\. The Analytic Pioneers: Sense, Reference, and Logical Form

Before the modern logicians codified truth-aptness, Frege and Russell established the groundwork of what a proposition actually _is_ in relation to language.

- Frege (The Sense of a Sentence): An assertoric sentence has both a _sense_ (Sinn) and a _reference_ (Bedeutung). The proposition (the thought) is the sentence's _sense_—it contains the "mode of presentation" of a situation. If the sentence refers to anything successfully, its ultimate reference is simply a truth-value: The True or The False _(Textor, Routledge Philosophy GuideBook to Frege on Sense and Reference, Introduction)_.
- Russell (Logical Form vs. Surface Grammar): Ordinary grammar actively disguises the true logical proposition. In "On Denoting," Russell proves that a denoting phrase like "the present King of France" is not a genuine subject term, but a disguised set of existential claims. A proposition must be analysed down to its constituents to avoid committing us to phantom entities _(Russell, On Denoting)_. Furthermore, belief is not a relation between a mind and a single "proposition" object; it is a multiple relation uniting a mind with several constituents. A belief is true only if those constituents correspond to a complex unity (a fact) in reality _(Russell, The Problems of Philosophy, Ch. XII)_.
- The Lens: _Logical Form_ and _Warrant_. We must strip away the grammatical illusion to find the testable claims beneath.

## 2\. The Tractarian Picture: The Ultimate Mirror

The early Wittgenstein built upon Russell's logic to make the proposition the absolute centre of philosophy.

- The Rule: A proposition is a "logical picture" of a state of affairs. Its elements (names) correspond to objects in the world, and their arrangement in the proposition logically mirrors the arrangement of objects in reality. A proposition only has sense if it depicts a possible factual situation _(Grayling, Wittgenstein, Ch. 2 / Tractatus 4.021, 4.031)_.
- The Lens: _Isomorphism_. If a sentence cannot be broken down into elementary propositions that picture reality, it is strictly senseless—which is why the early Wittgenstein relegated ethics and aesthetics to what can be "shown" but not "said".

## 3\. The Pragmatic Turn: Language Games

The later Wittgenstein dismantles his own earlier work, joining Toulmin (and later Foucault) in pulling propositions out of the abstract and into human behaviour.

- The Rule: Wittgenstein repudiated the idea that language has a single, hidden logical essence. Stating a proposition is not picturing reality; it is merely one "language-game" among countless others (such as commanding, questioning, or joking). The meaning of an expression is not the object it denotes, but its use in the language _(Grayling, Wittgenstein, Ch. 3 / Investigations §43)_.
- The Lens: _Mood_ and _Use._ We no longer ask "What fact does this picture?", we ask "What is the speaker using this move to do?"

You have excellent instincts. The strict formal logic of Russell and Tomassi is highly precise, but it can be sterile. It tests the validity of abstract structures, but it does not necessarily help you build a living body of knowledge.

In fact, Stephen Toulmin wrote _The Uses of Argument_ to stage exactly this rebellion. He argued that the mathematical, "narrow" approach of formal logic strips away the very context we need to rationally justify opinions in daily life or research _(Toulmin, The Uses of Argument, Introduction)_.

If your goal is to populate a slip-box with atomic ideas that ultimately synthesise into defensible positions, you do indeed need informal argumentation.

## The Shift: From Proposition to Premise

When we leave formal logic and enter argumentation, the "proposition" changes its job title.

- Walter Sinnott-Armstrong defines an argument as "a connected series of premises intended to present a reason for a conclusion" _(Sinnott-Armstrong, Think Again, Ch. 6)_.
- Here, a proposition is no longer just an isolated picture of reality. It becomes a functional component of a system: it acts either as a Premise (the reason) or the Conclusion (the claim you are stating your position on).

In a functional note-making system, your atomic notes act as these premises or data points. When you create a structural note linking them together, you are constructing an argument _(Ahrens, How to Take Smart Notes, Ch. 12; Doto, A System for Writing, Ch. 6)_.
