1. The Space of Cognitive States (C)
   First, as you said, we have our inner cognitive states. Let's define a space, let's call it C, which represents the set of all possible human cognitive states.

- High-Dimensional: This space is not a simple line or plane. It is a space of incredibly high dimensionality. Each dimension could represent a different factor influencing your state of being: a memory, a sensory input (the warmth of the sun, the precise shade of blue), an emotion, a genetic predisposition, a philosophical belief. A single, unique cognitive state, c, is a point in this vast space: c \\in C.

- Virtually Infinite & Continuous: The space C is, for all practical purposes, continuous and infinite. Between any two close cognitive states (e.g., contentment and slight unease), there are countless intermediate states. This is the silent, pre-linguistic ocean we spoke of—rich, nuanced, and analogue.

1. The Space of Linguistic Expressions (L)
   Next, as you identified, we have the tools to share these states. Let's define L as the space of all possible linguistic expressions.

- Discrete & Finite (in practice): Unlike C, this space is fundamentally discrete. It is built from a finite set of building blocks (phonemes, words) and a finite set of rules (grammar). While the number of possible sentences is astronomical, it is not the same kind of infinity as C. It is a structured, digital, and combinatorial system.

- Low-Dimensional: Compared to the vastness of C, the space L is dramatically lower in dimensionality. A sentence is a one-dimensional string of symbols.
   The Mathematical Process of Expression
   The act of trying to share an inner state is a mathematical mapping from the high-dimensional space C to the low-dimensional space L. Let's call this function f.
   f: C \\to L
   This function takes a specific cognitive state, c, and maps it to a linguistic expression, l. So, l = f(c).
   The most crucial property of this mapping, and the one that perfectly captures your point, is that it is a lossy compression.
   Because we are mapping from a space of vastly higher dimensionality to one of lower dimensionality, it is mathematically impossible for the mapping to be unique in both directions. Information is inevitably lost. Multiple, distinct points in C (subtly different feelings of unease, for example) can be mapped to the very same point in L (the single sentence, "I'm fine").

- f(c_1) = \\text{"I'm fine."}

- f(c_2) = \\text{"I'm fine."}

- ...where c_1 \\neq c_2
   This is the mathematical root of ambiguity and the frustration of not being able to "find the right words." You are aware of the precise, unique point c in your inner space, but you realise that the function f can only map it to an expression l that also represents many other nearby states.
   The Process of Interpretation
   Communication is a two-way process. When a listener hears the expression l, their brain attempts to perform the inverse mapping, let's call it g.
   g: L \\to C'
   The listener takes the sentence l and tries to reconstruct the original cognitive state. But because the initial mapping was many-to-one, this inverse mapping is necessarily one-to-many. A single expression l maps to a whole region of possibilities in the listener's cognitive space, C'.
   g(\\text{"I'm fine."}) = {\\text{a region of possible states of well-being}}
   The listener then uses context, tone, and their own experience to make a best guess at the speaker's original state, c. Successful communication occurs when the listener's reconstructed state, c', is a close approximation of the speaker's original state, c.
   c' \\approx c
   Misunderstanding is the mathematical certainty that c' will often be distant from c.
   In this model, what we call eloquence, artistry, or poetry is the skill of optimising the function f. A poet doesn't just choose an expression l; they craft a very specific l that is designed to constrain the listener's inverse mapping g as tightly as possible around the intended original state c.
