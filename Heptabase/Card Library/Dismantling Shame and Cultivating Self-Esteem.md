# **Dismantling Shame and Cultivating Self-Esteem**

Shame and low self-esteem are pervasive and deeply painful experiences for many adults with ADHD. These feelings often arise from a cumulative history of perceived failures, missed opportunities, and a persistent sense of not meeting personal or societal expectations. This chronic struggle can lead to the internalization of a core belief of inherent defectiveness, a maladaptive schema that profoundly impacts self-worth. Research robustly links ADHD with lower self-esteem, indicating a negative correlation between ADHD symptom severity and self-regard. This is compounded by negative life experiences and the societal stigma often associated with ADHD. Effective interventions aim to dismantle these ingrained patterns and cultivate a more compassionate and affirming self-relationship.

A noteworthy consideration from the UMAAP study was that some participants reported experiencing feelings of guilt and shame when they struggled to complete home practice assignments. This highlights the critical need for careful adaptation of such interventions for the ADHD population, ensuring that therapeutic tasks are manageable and that support is provided to navigate potential difficulties without reinforcing negative self-perceptions.

The commonality across CFT, ST, and ACT in addressing shame and low self-esteem in adults with ADHD lies in a fundamental shift in therapeutic focus. Instead of primarily targeting the *content* of negative thoughts and feelings (i.e., trying to directly eliminate or suppress them), these approaches emphasize *process-focused or context-focused change*. This involves altering the individual's *relationship* with their internal experiences and understanding the origins and functions of these experiences. CFT cultivates a compassionate self that can respond differently to suffering and self-criticism, rather than simply trying to stop critical thoughts. ST traces shame to its developmental roots in early maladaptive schemas and employs experiential techniques to heal these schemas, thereby changing their emotional power, which goes beyond mere cognitive disputation. ACT explicitly aims not to reduce the frequency of unpleasant internal experiences but to foster psychological flexibility, enabling individuals to pursue valued actions *despite* the presence of such experiences, with acceptance and defusion being key mechanisms. This shift is particularly crucial for adults with ADHD, for whom negative self-perceptions and challenging emotions can be chronic and deeply ingrained. Attempting to engage in a direct battle to eliminate these persistent experiences can be futile and counterproductive, potentially leading to more frustration and self-blame. Changing the relationship with these internal states offers a more sustainable path to well-being.

Furthermore, the efficacy of these therapies, particularly ST and CFT, underscores the profound influence of early life experiences and ongoing societal feedback in shaping the psychological landscape of adults with ADHD. ADHD is a neurodevelopmental condition, meaning its impact begins early and unfolds over the lifespan.18 The chronic nature of ADHD-related challenges, often met with misunderstanding or negative responses from others, can contribute significantly to the development of shame and low self-esteem. This implies that deep and lasting transformation often requires more than addressing present-day symptoms in isolation. It necessitates acknowledging, processing, and working through these biographical factors and their emotional sequelae. Therapeutic approaches that incorporate this developmental context, such as ST's focus on childhood origins of schemas 1 or CFT's attention to the roots of self-criticism, are therefore more likely to facilitate profound change compared to strategies that focus solely on current behavioural management without this deeper, historical understanding.
