## Lit: NotebookLM on JTB

Source: NotebookLM [2026-04-24.md](../Journal/2026-04-24.md)

For a long time, philosophers agreed with the "classical analysis" that knowledge is simply justified true belief. However, current thinking dictates that this definition is missing something.

In 1963, philosopher Edmund Gettier revolutionized the field by demonstrating that you can have a justified true belief that _isn't_ actually knowledge because you simply got lucky. For example, imagine you check a train station clock that says 1:17, and you form the justified belief that it is 1:17. It actually _is_ 1:17, so your belief is true. However, the clock has been broken for two days, and you just happened to look at it at the exact minute it was correct. You have a justified true belief, but most philosophers agree you didn't actually _know_ the time.

Because of these "Gettier problems," current thinking offers a few new approaches to defining knowledge:

- Adding Conditions: Some philosophers argue we need justified true belief plus an extra rule, like ensuring your belief wasn't based on any false premises, or that your belief doesn't have any hidden "defeaters" (true facts you don't know about that would ruin your justification).
- Externalist Theories: Others look outside the believer's mind, arguing that your belief must be directly caused by the fact itself, or that it must be produced by a highly reliable psychological mechanism.
- Knowledge-First: Some modern philosophers argue that we should stop trying to break knowledge down into parts. They suggest "knowing" is the most fundamental state of mind, and mere "believing" is just an approximation of it.

Does the broken clock example make sense as to why justified true belief isn't quite enough? We could look closer at the reliable mechanism theory or the "knowledge-first" idea next, if you're ready.
