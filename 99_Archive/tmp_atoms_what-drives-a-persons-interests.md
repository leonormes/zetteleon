### Atom 001: Enduring Interest Emerges From a Multi-Factor Feedback Loop, Not a Single Trait
- Kind: claim
- Statement: Enduring interest in an activity is generated by an interacting feedback system — temperament and ability, early experience, perceived competence, emotion, identity, opportunity, social reinforcement, and the kinds of reward a person finds meaningful — rather than by one hidden "true passion" or trait.
- Scope & Conditions: Applies to explaining why the same activity produces durable engagement in one person and not another; not a claim about any single causal factor being sufficient alone.
- Evidence: "A person's interests are usually not driven by one hidden 'true passion' or a single trait. They emerge from a feedback system between temperament and ability, early experience, perceived competence, emotion, identity, opportunity, social reinforcement, and the kinds of rewards a person finds meaningful."
- Implications:
    - Explaining "why do you like X" by naming one trait or one cause is likely incomplete.
    - The same activity can be differently rewarding to different people because the felt experience of doing it differs, not because the activity itself has fixed appeal.

### Atom 002: Self-Efficacy and Outcome Expectations Jointly Predict Durable Interest
- Kind: definition
- Statement: Social Cognitive Career Theory holds that durable interest is shaped by two linked expectations — self-efficacy ("can I become capable at this?") and outcome expectations ("what worthwhile outcome will this bring?") — which are continually updated by successes, failures, observing others, social encouragement, and emotional experience.
- Scope & Conditions: A career/vocational-psychology model; applied here to interests generally, not only career choice.
- Evidence: "A broad research model, Social Cognitive Career Theory, proposes that interests are heavily shaped by two linked expectations: Self-efficacy... Outcome expectations... People are more likely to develop durable interest where they expect both competence and valued outcomes." [^1][^2][^3]
- Implications:
    - An interest can be killed by low self-efficacy even where the outcome is genuinely valued, or vice versa.
    - Both expectations are updateable, not fixed — implying interest can be deliberately rebuilt.

### Atom 003: A Competence Feedback Loop Turns Early Success Into Durable Interest
- Kind: mechanism
- Statement: A small early success in a domain creates a self-reinforcing loop — perceived capability increases willingness to engage, which increases practice and pattern recognition, which produces more success and strengthens identity and interest — so two people of similar underlying ability can end up with radically different relationships to the same subject depending on when and how that loop started.
- Scope & Conditions: Illustrated in the source with mathematics specifically; the loop is disrupted by anxiety and repeated failure, which push engagement in the opposite direction. Interest formed this way is also contingent on how the subject was taught — procedural, speed- and evaluation-focused instruction can prevent a learner from ever meeting the parts of a subject (creativity, play, beauty, explanatory power) that build durable interest in someone else.
- Evidence: "A small success → 'I can do this' → greater willingness to engage → more practice and attention → more success and pattern recognition → stronger identity and interest... This means two people of similar underlying ability can develop radically different relationships to maths depending on instruction, timing, feedback, peer environment, whether mistakes felt safe, and whether the subject was presented as meaningful rather than as a performance test." [^7][^8][^9]
- Implications:
    - Whether a subject "clicked" early is a stronger predictor of adult interest than raw aptitude.
    - Interventions to build interest in a disliked subject should target early, safe wins over more exposure.

### Atom 004: Situational Interest Requires Maintenance to Become Durable Individual Interest
- Kind: distinction
- Statement: Interest-development research distinguishes situational interest — a temporary spike of attention triggered by a striking experience — from durable individual interest, which requires ongoing maintenance through meaningful engagement, growing knowledge, and enough positive feedback that a person returns voluntarily.
- Scope & Conditions: From Hidi & Renninger's Four-Phase Model of interest development.
- Evidence: "Research on interest development distinguishes temporary, situational interest from more durable individual interest. A striking experience may trigger attention, but enduring interest needs maintenance: meaningful engagement, growing knowledge, and enough positive feedback for the person to return voluntarily." [^10][^11]
- Implications:
    - A first exciting encounter with a topic is not evidence of a lasting interest; only repeated, well-maintained return visits are.
    - Distinguishes "this excited me once" from "this is something I actually maintain interest in" when auditing one's own interest history.

### Atom 005: Autonomy, Competence, and Relatedness Make an Interest Self-Sustaining
- Kind: definition
- Statement: Self-Determination Theory identifies three psychological needs — autonomy ("this is chosen and mine"), competence ("I can improve and become effective"), and relatedness ("I belong and connect") — and holds that an activity becomes more self-sustaining when it satisfies these through choice, visible progress, and connection, rather than through external reward alone.
- Scope & Conditions: The source explicitly notes these three needs are "a useful lens," not a complete account of all interests.
- Evidence: table mapping need → core experience → example ("Autonomy | 'This is chosen and mine.' | Self-directed research..."); "Activities tend to become more self-sustaining when they offer choice, visible progress, meaningful challenge, and connection rather than only external rewards." [^12][^13][^14]
- Implications:
    - An externally-imposed but otherwise well-taught activity may still fail to become a lasting interest if it offers no autonomy.
    - Useful checklist when redesigning a stalled interest or habit: which of the three needs is currently unmet?

### Atom 006: Genetic Influence on Interests Operates Through Precursor Traits, Not Fixed Destiny
- Kind: claim
- Statement: Twin and adoption research indicates individual differences in interests are meaningfully influenced by genetics, but indirectly — through precursor traits such as aptitude and personality that shape which environments a person enters and how they respond to them, rather than by specifying a fixed destiny.
- Scope & Conditions: Genetic influence is on precursor traits (aptitude, personality), not directly on named interests (e.g. "maths" or "music") themselves.
- Evidence: "Twin and adoption research suggests that individual differences in vocational and recreational interests are meaningfully influenced by genetics, partly through precursor traits such as aptitude and personality. But those traits influence which environments a person enters and how they respond to them; they do not specify a fixed destiny." [^4][^5][^6]
- Implications:
    - "It's genetic" is not a reason to treat an interest, or its absence, as fixed or beyond the reach of environment/practice.
    - Explains why interests can still be heritable-looking across a family while remaining highly responsive to exposure and encouragement.

### Atom 007: Loving a Subject Decomposes Into Distinct Reward Types, Not One Preference
- Kind: distinction
- Statement: "Loving" a subject such as mathematics is not one uniform preference; different people who enjoy the same subject are often drawn to distinct reward types within it — for example mastery/challenge, pattern discovery, rigour/certainty, aesthetic compression, explanatory power, abstract play, relief from social ambiguity, or belonging within a shared practice. Conversely, disliking the subject does not imply a lack of intelligence or curiosity — it may reflect a preference for different feedback (sensory, social, practical), open rather than formal criteria, or a competence loop (Atom 003) that started later or worse.
- Scope & Conditions: Illustrated with mathematics in the source; framed as generalisable to any subject.
- Evidence: table "What they enjoy | Felt reward" (solving difficult problems → mastery and challenge; finding hidden regularities → pattern recognition; formal proof → certainty and rigour; elegant solutions → aesthetic compression; ...); "Someone who dislikes maths may not lack intelligence, discipline, or curiosity. They may prefer: meaning expressed through story, people, lived experience... immediate sensory, social, or practical feedback."
- Implications:
    - Two people who both "love maths" may be pursuing incompatible experiences within it (e.g. rigour vs. play) and can talk past each other about what the subject is "really" about.
    - Useful diagnostic when an interest feels stalled: which specific reward type is currently missing, not just "am I still interested."

### Atom 008: Interest Bundles Form Through Five Distinct Mechanisms
- Kind: distinction
- Statement: Interests that co-occur in one person (an "interest bundle") can be explained by at least five distinct, non-exclusive mechanisms: shared cognitive ingredients (the activities reward the same kind of mental operation, e.g. pattern detection), shared reward dynamics (the same challenge → feedback → improvement loop), shared values or drivers (the activities satisfy the same underlying want, e.g. mastery or belonging), shared environments and social pathways (co-exposure via household, school, peers, or online communities), and identity/network effects (adopting one identity lowers the friction for trying adjacent domains).
- Scope & Conditions: A bundle is evidence worth investigating, not proof of a single cause. Absence of an interest is informative but ambiguous — it can mean genuine lack of fit, or lack of access, wrong timing, or a high entry cost, so absence alone should not be read as disproof of a candidate driver.
- Evidence: "There are at least five mechanisms." — followed by the five named subsections (Shared Cognitive Ingredients / Shared Reward Dynamics / Shared Values or Drivers / Shared Environments and Social Pathways / Identity and Network Effects); "A bundle is not proof of one cause, but it is a useful clue."; "absence is informative but ambiguous... it can also mean 'I never had access,' 'I met it at the wrong time,' or 'the entry cost was too high.'"
- Implications:
    - Before proposing a psychological "driver" to explain a bundle, first check whether shared environment or shared cognitive demands already explain it more cheaply.
    - Gives a checklist for auditing any interest cluster: which of the five mechanisms actually applies here, and is more than one operating at once?

### Atom 009: Mathematical and Musical Ability Show a Moderate Correlation Without Proving Shared Causation
- Kind: claim
- Statement: A moderate positive association has been found between latent mathematical and musical ability (r = 0.41), but this is a correlation, not proof that either skill causes the other; broader evidence that music instruction specifically transfers to mathematics ability is inconsistent or limited.
- Scope & Conditions: Correlational finding; do not read as evidence of a direct causal or shared-mechanism link without further qualification.
- Evidence: "Recent work found a moderate positive association between latent mathematical and musical abilities, r = 0.41, but this is an association rather than proof that either skill directly causes the other. Other reviews find that music instruction may benefit certain spatial-temporal abilities, while broad transfer to mathematics is inconsistent or limited." [^15][^16][^17][^18]
- Implications:
    - A commonly cited "maths and music are linked" claim should be stated as correlational, not causal.
    - Useful caution against over-interpreting any single interest-bundle correlation (Atom 008) as proof of a shared cognitive mechanism.

### Atom 010: Identity Adoption in One Domain Lowers Friction for Adjacent Interests
- Kind: mechanism
- Statement: Once a person adopts an identity such as "a systems person" or "a musician," success in that domain supplies knowledge, tools, communities, and confidence that lower the entry friction for trying adjacent domains, producing an expanding cluster of related interests over time.
- Scope & Conditions: Describes one of the five bundling mechanisms in Atom 008, isolated here because it is a distinct causal loop (identity → lower friction → new competence → denser cluster) rather than a static classification.
- Evidence: "Once someone sees themselves as 'a systems person,' 'a musician,' 'an investigator,' 'a craftsperson,' or 'a lifelong learner,' adjacent interests become easier to notice and try. Each successful adjacent interest supplies knowledge, tools, communities, and confidence that lower entry friction for the next."
- Implications:
    - Predicts that a person's next new interest is more likely to be adjacent to an already-adopted identity than randomly distributed across all possible domains.
    - Suggests a lever for deliberately broadening interests: adopt the identity first, in a low-stakes way, before expecting competence.

### Atom 011: Holland's RIASEC Model Frames Interests as Environmental Patterns, Not Isolated Preferences
- Kind: definition
- Statement: Holland's vocational-interest framework (RIASEC) represents interests as recurring patterns of fit across six environment types — realistic, investigative, artistic, social, enterprising, and conventional — rather than as a list of isolated individual preferences.
- Scope & Conditions: A longstanding vocational-psychology model, cited here as a precedent for treating interest clusters as patterned rather than atomised.
- Evidence: "Holland's vocational-interest framework is one longstanding example of this idea: it represents interests as recurring patterns across realistic, investigative, artistic, social, enterprising, and conventional environments, rather than as isolated preferences." [^19]
- Implications:
    - Supports treating an "interest graph" (plural, patterned) as the right unit of analysis rather than auditing each named interest in isolation.

### Atom 012: How to Interrogate a Candidate Interest Driver
- Kind: procedure
- Statement: For any interest or interest bundle, test a proposed psychological driver by working through seven questions in sequence: (1) what does this interest let me do, experience, or avoid; (2) what moment within it feels most alive or satisfying; (3) what elements are shared across otherwise unrelated interests; (4) which similar-looking activities fail to hold me, and what is missing from them; (5) what conditions make the interest stronger or weaker; (6) what would I predict I would enjoy next if this driver were real; (7) what evidence would make me downgrade the explanation.
- Scope & Conditions: Trigger — a candidate driver (e.g. "love of pattern") has been proposed to explain an interest bundle and needs testing rather than just narrative plausibility. Verification — the driver survives if a predicted next-interest is later confirmed and no strong disconfirming case turns up; e.g. if "pattern" is proposed to explain both maths and music, check whether other pattern-rich activities that lack mastery progression or aesthetic payoff are also enjoyed — if not, "pattern" alone is too broad and the real driver is a narrower bundle such as pattern + progressive competence + meaningful feedback.
- Evidence: "For any interest bundle, ask: 1. What does each interest let me _do_, experience, or avoid? ... 7. What evidence would make me downgrade the explanation?"; "if you hypothesise that maths and music are both driven by 'love of pattern,' test whether you also enjoy pattern-rich activities that lack mastery progression, aesthetic satisfaction, or personal relevance. If not, 'pattern' may be too broad."
