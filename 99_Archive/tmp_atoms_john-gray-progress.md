## Atoms

### Atom 001: Progress Is Multidimensional and Must Specify Its Domain Before Being Judged Real or Illusory
- Kind: distinction
- Statement: "Progress" is not one variable — life expectancy, scientific knowledge, personal liberty, equality, political stability, material prosperity, ecological sustainability, and subjective wellbeing can all move in different directions at once — so a claim that humanity is or isn't progressing is incomplete until it specifies the dimension, population, time period, and baseline.
- Scope & Conditions: A corrective to both blanket-optimist and blanket-pessimist claims about "progress"; doesn't itself settle whether any given dimension is improving.
- Evidence: "'Progress' is not one variable... A claim that humanity is or is not progressing is therefore incomplete until it specifies the dimension, population, time period, and baseline."
- Implications:
    - Any argument that opens with "progress is real" or "progress is a myth" without naming the dimension should be treated as under-specified until it does.

### Atom 002: Technological Progress Accumulates Reliably While Moral and Political Progress Is Fragile and Reversible
- Kind: claim
- Statement: Scientific knowledge and technology tend to preserve earlier gains and can be built upon reliably, but ethical and political achievements — rights, norms, peace, institutional competence, public trust — do not behave the same way and can weaken or disappear within a few generations, because they depend on continued enforcement, legitimacy, and public support rather than being self-maintaining.
- Scope & Conditions: The narrower, empirically defensible core of John Gray's argument, distinct from the stronger and less supported claim that moral/political progress is simply a myth or illusion — measurable gains in health, education, and liberty are real even though they are uneven and reversible.
- Evidence: "Scientific knowledge and technology often preserve earlier gains: a technique, discovery, or engineering capability can be transmitted and built upon. Ethical and political achievements do not behave as reliably. Rights, norms, peace, institutional competence, and public trust can weaken or disappear within a few generations."
- Implications:
    - A useful warning against assuming new technology automatically produces wiser politics — the two kinds of "progress" don't transfer into each other.

### Atom 003: A Reversed Gain Was Still a Real Improvement While It Lasted — Reversibility Concerns Durability, Not Occurrence
- Kind: distinction
- Statement: Treating the possibility of reversal as evidence against progress having occurred is a logical overreach — a gain can be real even if it is later lost, because reversibility is a question about how long an improvement lasts, not about whether the improvement happened at all; a country that became freer for a period and then authoritarian again was still actually freer during that period.
- Scope & Conditions: Corrects a specific inferential error (reversibility → the gain wasn't real), not a claim that reversed gains don't matter — the relevant follow-up questions are how large the gain was, who benefited, how durable it was, and what caused its reversal.
- Evidence: "Gray treats the possibility of reversal as evidence against progress. That inference is too strong. A gain can be real even if it is later lost; reversibility concerns durability, not whether the improvement occurred."
- Implications:
    - Citing a later reversal to retroactively deny an earlier gain ever happened is a distinct, checkable fallacy worth naming when it appears in political argument.

### Atom 004: Historical Recurrence Means Recurring Mechanisms, Not Identical Events
- Kind: distinction
- Statement: Claims that "history repeats" or that a current situation resembles a past one are useful as anti-utopian warnings, but risk flattening important differences — recurring motives (status competition, resource conflict, fear, identity, domination, the search for meaning) can operate through radically different institutions and technologies, so recurrence should mean recurring mechanisms or patterns, not identical events.
- Scope & Conditions: A corrective for historical-analogy arguments generally; the burden is on the arguer to show the pattern has genuinely recurred (shared mechanism) rather than merely resembles a past case on the surface.
- Evidence: "Recurring motives—status competition, resource conflict, fear, identity, domination, and the search for meaning—can operate through radically different institutions and technologies... Recurrence should therefore mean recurring mechanisms or patterns, not identical events."
- Implications:
    - When evaluating a historical analogy, ask whether it identifies a shared causal mechanism or merely an evocative surface resemblance — these support very different strengths of conclusion.

### Atom 005: Radical Nominalism Treats Language as an Inherited, Provisional Tool, Not a Direct Conduit to Objective Reality
- Kind: definition
- Statement: Radical nominalism (as argued by John Gray) holds that language and conceptual frameworks are not direct conduits to an absolute, objective reality; they are inherited, provisional tools — likened to a spider's web — that serve functional purposes but should not be reified as universal truths.
- Scope & Conditions: A position in philosophy of language distinct from realism about universals or concepts; the "spider's web" metaphor emphasises that the framework is spun by and useful to the organism using it, not a neutral window onto the world.
- Evidence: "Radical Nominalism: Language and conceptual frameworks are not direct conduits to an absolute, objective reality. Instead, they are inherited, provisional tools (likened to a spider's web) that serve functional purposes but should not be reified as universal truths."
- Implications:
    - Treating a conceptual framework as if it directly mirrors reality (rather than as a useful, revisable tool) is the specific move this position warns against.

### Atom 006: Selective Citation of Successful Predictions Creates a Hindsight-Bias Illusion of Predictive Skill
- Kind: claim
- Statement: Citing one's own past correct warnings (e.g. opposing a war that later went badly) as general evidence of superior judgement is unreliable without a systematic record, because successful predictions are more memorable and more likely to be cited than failed ones — without a full record of predictions, probabilities, and falsification criteria, a claim of predictive superiority remains anecdotal rather than demonstrated.
- Scope & Conditions: Applies to any argument-from-track-record that cites specific hits without also disclosing the full set of predictions made, including misses.
- Evidence: "There is also a selection problem: successful warnings are more memorable than failed ones, and the transcript does not provide a systematic record of predictions, probabilities, or falsification criteria. Without that, claims of predictive superiority remain anecdotal rather than demonstrated."
- Implications:
    - A single striking correct call (even a well-known one) is weak evidence of a person's general forecasting reliability unless their full track record, including misses, is available.
