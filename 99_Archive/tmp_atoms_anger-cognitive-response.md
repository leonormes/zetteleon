## Atoms

### Atom 001: Anger Is a Cognitive Interpretation of Injustice, Not an Automatic Physical Reaction
- Kind: claim
- Statement: Anger stems from the mind's interpretation of an event — specifically the belief that an undeserved wrong or injustice has occurred — rather than from the event itself; this cognitive-appraisal view is supported by Aristotle, Seneca, Epictetus, and modern psychological definitions (e.g. the American Psychological Association).
- Scope & Conditions: Applies to neurotypical emotional responses; does not fully account for anger driven primarily by biological or neurological pathology.
- Evidence: "Anger stems from the mind's interpretation of an event—specifically the belief that an undeserved wrong or injustice has occurred—rather than the event itself."
- Implications:
    - Since the trigger is the interpretation, not the event, changing the interpretation (cognitive reframing) is a direct lever on the anger, not a workaround.

### Atom 002: Catharsis Theory Is False — Venting Anger Rehearses and Reinforces Aggressive Pathways Rather Than Resolving Them
- Kind: claim
- Statement: Expressing anger physically (shouting, breaking objects) does not resolve the emotion; it rehearses and reinforces the neural and behavioural pathways of aggression, contrary to the popular "catharsis" idea that anger must be let out to heal. This is supported by both Buddhist teaching and modern psychological research.
- Scope & Conditions: Debunks a widespread pop-psychology myth; aligns with established neuroscientific and psychological consensus rather than being a novel or contested claim.
- Evidence: "Expressing anger physically (e.g., shouting, breaking objects) does not resolve the emotion but rather rehearses and reinforces the neural and behavioural pathways of aggression."
- Implications:
    - "Let it out to feel better" is actively counterproductive advice for anger specifically — practising the aggressive response strengthens it for next time.

### Atom 003: Whether Anger Can Be Virtuous Is a Genuinely Contested Philosophical Split, Not Settled Science
- Kind: distinction
- Statement: One tradition (Aristotle's "good temper," Plato's thumos, modern philosophers like Martha Nussbaum's "transition anger") holds that anger can be a fitting, proportionate, politically useful response to genuine injustice when strictly governed by reason and stripped of a desire for petty revenge; Stoic and Buddhist traditions counter that anger inherently corrupts reason, making any attempt at "measured" anger dangerous and prone to escalation. Neither side has a decisive empirical answer.
- Scope & Conditions: Presented as a live philosophical disagreement, not a resolved question — treating either side as simply correct overstates the state of the debate.
- Evidence: "Anger can be a fitting, proportionate, and politically useful response to genuine injustice, provided it is strictly governed by reason and stripped of the desire for petty revenge... Stoics and Buddhists counter this by arguing that anger inherently corrupts reason, making any attempt to wield 'measured' anger highly dangerous and prone to escalation."
- Implications:
    - Citing "righteous anger" as an unqualified virtue, or dismissing all anger as always corrupting, both skip past a genuine, unresolved philosophical dispute.
