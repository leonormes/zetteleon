### Atom 006: A Sense of Control Is Belief in an Available Response, Not a Guarantee Against Bad Outcomes
- Kind: distinction
- Statement: A healthy sense of control is not the belief that nothing bad can happen or that one knows everything; it is the belief that one has an available response that can influence or mitigate an adverse event, without that being a guarantee the event will never occur.
- Scope & Conditions: Distinguishes control from certainty specifically; certainty can support a sense of control but is not required for it.
- Evidence: "A healthy sense of control is not 'nothing bad can happen' or 'I know everything.' It is closer to: 'I have enough orientation to identify the relevant variables, act safely, notice when the model is failing, and adapt or escalate.'... control is fundamentally the belief that you have an available response that can influence or mitigate an adverse event—not a guarantee that it will never occur."
- Implications:
    - Reframes "I need to understand everything before I act" as targeting the wrong variable (certainty) when the actual need is an available response (control).
    - Gives a concrete operational translation for technical risk: not "understand the whole system" but "know the blast radius, rollback route, and escalation path."

### Atom 007: The Belongingness Hypothesis Holds That Humans Have a Pervasive Drive to Form Lasting Positive Social Bonds
- Kind: definition
- Statement: The belongingness hypothesis (Baumeister & Leary) proposes that people have a pervasive motivation to establish and maintain lasting, positive social bonds, and that threats of exclusion affect cognition, emotion, and behaviour.
- Scope & Conditions: A foundational social-psychology theory, cited here as the basis for why competence and social acceptance become linked (see [[Sociometer Theory Treats Self-Esteem as a Monitor of Perceived Social Acceptance]]).
- Evidence: "The belongingness hypothesis proposes that people have a pervasive motivation to establish and maintain lasting, positive social bonds; threats of exclusion affect cognition, emotion, and behaviour." (Baumeister & Leary, 1995)
- Implications:
    - Explains why social rejection registers as more than an informational event — it engages a core motivational system, not just a preference.

### Atom 008: Sociometer Theory Treats Self-Esteem as a Monitor of Perceived Social Acceptance
- Kind: definition
- Statement: Sociometer theory holds that self-esteem functions partly as a monitor of whether one is valued and included by relevant others, rather than as a standalone trait; rejection tends to lower self-esteem more strongly than acceptance raises it.
- Scope & Conditions: Does not claim every competence concern is fundamentally social, but explains why competence often becomes socially loaded (competence → reliability → respect → acceptance → felt safety in the group).
- Evidence: "Self-esteem is also closely tied to perceived social acceptance in sociometer theory. The theory treats self-esteem partly as a monitor of whether one is valued and included by relevant others; rejection tends to lower it more strongly than acceptance raises it."
- Implications:
    - Gives a mechanism for why being wrong in front of others can feel disproportionately threatening: it isn't only about the error, it's a live read on the sociometer.

### Atom 009: Rejection Sensitivity Is an Established Research Construct; "RSD" Is a Popular Term Without Formal Diagnostic Status
- Kind: distinction
- Statement: Rejection sensitivity — anxiously expecting, readily perceiving, and reacting intensely to potential rejection — is an established research construct; "Rejection Sensitive Dysphoria" (RSD) is widely used in ADHD communities to describe especially intense rejection-related pain, but it is not currently a distinct DSM diagnosis with universally accepted criteria or a standard validated assessment measure, so the lived phenomenon can be real without the specific "RSD" label being a settled clinical mechanism.
- Scope & Conditions: The useful distinction is graded: "I experience intense pain around perceived rejection" is a valid experience report; "I show a pattern consistent with rejection sensitivity" is a reasonable hypothesis; "RSD is a fully established diagnosis explaining this" overstates current evidence; "my drive is only trauma or avoidance" is also an overstatement in the other direction.
- Evidence: "Rejection sensitivity is an established research construct... 'Rejection sensitive dysphoria' is widely used in ADHD communities... it is not currently a distinct DSM diagnosis with universally accepted diagnostic criteria or a standard validated assessment measure. The lived phenomenon may be real and important; the caution is simply not to treat the label as a fully settled, ADHD-specific scientific mechanism."
- Implications:
    - Using "RSD" as a personal working description of experience is reasonable; citing it as an established, distinct neurological diagnosis is not currently supported.

### Atom 010: Need for Cognition, Need for Cognitive Closure, and Intolerance of Uncertainty Are Related but Distinct Constructs
- Kind: distinction
- Statement: Need for cognition (enjoyment of sustained, effortful thinking), need for cognitive closure (wanting a firm answer and disliking ambiguity), and intolerance of uncertainty (distress specifically about unclear future outcomes or risks) overlap but are not identical — a person can be high on one without being equally high on the others.
- Scope & Conditions: A four-to-six-construct comparison in the source also includes competence need, contingent self-worth, and rejection sensitivity as neighbouring but distinct constructs.
- Evidence: "Need for cognitive closure is typically defined as wanting a firm answer and disliking ambiguity. Intolerance of uncertainty is more specifically about difficulty bearing an uncertain future or possible negative outcomes. They overlap but are not identical."
- Implications:
    - "I need a firm answer" (closure) and "I can't tolerate not knowing what might go wrong" (IU) are different complaints and may call for different responses even when they co-occur.

### Atom 011: Predictive-Processing Accounts Frame Discomfort With Uncertainty as a Prediction-Error Problem
- Kind: definition
- Statement: One influential account in cognitive science treats the brain as a system that continually predicts incoming sensory information and updates its models when predictions fail; on this view, uncertainty becomes uncomfortable specifically when the world's behaviour is difficult to predict and the cost of being wrong seems high, rather than uncertainty being aversive in itself.
- Scope & Conditions: The source explicitly notes one does not need to adopt the full predictive-processing framework to use this narrower insight.
- Evidence: "One influential account in cognitive science treats brains as systems that continually predict incoming sensory information and update their models when predictions fail. You do not need to adopt the whole predictive-processing framework to use the insight: uncertainty becomes uncomfortable when the world's behaviour is difficult to predict and the cost of being wrong seems high."
- Implications:
    - Suggests two independent levers for reducing discomfort with uncertainty: improve predictability (build a better model) or lower the perceived cost of being wrong (reduce stakes/exposure) — either one weakens the discomfort even without resolving the other.
