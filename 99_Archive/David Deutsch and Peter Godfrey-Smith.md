Based on the provided sources, David Deutsch and Peter Godfrey-Smith offer fundamentally different views of the scientific method. Deutsch champions a strict, explanation-focused evolution of Karl Popper's philosophy, entirely rejecting empiricism and induction. In contrast, Godfrey-Smith critiques strict Popperian views and advocates for a "naturalistic" approach that salvages core empiricist ideals by focusing on the social structures and procedures of science.

Here is a detailed breakdown of how their views differ:

The Source of Scientific Theories: Conjecture vs. Experience

- Deutsch's View: Deutsch entirely rejects empiricism—the doctrine that we derive our theories from sensory experience [1-3]. He argues that scientific theories are not "derived" from nature, nor are they extrapolations of repeated observations [2-4]. Instead, they are bold conjectures created by the human imagination [2, 5]. We do not learn by "reading" the Book of Nature; we invent theories to solve problems and then use experience merely to choose between these pre-existing guesses [2, 3, 6].
- Godfrey-Smith's View: Godfrey-Smith defends a modernized version of empiricism. While he acknowledges that simplistic models of induction are flawed, he maintains that observation and experience remain the fundamental source of knowledge [7, 8]. He views empiricism as an essential "mindset" for science, characterizing it by open-mindedness, a distrust of dogmatism, and a grounded orientation toward learning from the world [8].

The Role of Testing and Confirmation

- Deutsch's View: Following Popper, Deutsch argues that scientific progress occurs through a cycle of conjecture and refutation [9, 10]. He completely rejects the idea of "induction" or "confirmation" (the idea that repeated observations make a theory more likely to be true) [11-13]. For Deutsch, experimental tests are simply a method of rational criticism used to expose false predictions and eliminate bad theories [14, 15].
- Godfrey-Smith's View: Godfrey-Smith is highly critical of Popper's refusal to accept that theories can be confirmed by evidence. He points out a major flaw in strict falsificationism: if theories can never be confirmed, we have no rational basis for relying on them [16, 17]. Godfrey-Smith uses the example of building a bridge: if we cannot increase our confidence in a theory through testing, then it would be no more rational to build a bridge using a well-tested design than it would be to use a brand-new, completely untested design [16, 17]. He also points out that simple falsification rarely works in practice because of "holism about testing" (the Duhem-Quine thesis): an unexpected observation might mean the main theory is wrong, or it might just mean a background assumption or measuring instrument is faulty [18, 19].

_(Note: Interestingly, Deutsch explicitly addresses a dilemma identical to Godfrey-Smith's bridge example, using a scenario where a person considers jumping off a tower. Deutsch argues that we are justified in relying on the prevailing theory of gravity not because past observations "confirmed" it, but because it is the only rationally tenable theory remaining that provides a good explanation, whereas alternative theories provide bad or unexplained assertions [20-23].)_

The Ultimate Goal of Science: Explanation vs. Procedure and Realism

- Deutsch's View: For Deutsch, the absolute core of the scientific method is the quest for good explanations [24, 25]. A "good explanation" is an assertion about reality that is hard to vary—meaning its details play a highly specific, functional role in the explanation and cannot be easily swapped out [3, 26, 27]. Deutsch explicitly warns against "instrumentalism" (the view that science only aims to predict outcomes) [28, 29]. He argues that prediction is not the purpose of science; prediction is merely a means to achieve the real purpose, which is to explain the fabric of reality [30].
- Godfrey-Smith's View: Godfrey-Smith also supports "scientific realism" (the idea that science aims to describe the hidden structures of the world) and values explanatory inference [31-33]. However, his view of how we evaluate theories focuses heavily on procedures. He argues that an observation provides support for a theory because of the reliability of the _procedure_ (such as randomized sampling or experimental intervention) in which the observation was embedded [34-36].

The Social and Naturalistic Context of Science

- Deutsch's View: Deutsch views the scientific method as a tradition of criticism rooted in the Enlightenment [3, 25, 37]. While he acknowledges that science relies on a "community of thinkers" [38], his focus remains primarily on the logical and epistemological structure of individual theories and the objective nature of problem-solving.
- Godfrey-Smith's View: Godfrey-Smith heavily emphasizes the social structure of science, arguing that traditional empiricism and Popperianism failed by neglecting it [39]. Drawing on naturalistic philosophy and the work of David Hull, Godfrey-Smith argues that the scientific method works largely because of a division of labor and a specific reward system [40, 41]. For example, whereas Popper thought every individual scientist must be constantly open-minded and self-critical, Godfrey-Smith suggests that individual scientists can actually be quite dogmatic about their own theories; the scientific method works because the _community_ distributes the labor, and rival scientists will eagerly do the critical testing for them [42-44]. To Godfrey-Smith, philosophy of science must be "naturalistic"—meaning it should be continuous with science itself, utilizing fields like psychology to understand how observation works and sociology to understand how scientific communities function [45-47].
