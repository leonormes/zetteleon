## Atoms

### Atom 001: Hume's Fork Holds That All Valid Knowledge Is Either Analytic or Synthetic, With Nothing Else
- Kind: definition
- Statement: Hume's Fork divides all valid knowledge into exactly two categories — analytic truths (logic, mathematics, definitions, which tell us nothing new about the physical world) and synthetic truths (matters of fact derived from direct experience) — and holds that knowledge of the world cannot be obtained through reason alone.
- Scope & Conditions: A historically specific epistemological claim, not a linguistic law; one of the most contested binaries in epistemology (see Kant's rebuttal).
- Evidence: "All valid knowledge falls into exactly two categories: analytic truths (logic, mathematics, definitions, which tell us nothing new about the physical world) and synthetic truths (matters of fact derived from direct experience). Knowledge of the world cannot be obtained through reason alone."
- Implications:
    - A claim that fits neither category (not definitional, not experientially verifiable) is, on this view, not knowledge at all — which is the basis for the impressions test.

### Atom 002: The Impressions Test Traces an Abstract Concept Back to Its Originating Sense-Impression or Discards It as Meaningless
- Kind: procedure
- Statement: To determine whether a suspicious or highly abstract concept has actual meaning, trace it back to the specific sense-impression from which it was supposedly derived; if no such impression exists, treat the concept as meaningless rather than granting it the benefit of the doubt.
- Scope & Conditions: Trigger — encountering vague, theoretical, or heavily academic terminology. Historically Hume's own method, not a universal linguistic law; over-rigid application can wrongly discard moral, aesthetic, or philosophical concepts that aren't derived from a single physical impression.
- Evidence: "To determine if a suspicious or highly abstract concept has actual meaning, one must trace it back to the specific impression from which it was supposedly derived. If no such impression exists, the concept is meaningless."
- Implications:
    - A practical jargon-detector: ask a speaker using an abstract term (e.g. "hegemony") to name the concrete experience that generates it; inability to do so is itself informative.

### Atom 003: Kant's Synthetic A Priori Challenges Hume's Fork by Claiming a Third Knowledge Category
- Kind: claim
- Statement: Immanuel Kant directly challenged Hume's Fork by arguing for the existence of synthetic a priori knowledge — claims that are both informative about the world (synthetic) and knowable independent of experience (a priori) — a category Hume's two-way division has no room for.
- Scope & Conditions: One of the deepest, most persistent disputes in epistemology; not resolved by either side's original formulation.
- Evidence: "This binary remains one of the greatest debates in epistemology; Immanuel Kant famously dedicated his work to proving that synthetic a priori knowledge exists."
- Implications:
    - Citing Hume's Fork as a settled, closed dichotomy overstates its status — it's a strong historical position with a major unresolved rival, not a consensus result.
