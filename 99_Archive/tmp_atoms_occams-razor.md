### Atom 001: Occam's Razor Favours Fewer Assumptions Among Equally Explanatory Theories, Not Simplicity Itself
- Kind: distinction
- Statement: Occam's razor states that when competing explanations account for the available evidence equally well, the one requiring fewer additional assumptions is generally preferable — it does not mean the simplest explanation is always true, and complexity is not automatically evidence against a theory.
- Scope & Conditions: Only discriminates between explanations that already fit the evidence comparably; a more complex explanation is preferable when the evidence actually requires it. Most useful when comparing explanations of similar explanatory power and predictions, not as a blanket preference for simplicity.
- Evidence: "when competing explanations account for the available evidence equally well, the one requiring fewer additional assumptions is generally preferable. This does not mean that the simplest explanation is always true, nor that complexity is automatically evidence against a theory."
- Implications:
    - Citing "Occam's razor" to dismiss a more complex explanation is only valid if the simpler explanation actually accounts for the same evidence equally well — otherwise the razor doesn't apply.

### Atom 002: The Sagan Standard Requires Evidence Proportionate to How Much a Claim Conflicts With Established Knowledge
- Kind: definition
- Statement: The Sagan standard ("extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence") raises the evidential burden for claims that conflict substantially with ordinary experience or established knowledge; "extraordinary evidence" refers to the strength and quality of the evidence required, not to evidence that merely appears dramatic or unusual.
- Scope & Conditions: A widely used principle of scientific scepticism; popularised by Carl Sagan but the underlying reasoning predates him (echoed in earlier work by David Hume and Marcello Truzzi on evidence for extraordinary events).
- Evidence: "The principle raises the evidential burden for claims that conflict substantially with ordinary experience or established knowledge... 'Extraordinary evidence' is not a precise measurement. The strength and quality of evidence matter more than whether evidence appears dramatic or unusual."
- Implications:
    - A claim shouldn't be granted a lower evidential bar just because the claimed evidence feels dramatic or compelling in the moment — drama is not the same axis as strength.

### Atom 003: Hitchens's Razor Places the Burden of Proof on the Claimant, Not the Sceptic
- Kind: definition
- Statement: Hitchens's razor holds that whoever asserts a claim must supply evidence for it; the absence of a disproof does not count as evidence that the claim is true — "dismissed without evidence" should be read as "not accepted as established," not as "proven false."
- Scope & Conditions: A basic principle of rational argument, not a complete method for evaluating evidence on its own — it settles who must argue first, not how strong their evidence needs to be (that's closer to the Sagan standard) or whether it is conclusive.
- Evidence: "someone asserting a supernatural explanation must provide evidence for it; the absence of a disproof does not count as evidence that the claim is true... 'Dismissed without evidence' is best understood as 'not accepted as established', rather than automatically proven false."
- Implications:
    - Rejecting a claim for lack of evidence is not the same speech act as proving the claim false; conflating the two overstates what scepticism has actually established.

### Atom 004: Alder's Razor Restricts Worthwhile Debate to Testable Claims, But Over-Applies as an Absolute Rule
- Kind: distinction
- Statement: Alder's razor (Newton's Flaming Laser Sword) holds that claims which cannot be settled through observation or experiment are not worth debating; taken as an absolute rule this is too restrictive, because historical, ethical, political, conceptual, and interpretive questions can be meaningful without being experimentally decidable.
- Scope & Conditions: Useful as a practical filter for deciding whether a specific factual disagreement is resolvable through evidence; overstated if used as a general rule for what is worth discussing at all.
- Evidence: "propositions should have observable consequences or be connected to precise logic or mathematics... this approach 'cuts out the crap' but can also eliminate much of what people ordinarily consider meaningful discussion... Historical, ethical, political, conceptual, and interpretive questions may be meaningful without being experimentally decidable."
- Implications:
    - Before invoking Alder's razor to end a discussion, check whether the question is actually a factual dispute (where testability matters) or a values/interpretive question (where it doesn't apply).

### Atom 005: The RISA Framework Pre-Screens a Disagreement for Being Real, Important, Specific, and Aligned Before Engaging
- Kind: procedure
- Statement: Before entering a disagreement, check it against four questions — Real (is this genuine or based on a misunderstanding?), Important (does it justify the time and effort?), Specific (is it concrete enough to make progress on?), and Aligned (do participants share a broadly compatible goal for the discussion?) — to avoid spending effort on disagreements that are unreal, unimportant, vague, or working at cross purposes.
- Scope & Conditions: Trigger — about to enter or already stuck in a disagreement. Verification — a "no" on Real or Specific suggests clarifying the actual disagreement first; a "no" on Important suggests disengaging; a "no" on Aligned suggests naming the mismatch in goals before continuing, since no amount of evidence resolves a disagreement where the parties want different things from the conversation.
- Evidence: "Real: Is the disagreement genuine, or is it based on a misunderstanding? Important: Is the issue significant enough to justify the time and effort? Specific: Is the disagreement sufficiently clear and concrete to make progress? Aligned: Do the participants share a broadly compatible goal for the discussion?"
- Implications:
    - Alignment is the hardest of the four to assess honestly, since people frequently conceal (even from themselves) that they want validation or victory rather than resolution.

### Atom 006: "Worth Debating" Splits Into Factual Resolution and Relational or Philosophical Value, and Conflating Them Causes Confusion
- Kind: distinction
- Statement: "Worth debating" carries two different senses that are easy to conflate: worth debating to establish factual truth (where testability, per Alder's razor, matters), and worth discussing for philosophical, emotional, relational, or imaginative value (where it doesn't) — treating an untestable question as pointless, or a factual dispute as merely a matter of perspective, both come from collapsing this distinction.
- Scope & Conditions: Surfaces as an apparent tension whenever a testability-based filter (like Alder's razor) is paired with an argument that unresolvable questions can still be worth exploring — the tension dissolves once the two senses of "worth" are named separately.
- Evidence: "Alder's razor recommends setting aside claims that cannot be tested, while the conclusion argues that unresolvable questions can still be worth discussing for the sake of wonder, reflection, and mutual understanding. This is not necessarily a contradiction, but it means the video is using 'worth debating' in two different senses."
- Implications:
    - Before applying a testability filter to end a conversation, check which sense of "worth" is actually at stake — factual resolution or something else — since the filter only bears on the first.
