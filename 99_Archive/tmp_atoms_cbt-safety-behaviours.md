## Atoms

### Atom 001: Graded Exposure Reduces a Safety Behaviour by Ranking Situations From Mildly to Strongly Uncomfortable and Working Upward
- Kind: procedure
- Statement: To reduce a safety behaviour without abandoning genuine safeguards, build a hierarchy of situations ranked from modestly to strongly uncomfortable, each reducing one specific safety behaviour while retaining real controls, and work upward — starting at a level that creates discomfort but remains safe and manageable, then repeating each level until anxiety becomes more manageable before advancing.
- Scope & Conditions: Trigger — a specific, named safety behaviour (over-preparation, reassurance-seeking, checking) has been identified and needs reducing. Verification — anxiety at a given level becomes more manageable with repetition before moving to the next; NHS graded-exposure guidance recommends beginning with the lowest-ranked feared situation.
- Evidence: "Create a hierarchy, from modestly uncomfortable to strongly uncomfortable situations where you reduce one safety behaviour but retain real safeguards... NHS graded-exposure guidance recommends beginning with the lowest-ranked feared situation, repeating it until anxiety becomes more manageable, and then working upward."
- Implications:
    - Attempting to skip straight to the hardest instance of a safety behaviour (e.g. a high-stakes production change) rather than starting low on the hierarchy is a common way graded exposure gets abandoned.

### Atom 002: A Behavioural Experiment Tests a Feared Prediction Under Safe Conditions Instead of Arguing With It
- Kind: procedure
- Statement: Rather than trying to talk yourself out of an anxious belief, state the belief as a specific, falsifiable prediction, then design a low-risk action that tests it directly — retaining real safety controls but deliberately reducing the safety behaviour in question — and compare what was predicted against what actually happened.
- Scope & Conditions: Trigger — a recurring anxious belief (e.g. "if I act without complete understanding, I'll be exposed as incompetent") that has never actually been tested against evidence. Verification — the experiment records the specific prediction, what safety behaviour was withheld, and whether the predicted consequence occurred.
- Evidence: "A behavioural experiment tests a prediction, rather than arguing with it endlessly... Prediction: 'The change will fail or I will be unable to cope.' Measure: Did the anticipated failure occur? Did the existing controls detect it? Could you recover?"
- Implications:
    - A belief that has survived years of avoidance untested is not thereby confirmed — it has simply never been checked; a single well-designed low-risk experiment can do more than years of reasoning about it.

### Atom 003: Reframing "Mistakes Prove Inadequacy" as "Mistakes Are System-Improvement Signals" Targets the Deeper Cognition Behind Surface Anxiety
- Kind: claim
- Statement: CBT treatment of anxious over-preparation works better when it challenges the deeper inference behind the surface prediction — not "I might be wrong" but "being wrong proves I am inadequate" — replacing it with a more accurate alternative such as "an error can be evidence that a model, checklist, or system needs improvement, not a verdict on me."
- Scope & Conditions: The surface prediction ("I might make a mistake") is often not the actual load-bearing belief; the deeper belief about what an error would mean about the person is what drives the safety behaviour and is the more productive target for reframing.
- Evidence: "CBT works better when you challenge the deeper inference, not only the surface prediction... 'If I make an error, I have failed.' → 'An error can be evidence that a model, checklist, or system needs improvement—not a verdict on me.'"
- Implications:
    - Challenging only the surface prediction ("you probably won't make a mistake") leaves the deeper belief ("if I did, it would mean I'm inadequate") untouched and the safety behaviour intact.
