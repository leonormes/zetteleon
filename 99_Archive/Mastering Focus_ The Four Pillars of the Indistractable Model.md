The Indistractable Model, as detailed primarily in Nir Eyal’s work, serves as a framework for understanding and controlling attention. It is built upon the distinction between **Traction** (actions that move you toward what you really want) and **Distraction** (actions that move you away from what you want) 1\. Eyal posits that both behaviors are prompted by triggers, which can be internal or external 2\.

Drawing from the provided sources, the following is a discussion of the four parts of the Indistractable Model within the larger context of overcoming procrastination.

## **Part 1: Master Internal Triggers**

The first step of the model addresses the root cause of distraction. Eyal argues that all motivation is a desire to escape discomfort 3\. Therefore, time management is actually "pain management" 4\. To master distraction, one must learn to cope with internal discomfort (boredom, anxiety, stress) rather than trying to escape it through digital devices or other diversions 5, 6\.

- **The Context of Anxiety and Safety:** This aligns closely with Neil Fiore’s *The Now Habit*, which defines procrastination not as laziness, but as a mechanism for coping with the anxiety associated with starting or completing a task 7\. Fiore suggests that to stop procrastinating, one must create "safety" to lower the threat to one's self-worth, thereby reducing the need to escape 8\.
- **The Neurology of Boredom:** In *ADHD 2.0*, Hallowell and Ratey explain that for some, boredom is "kryptonite," causing the brain to reflexively seek stimulation 9\. They describe a "glitchy switch" in the brain that traps people in the Default Mode Network (DMN), leading to rumination 10, 11\. To master this internal trigger, they suggest engaging the Task-Positive Network (TPN) by focusing on something external to shut down the DMN 12\.
- **Confronting Finitude:** Oliver Burkeman adds a philosophical dimension, arguing that distraction is often an attempt to flee the painful confrontation with our "finitude" (our limited time and control) 13\. He suggests that we must stop fighting the discomfort and instead accept that difficult tasks inevitably involve uncomfortable feelings 14\.

## **Part 2: Make Time for Traction**

The second step involves planning. Eyal asserts that you cannot call something a distraction unless you know what it is distracting you *from* 15\. He advocates for "timeboxing"—deciding what you are going to do and when—to ensure time is reserved for traction in three life domains: you, relationships, and work 16, 15\.

- **The Methodology of Timeboxing:** Marc Zao-Sanders provides a comprehensive breakdown of this practice, defining timeboxing as selecting what to do before the day's distractions arise and specifying start and finish times in a calendar 17\. He argues this method reduces the burden of choice and creates a sense of agency 18\.
- **The Unschedule:** Fiore offers a variation called the "Unschedule," where one schedules guilt-free play *before* work 19\. By guaranteeing leisure time, one reduces the resentment and feelings of deprivation that often lead to procrastination 20\.
- **Energy Management:** Rob Moore suggests that planning shouldn't just be about time, but about energy. He advises tracking energy highs and lows to schedule "Key Result Areas" (KRAs) during peak performance times 21, 22\.

## **Part 3: Hack Back External Triggers**

This step focuses on modifying the environment to reduce unwanted interruptions. Eyal advises asking the critical question: "Is this trigger serving me, or am I serving it?" 23\. If the trigger (a notification, a meeting, an open office plan) leads to distraction, it must be "hacked back" or removed 24\.

- **Environmental Engineering:** Hallowell and Ratey emphasize creating "stellar environments" to support focus 25, 26\. This includes establishing structures like lists and schedules to keep on task 27\.
- **Managing Prompts:** Zao-Sanders categorizes distractions as digital, physical, or mental prompts 28\. He suggests optimizing the environment by turning off notifications and decluttering the workstation to prevent these prompts from derailing a timebox 29\.
- **Asserting Boundaries:** Moore suggests "retraining the world" on how to communicate with you by turning off notifications, not answering the phone unless it is an emergency, and isolating oneself to do deep work 30, 31\.

## **Part 4: Prevent Distraction with Pacts**

The final step involves "precommitments"—decisions made in advance to bind oneself to a future course of action, preventing impulsive behavior 32\. Eyal outlines three types of pacts:

1. **Effort Pacts:** Increasing the effort required to perform an undesirable action (e.g., using app blockers) 33\.
2. **Price Pacts:** Putting money on the line to encourage follow-through (e.g., burning a £100 bill if you miss a workout) 34, 35\.
3. **Identity Pacts:** Changing one's self-image to align with desired behaviors (e.g., calling oneself "indistractable") 36, 37\.
- **Gaming Yourself:** Rob Moore refers to similar strategies as "gaming, tricking & second-guessing yourself" 38\. He advocates using rewards and penalties, public declarations, and accountability partners to force action 39, 40\.
- **Strategic Limits:** Burkeman supports the use of "fixed volume" approaches to productivity, such as limiting work-in-progress or deciding in advance what to fail at, as a way to accept limitations and focus on what matters 41, 42\.
- **Reframing Identity:** Fiore suggests replacing the self-talk of a procrastinator ("I have to") with the language of a producer ("I choose to"), effectively creating an identity pact that empowers the individual 43\.
