### Atom 001: Abductive Reasoning Infers the Best Explanation for a Set of Observations
- Kind: definition
- Statement: Abductive reasoning ("inference to the best explanation") starts from a set of data or circumstances and formulates the hypothesis that best accounts for those facts; it is the primary reasoning method used in medical diagnosis and investigative work, and — unlike deduction — never guarantees its conclusion, only proposes the most plausible one available.
- Scope & Conditions: The "best" explanation is bounded by the reasoner's current knowledge and imagination — a more accurate explanation may exist outside their awareness, so an abductive conclusion should remain a working hypothesis, not a settled fact.
- Evidence: "Abduction, or 'inference to the best explanation', involves examining a set of data or circumstances and formulating a hypothesis that best accounts for those facts. This is the primary reasoning method used in medicine (diagnostics) and detective work... The 'best' explanation is limited by the observer's current knowledge and imagination."
- Implications:
    - Diagnostic and troubleshooting work (medical, technical, or otherwise) is structurally abductive, not deductive — the goal is the best available account, not a proof, so holding it loosely is correct practice, not a weakness.

### Atom 002: Inductive Reasoning Draws Probable, Not Guaranteed, Conclusions From Past Regularity
- Kind: definition
- Statement: Inductive reasoning uses past experience to infer future outcomes, on the premise that nature is generally predictable — but unlike deductive reasoning, an inductive conclusion is only ever probable, never logically guaranteed, and can be overturned if the underlying circumstances change or new evidence emerges.
- Scope & Conditions: Completes the standard reasoning triad alongside deduction (guaranteed conclusions from premises) and abduction (best available explanation) — distinct in what kind of conclusion each is entitled to claim.
- Evidence: "Inductive arguments use past experiences to infer future outcomes, functioning on the premise that nature is generally predictable. Unlike deductive reasoning, inductive conclusions are only ever probable, not logically guaranteed... Even highly probable inductive conclusions can be proven false if the underlying circumstances change or new evidence emerges."
- Implications:
    - Treating an inductive conclusion (e.g. "this has always worked before") with the same confidence as a deductive one is a category error that overstates what past regularity can license.

### Atom 003: The Idealised Socratic Method Omits That Socrates Also Used Questioning to Corner and Embarrass Opponents
- Kind: claim
- Statement: Popular presentations of the Socratic method as pure, collaborative truth-seeking omit the historical reality that Socrates himself often used relentless questioning to deliberately corner, confuse, and embarrass his interlocutors, not only to jointly approach truth.
- Scope & Conditions: A historical corrective to the idealised version of the method, not a claim that the collaborative framing is worthless — the two can both be true of the same historical practice.
- Evidence: "The portrayal of the Socratic method serves as a useful ethical and intellectual ideal for debate, though it omits the historical reality that Socrates himself often used relentless questioning to deliberately corner, confuse, and embarrass his interlocutors."
- Implications:
    - Citing "the Socratic method" as an authority for purely good-faith dialogue should be qualified — the historical practice it's named after wasn't only that.
