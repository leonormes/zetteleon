# SSoT: ADHD Momentum & Task Initiation Strategies

## Core Concept

Activation Energy is the Primary Bottleneck
The brain is designed to conserve energy and naturally seeks the path of least resistance. For the ADHD brain, this creates a significant barrier known as "activation energy"—the initial psychological effort required to overcome inertia and begin a task. The difficulty lies not in the work itself, but in the transition from rest to action. Motivation is not a prerequisite for this action but a result of it; motion creates emotion.

The Momentum Method
The Momentum Method is a behavioral strategy designed to bypass this bottleneck. Instead of relying on willpower or "discipline" to force action, it focuses on engineering the task to lower the activation barrier to a near-zero level. By making the first step (a "Starter Task") trivially small and physically impossible to resist, one breaks the initial inertia and builds the psychological momentum needed to tackle larger challenges.

## Details

### The Mechanics of Momentum

- Starter Tasks: These are micro-actions whose sole purpose is to initiate movement (e.g., "put on running shoes" rather than "go for a run"). They serve to shift cognitive focus and bypass perfectionism or overthinking.
- Chaining & Ramping: Once inertia is broken, momentum can be sustained by "chaining" starter tasks together, creating a "momentum ramp" that leads into more difficult work.
- Procras-working: A technique of using productive procrastination—completing small, low-stakes tasks—to generate the energy required for a primary, more daunting task.
- The Five-Item To-Do List: A constraint strategy to reduce overwhelm by limiting immediate choices, thereby lowering the decision fatigue that spikes activation energy.

### Building Sustainable Routines

Effective ADHD routines must be distinct from neurotypical "self-help" systems, often drawing on principles that prioritize brain chemistry over rigid discipline (e.g., Caren Magill's framework):

1. Intrinsic Motivation: Routines must be anchored in _why_ it matters to the individual ("Does this align with my values?") rather than external pressure or trends.
2. Friction Reduction: Success comes from identifying specific friction points in a workflow and applying the smallest possible tweak to smooth the path (e.g., setting out clothes the night before), rather than overhauling the whole system.
3. Self-Trust: Sustainable routines require trusting one's own intuition and allowing the process to look different from standard advice.
4. Immediate Rewards: Because the ADHD brain prioritizes the now, habits must be reinforced by immediate feedback loops or rewards; relying on long-term gratification is ineffective.

Example Workflow for Habit Formation:
1. Pick one area to optimize.
2. Note friction points hindering the action.
3. Identify the smallest change to remove that friction.
4. Layer changes one at a time, adding a new tweak only when the previous behavior becomes automatic.

## Conflicts/Nuances

- Willpower vs. Engineering: Conventional advice prescribes "willpower" to start tasks. For ADHD, this is a losing battle. The effective approach is _engineering_ the environment and task definitions to remove the need for willpower.
- Consistency vs. Expectations: The primary obstacle to maintaining routines is not an inherent inability to be consistent, but unrealistic expectations. Failure often stems from trying to change "too much, too fast" or copying neurotypical influencers.
- Individualization: There is no universal "right way." A routine's longevity depends on how well it is customized to the individual's specific energy levels and context.
