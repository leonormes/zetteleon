The Telos Method is described in the sources as an open-source framework designed for **"self-capture"**, creating deep context about what matters to a human being so that they can effectively function in an AI-driven world 1, 2\. Within the larger context of a concept called **"Human 3.0"**, the framework argues that as AI takes over task execution, humans must shift their economic value to becoming "originators of ideas" who know exactly what they are about 3, 2\.

**The Framework Structure**

The method organises self-knowledge into a specific hierarchical flow, moving from abstract purpose down to tangible execution:

- **Problems:** This is the foundational layer, identifying specific issues in the world the individual aims to address, such as a lack of understanding regarding secure open-source software 4, 5\.
- **Mission:** Derived from the identified problem, this is the primary statement of intent. While an individual should have a primary mission, they may have multiple narratives addressing different problems 5, 6\.
- **Narratives:** These are described as "quick handles" or scripts used to explain oneself to others (e.g., at parties) and to oneself. They serve a psychological function to combat "impostor syndrome" by reminding the user of their value and ideas 7, 8\.
- **Goals:** These are concrete targets the individual wants to hit, such as achieving a specific number of downloads or starting a family. The framework allows for categorising these by urgency and importance, noting that some goals (like digital immortality) may not be started for decades 9, 10\.
- **Challenges:** These are specific obstacles preventing progress on goals. Examples include a lack of motivation, difficulty building a user experience (UX), or anxiety about a competitor 10\.
- **Strategies:** The sources define a strategy specifically as **"a very specific way that you are addressing a challenge"** 11\. It is the method devised to bypass the obstacle to reach the goal.
- **Projects:** These represent meaningful, long-term undertakings that "fall out of the strategies" 11\. The sources distinguish these from simple daily tasks or errands, suggesting that anything meaningful should be treated as a project 12\.

**The Chain of Explainability**
A critical aspect of this structure is the **"explainability"** it provides for an individual's life. The framework ensures that when a user engages in a **Project**, they understand its lineage: it is being done because it is part of a **Strategy** to overcome a **Challenge**, which is blocking a **Goal**, which serves the **Mission** to solve a specific **Problem** 4\. This structure prevents aimlessness by ensuring every action has a clear link back to the user's core values.
**Implementation and AI Integration**
The framework is designed to be machine-readable, often stored as Markdown files or visual diagrams (using tools like Excalidraw) 1, 13\. By feeding this structured data into an AI, the system gains "ultimate context" on the user, allowing it to act as a highly personalised assistant that can "threat model" plans, find neglected goals, or identify negative thinking patterns 14, 15\.
To illustrate the relationship between these concepts, the sources provide a military analogy:If your **Challenge** is facing 10,000 enemy troops on an open field with only 1,000 of your own, a direct fight means you lose. The **Strategy** is the specific method to bypass this disadvantage, such as attacking only at night using camouflage. The **Project** is the actual training and deployment of the troops to execute that night attack 11\.
