## Atoms

### Atom 001: Ethical Intuitionism Reasons From Uncontroversial Concrete Cases to Controversial Ones, Not From Abstract Theory Downward
- Kind: procedure
- Statement: Common-sense ethical intuitionism starts moral reasoning from concrete examples where the intuitive verdict is uncontroversial, then draws analogies to controversial cases — rather than starting from an abstract theory or generalisation and accepting whatever conclusions it logically forces, even when they contradict strong intuitions.
- Scope & Conditions: A stated methodological alternative to top-down ethical theorising (e.g. strict utilitarianism); intuitions can vary between cultures and individuals, which limits how universal the "uncontroversial" starting cases really are.
- Evidence: "Moral reasoning should begin with concrete examples where intuitive verdicts are uncontroversial, and then draw analogies to controversial cases. Starting with abstract generalisations often forces people to embrace false or absurd consequences to remain intellectually consistent."
- Implications:
    - When a moral theory's logical conclusion feels absurd, that clash is itself evidence against the theory on this method, not something to be explained away in the theory's favour.

### Atom 002: The Utilitarian Organ-Harvesting Thought Experiment Shows Maximising Aggregate Welfare Can Justify Intuitively Abhorrent Acts
- Kind: claim
- Statement: Classic utilitarianism (maximising overall enjoyment or happiness) is criticised because it would logically require murdering one healthy patient to harvest their organs to save five dying patients, if the net welfare calculation favours it — a standard objection used to argue the theory's aggregate-maximising logic can license acts that are intuitively monstrous.
- Scope & Conditions: A well-known objection within moral philosophy, not a knockdown refutation — modern rule-utilitarians have developed defences against this specific case that this treatment doesn't address.
- Evidence: "Utilitarianism... is flawed because it justifies intuitively abhorrent actions. For example, it would logically require murdering one healthy patient to harvest organs to save five dying patients."
- Implications:
    - Any aggregate-welfare argument should be checked against this kind of edge case before being accepted as a complete ethical framework.

### Atom 003: The Chinese Room Argument Contests Whether Symbol Manipulation Alone Constitutes Genuine Understanding
- Kind: definition
- Statement: John Searle's Chinese Room thought experiment argues that a computer program does not possess true understanding or consciousness merely by manipulating symbols correctly — like a person following a rulebook to manipulate Chinese characters without knowing the language, a system can produce correct output through syntax alone, without semantic comprehension.
- Scope & Conditions: One of the most famous, and most fiercely contested, thought experiments in philosophy of mind and cognitive science; not a settled result.
- Evidence: "A computer running a program does not possess true understanding or consciousness. Like a person manipulating Chinese symbols using a rulebook without knowing the language, a computer relies solely on syntax without semantic comprehension."
- Implications:
    - Directly relevant to any claim that a language model "understands" its output — this argument is the classical challenge such a claim has to answer, not something it can bypass by citing correct performance alone.

### Atom 004: Treating State Coercion as Equivalent to Interpersonal Coercion Is a Contested Anarcho-Capitalist Argument, Not a Neutral Analysis
- Kind: distinction
- Statement: The argument that state actions should be judged by the same moral standards as individual actions — so that if it is wrong for a person to force a neighbour to labour or hand over money, it is in principle wrong for the state to enforce jury duty or taxation — relies entirely on an anarcho-capitalist framework, and its presentation typically omits standard counterarguments such as democratic consent, the social contract, and the necessity of solving collective-action problems.
- Scope & Conditions: A recognisable, coherent position within political philosophy, but one contested premise among several, not a neutral extension of common-sense ethics into politics.
- Evidence: "If it is wrong for an individual to force a neighbour to perform labour or surrender money, it is in principle wrong for the state to enforce jury duty or taxation... This relies entirely on an anarcho-capitalist framework and dismisses concepts like the social contract or public goods."
- Implications:
    - When this "substitute an individual for the state" analogy is used to argue against a specific policy, check whether the argument has actually addressed the standard structural/legal differences political theorists cite to justify state authority, or merely asserted the analogy holds.
